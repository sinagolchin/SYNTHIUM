                            PER CURIAM

      A mother released liability on her minor daughter’s behalf in
favor of a trampoline park. The agreement contains indemnity and
arbitration provisions.   The indemnity provision identifies several
park-affiliated individuals and entities as “protected parties,” but the
protected parties did not sign the agreement.
      The daughter was injured while visiting the park. Her mother
sued on her behalf, naming the park and the protected parties as
defendants. The defendants moved to compel arbitration under the
release agreement, and the trial court denied the motion. The court of
appeals reversed as to the park, holding that an arbitration agreement
existed between the park and the minor child. The court further held,
however, that the non-signatory affiliates identified in the agreement
had no contractual right to compel arbitration. Because the protected
parties are third-party beneficiaries of the agreement, we reverse this
portion of the court of appeals’ judgment and remand the case to the
trial court for further proceedings.

                                       I

       Chiniqua Geter sued on behalf of her minor daughter, K.G., for
injuries K.G. sustained while playing at an Urban Air trampoline park
in Beaumont, Texas.         The named defendants include Beaumont
Adventure Park Urban Air LLC (Beaumont Urban Air) and petitioners
UATP Management, LLC; UATP IP, LLC; UA Attractions, LLC;
Rachelle Nurse-Goodly; and Joseph Goodly.
       Before entering the play area of the park, Geter signed a release
and indemnification agreement in favor of Beaumont Urban Air on
behalf of K.G. The release and indemnity provision in the agreement
identifies the non-signatory petitioners as “protected parties” that
Geter, in signing the agreement, “releases, agrees not to sue, and shall
indemnify.” 1 The agreement further contains an arbitration provision
providing that “[a]ny dispute or claim arising out of or relating to this
Agreement, breach thereof, the Premises, Activities, property damage
(real or personal), personal injury (including death), or the scope,

[and] the legal owner of the premises . . . (collectively, protected parties).”

                                       II

      As a general matter, a party seeking to compel arbitration must
establish that the claims at issue fall within the scope of a valid
arbitration agreement. Henry v. Cash Biz, LP, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6109094"><a href="/opinion/6242023/henry-v-cash-biz-lp/" aria-description="Citation for case: Henry v. Cash Biz, LP">551 S.W.3d 111</a></span><pre class="inline">, 115




                                       3
(Tex. 2018). Whether an agreement to arbitrate exists is a question of
law for courts to decide. Baby Dolls Topless Saloons, Inc. v. Sotero, </pre><span class="citation no-link">642
S.W.3d 583</span><pre class="inline">, 586 (Tex. 2022).
      The court of appeals held that a valid arbitration agreement
exists and binds K.G. because Geter signed the agreement on K.G.’s
behalf and K.G. accepted benefits under the agreement by gaining
access to the park. ___ S.W.3d at ___, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2024 WL 4163646</span><pre class="inline">, at *3. Geter
challenges this holding, arguing that K.G., as a minor, may disavow the
contract and did so by instituting this action. 2
      The arbitration agreement binds K.G. because Geter signed it on
K.G.’s behalf as her parent as Texas law permits. The direct-benefits
estoppel doctrine is not required to reach that conclusion. The court of
appeals relied on our opinion in Taylor Morrison of Texas, Inc. v. Ha to
support its direct-benefits estoppel application. </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline"> at *2-3 (citing </pre><span class="citation no-link">660
S.W.3d 529</span><pre class="inline">, 534 (Tex. 2023)). In Ha, we held that a contractor could
compel minor children to arbitrate their construction-defect claims
under a home-purchase agreement their father signed because the
children had accepted the benefits of the agreement in occupying the
home. 660 S.W.3d at 531-35. The direct-benefits estoppel doctrine was
necessary to reach this conclusion because the children’s father had not
signed the purchase agreement on the children’s behalf. See id. at 531.




      2 As Geter did not timely file a petition challenging the court of appeals’

judgment, the judgment in favor of Beaumont Urban Air is final. See TEX. R.
APP. P. 53.1 (“A party who seeks to alter the court of appeals’ judgment must
file a petition for review.”). We address the validity of the agreement only
insofar as it affects the protected parties’ appeal.




                                       4
      As we recognized in Ha, “parents may sign arbitration
agreements on behalf of their children” under the Family Code, which
recognizes parents’ rights to make “‘decisions of substantial legal
significance’ concerning their children.” Id. at 534 (quoting TEX. FAM.
CODE § 151.001(a)(7)). Exceptions apply only “[i]n rare cases . . . such
as when a parent’s interests are adverse to the child’s interests.” Id. at
534 n.6. In this case, the parties do not advance an exception to the
general rule. Geter signed the agreement on K.G.’s behalf to allow her
to play at the trampoline park. K.G. thus is bound by her mother’s
signature irrespective of the direct-benefits estoppel doctrine.
      Nor can K.G. disavow the agreement, having accepted the benefit
of visiting the trampoline park.     As Geter correctly points out, we
recognize that a contract obligating a minor is “voidable at the election
of the minor.” Dairyland Cnty. Mut. Ins. Co. of Tex. v. Roman, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1499001"><a href="/opinion/1499001/dairyland-county-mutual-insurance-co-of-texas-v-roman/#158" aria-description="Citation for case: Dairyland County Mutual Insurance Co. of Texas v. Roman">498
S.W.2d 154, 158</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Tex. 1973). However, we also have long recognized the
corollary principle that a minor “is not permitted to retain the benefits
of a contract while repudiating its obligations.” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1499001"><a href="/opinion/1499001/dairyland-county-mutual-insurance-co-of-texas-v-roman/" aria-description="Citation for case: Dairyland County Mutual Insurance Co. of Texas v. Roman">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> K.G. lost any ability
to disavow the agreement upon gaining entry to the trampoline park.
      Having confirmed that an arbitration agreement exists and binds
K.G., the remaining issue is whether the protected parties, who did not
sign the agreement, may nonetheless compel arbitration as beneficiaries
of that agreement. We hold they can.
      As a preliminary matter, the protected parties assert that the
court of appeals erred in reaching the issue of enforceability against
non-signers to the agreement because the agreement delegates
questions of arbitrability to the arbitrator. We disagree.




                                    5
      Parties may agree to delegate questions about the scope of an
arbitration agreement to arbitrators rather than courts. TotalEnergies
E&amp;P USA, Inc. v. MP Gulf of Mex., LLC, </pre><span class="citation no-link">667 S.W.3d 694</span><pre class="inline">, 702 (Tex.
2023). If a valid delegation clause exists, “courts must enforce that
agreement just as they must enforce an agreement to delegate
resolution of the underlying merits to the arbitrator.” </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline"> Whether a
party who did not sign the agreement may compel arbitration, however,
“raises the question of whether an agreement to arbitrate exists between
those parties.” Cerna ex rel. R.W. v. Pearland Urb. Air, LLC, </pre><span class="citation no-link">714 S.W.3d
585</span><pre class="inline">, 589 (Tex. 2025).    “[T]he existence of a valid arbitration clause
between specific parties” is “a gateway matter for the court to decide.”
G.T. Leach Builders, LLC v. Sapphire V.P., LP, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2831475"><a href="/opinion/2831475/gt-leach-builders-llc-v-sapphire-vp-lp/#524" aria-description="Citation for case: G.T. Leach Builders, LLC v. Sapphire V.P., Lp">458 S.W.3d 502, 524</a></span><pre class="inline">
(Tex. 2015) (quoting In re Rubiola, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="895301"><a href="/opinion/895301/in-re-rubiola/#224" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Rubiola">334 S.W.3d 220, 224</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Tex. 2011)). We
hold that it is for the courts, not an arbitrator, to decide whether the
non-signatory protected parties may invoke the agreement. We review
the court of appeals’ resolution of that question de novo. Jody James
Farms, JV v. Altman Grp., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4276220"><a href="/opinion/4498967/jody-james-farms-jv-v-the-altman-group-inc-and-laurie-diaz/#633" aria-description="Citation for case: Jody James Farms, Jv v. the Altman Group, Inc. and Laurie...">547 S.W.3d 624, 633</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Tex. 2018).
      Because “[a]rbitration is a creature of contract between
consenting parties,” we examine contract principles to determine
whether an agreement encompasses non-signatory parties. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4276220"><a href="/opinion/4498967/jody-james-farms-jv-v-the-altman-group-inc-and-laurie-diaz/#629" aria-description="Citation for case: Jody James Farms, Jv v. the Altman Group, Inc. and Laurie...">Id. at 629</a></span><pre class="inline">.
We have recognized six such principles that may establish contractual
application   to   non-signatories:       “(1) incorporation   by   reference,
(2) assumption, (3) agency, (4) alter ego, (5) equitable estoppel, and
(6) third-party beneficiary.”   </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4276220"><a href="/opinion/4498967/jody-james-farms-jv-v-the-altman-group-inc-and-laurie-diaz/#633" aria-description="Citation for case: Jody James Farms, Jv v. the Altman Group, Inc. and Laurie...">Id. at 633</a></span><pre class="inline">.      Pertinent to this case, the
protected parties claim third-party beneficiary status as to the
agreement.




                                      6
      “Like other contracts,” third-party beneficiaries may enforce
arbitration agreements “so long as ‘the parties to the contract intended
to secure a benefit to that third party and entered into the contract
directly for the third party’s benefit.’” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4276220"><a href="/opinion/4498967/jody-james-farms-jv-v-the-altman-group-inc-and-laurie-diaz/" aria-description="Citation for case: Jody James Farms, Jv v. the Altman Group, Inc. and Laurie...">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> at 635 (quoting In re Palm
Harbor Homes, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9512915"><a href="/opinion/894735/in-re-palm-harbor-homes-inc/#677" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Palm Harbor Homes, Inc.">195 S.W.3d 672, 677</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Tex. 2006)). “The benefit
must be more than incidental, and the contracting parties’ intent to
‘confer a direct benefit to a third party must be clearly and fully spelled
out or enforcement by the third party must be denied.’” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9512915"><a href="/opinion/894735/in-re-palm-harbor-homes-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Palm Harbor Homes, Inc.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> (quoting MCI
Telecomms. Corp. v. Tex. Utils. Elec. Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2463940"><a href="/opinion/2463940/mci-telecommunications-corp-v-texas-utilities-electric-co/#651" aria-description="Citation for case: MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. Texas Utilities Electric Co.">995 S.W.2d 647, 651</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Tex.
1999)).
      In this case, the agreement names each non-signatory as among
the “protected parties” that Geter “releases, agrees not to sue, and shall
indemnify.” Geter argues that this indemnity provision does not confer
third-party beneficiary status to the parties’ arbitration provision
because the arbitration provision does not separately include them. The
protected parties respond that the dispute resolution clauses—including
the arbitration provision—are the mechanisms to enforce the benefits of
release and indemnity conferred to them as intended third-party
beneficiaries. We agree.
      The release and indemnity provision expressly identifying the
non-signatory entities as “protected parties” demonstrates the parties’
intent to benefit these entities. “By its own terms, the agreement was
entered into, in part, directly for [the non-signatory entities’] benefit.”
Palm Harbor Homes, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9512915"><a href="/opinion/894735/in-re-palm-harbor-homes-inc/#677" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Palm Harbor Homes, Inc.">195 S.W.3d at 677</a></span><pre class="inline">. The arbitration provision
applies to undefined “parties” and does not exclude the protected parties
named elsewhere in the agreement. Construing the contract as a whole




                                    7
and harmonizing its provisions, as we must, Valence Operating Co. v.
Dorsett, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9512883"><a href="/opinion/894667/valence-operating-co-v-dorsett/#662" aria-description="Citation for case: Valence Operating Co. v. Dorsett">164 S.W.3d 656, 662</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Tex. 2005), it follows that the protected
parties may enforce the arbitration agreement as third-party
beneficiaries; arbitration is the parties’ chosen means of enforcing the
agreement’s release and indemnification provision. Having decided that
the parties agreed to arbitrate, “courts must respect the agreement’s
terms.” Cerna, 714 S.W.3d at 592. As third-party beneficiaries of the
agreement, the protected parties may invoke the arbitration provision.

                             *      *      *
      Without hearing oral argument, see TEX. R. APP. P. 59.1, we grant
the petition for review and reverse the portion of the court of appeals’
judgment affirming the trial court’s denial of the motion to compel
arbitration as to the protected parties. The remainder of the court of
appeals’ judgment, unchallenged on appeal, remains in effect.         We
remand the case to the trial court for further proceedings consistent with
this opinion.
