    DEWAR, J.    The defendant, Clerence H. Goncalves,

surreptitiously photographed a sexual encounter between himself

and the victim using a camera installed in his living room and
                                                                     2


later sent a photograph of the encounter to the victim.    A jury

convicted the defendant of two offenses:    photographing an

unsuspecting nude person, in violation of G. L. c. 272,

§ 105 (b), first par., and disseminating the resulting

photograph, in violation of G. L. c. 272, § 105 (c), first par.

The defendant challenges both convictions on appeal.

       We reject the defendant&#x27;s challenges to his conviction of

photographing an unsuspecting nude person.    Notwithstanding the

defense at trial that the photograph resulted accidentally from

the defendant&#x27;s installation of the camera for security

purposes, the evidence was sufficient to prove that the

defendant willfully photographed the victim in the nude with the

intent to do so secretly.    And, contrary to the defendant&#x27;s

argument raised for the first time on appeal, the jury

instructions adequately conveyed to the jury that the

Commonwealth was required to prove that the defendant willfully

photographed the victim while the victim was nude.

       We agree with the defendant, however, that his conviction

of disseminating the resulting photograph must be reversed.     We

hold that sending a photograph of a victim solely to the victim

himself or herself does not amount to &quot;disseminat[ing]&quot; the

photograph within the meaning of G. L. c. 272, § 105 (c), first

par.   And here, the Commonwealth did not present evidence that
                                                                        3


the defendant sent the photograph to any person other than the

victim.

    We therefore affirm the defendant&#x27;s conviction under G. L.

c. 272, § 105 (b), first par., and reverse the defendant&#x27;s

conviction under G. L. c. 272, § 105 (c), first par.

    Discussion.   We address in turn the defendant&#x27;s arguments

with respect to each of his two convictions.

    1.   Photographing an unsuspecting nude person.    General

Laws c. 272, § 105 (b), first par., makes it a crime to

    &quot;willfully photograph[], videotape[] or electronically
    surveil[] another person who is nude or partially nude,
    with the intent to secretly conduct or hide such activity,
    when the other person in such place and circumstance would
    have a reasonable expectation of privacy in not being so
    photographed, videotaped or electronically surveilled, and
    without that person&#x27;s knowledge and consent.&quot;

The Commonwealth thus must prove two things with respect to the

defendant&#x27;s intent:   that the defendant (1) &quot;willfully

photograph[ed], videotape[d] or electronically surveil[led]

another person who is nude or partially nude,&quot; and (2) did so

with the &quot;intent to secretly conduct or hide&quot; this activity.

See Commonwealth v. Robertson, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6454995"><a href="/opinion/6580812/commonwealth-v-robertson/#375" aria-description="Citation for case: Commonwealth v. Robertson">467 Mass. 371, 375-376</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2014).

    The defendant challenges the sufficiency of the evidence

that he willfully photographed the victim in the nude, the

sufficiency of the evidence that he did so with the intent to

hide this activity, and the jury instruction on willfulness.
                                                                    8


    a.    Evidence of willfulness.   When reviewing the

sufficiency of the evidence, we assess whether, &quot;after viewing

the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, any

rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements

of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt&quot; (citation omitted).

Commonwealth v. Latimore, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2083224"><a href="/opinion/2083224/commonwealth-v-latimore/#677" aria-description="Citation for case: Commonwealth v. Latimore">378 Mass. 671, 677</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1979).      See

Commonwealth v. MacCormack, </pre><span class="citation no-link">491 Mass. 848</span><pre class="inline">, 859-860 (2023) (court

&quot;must look at the evidence as a whole&quot; to determine whether it

is &quot;of sufficient force to bring minds of ordinary intelligence

and sagacity to the persuasion of [guilt] beyond a reasonable

doubt&quot; [citations omitted]).   &quot;A conviction may rest exclusively

on circumstantial evidence, and, in evaluating that evidence, we

draw all reasonable inferences in favor of the Commonwealth.&quot;

Commonwealth v. Spaulding, </pre><span class="citation no-link">495 Mass. 300</span><pre class="inline">, 309-310 (2025),

quoting Commonwealth v. Bonner, </pre><span class="citation no-link">489 Mass. 268</span><pre class="inline">, 275 (2022).

These inferences &quot;need only be reasonable and possible, not

necessary or inescapable&quot; (quotation omitted).    Commonwealth v.

Witkowski, </pre><span class="citation no-link">487 Mass. 675</span><pre class="inline">, 679 (2021), quoting Commonwealth v.

Copeland, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4360534"><a href="/opinion/4583281/commonwealth-v-copeland/#259" aria-description="Citation for case: Commonwealth v. Copeland">481 Mass. 255, 259-260</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2019).    The Commonwealth&#x27;s

evidence is insufficient, however, if it &quot;relies on conjecture

or speculation.&quot;   Commonwealth v. Rodriguez, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2483754"><a href="/opinion/2483754/commonwealth-v-rodriguez/#582" aria-description="Citation for case: Commonwealth v. Rodriguez">456 Mass. 578, 582</a></span><pre class="inline">

(2010).

    The parties agree that, in the context of G. L. c. 272,

§ 105 (b), &quot;willfully&quot; means &quot;intentional[ly] and by design[,]
                                                                    9


in contrast to that which is thoughtless or accidental.&quot;

Commonwealth v. Pfeiffer, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7088801"><a href="/opinion/7178394/commonwealth-v-pfeiffer/#116" aria-description="Citation for case: Commonwealth v. Pfeiffer">482 Mass. 110, 116</a></span><pre class="inline">, cert. denied, </pre><span class="citation no-link">589

U.S. 1061</span><pre class="inline"> (2019), quoting Commonwealth v. McGovern, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2033353"><a href="/opinion/2033353/commonwealth-v-mcgovern/#868" aria-description="Citation for case: Commonwealth v. McGovern">397 Mass.

863, 868</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1986).   The defendant also contends, and the

Commonwealth does not dispute, that the Commonwealth was

required to prove more than just the willful photographing of a

victim generally; rather, the Commonwealth was required to prove

more specifically that the defendant willfully photographed a

victim he knew to be nude or partially nude.    See G. L. c. 272,

§ 105 (b), first par. (&quot;Whoever willfully photographs . . .

another person who is nude or partially nude . . .&quot;).     Cf.

Commonwealth v. Castro, </pre><span class="citation no-link">99 Mass. App. Ct. 502</span><pre class="inline">, 508 (2021)

(Commonwealth must &quot;prove the defendant&#x27;s specific intent at the

time of the actus reus -- . . . the taking of the photograph&quot;).

And the defendant argues that here, the Commonwealth failed to

prove such willfulness, because the evidence presented at trial

was &quot;equally or more consistent&quot; with accidental taking of the

photograph.

    The Commonwealth presented sufficient circumstantial

evidence to prove this element.   See Commonwealth v. Gonzalez,

</pre><span class="citation" data-id="9835903"><a href="/opinion/4254133/commonwealth-v-gonzalez/#414" aria-description="Citation for case: Commonwealth v. Gonzalez">475 Mass. 396, 414</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2016) (intent is &quot;rarely proved by direct

evidence&quot; [citation omitted]).    The jury could infer from the

empty camera box located in the defendant&#x27;s dresser that the

camera had been installed by the defendant during his residency
                                                                      10


at the apartment.   He had installed it in a location that the

jury could infer was intended to make the camera less

conspicuous to visitors; it was positioned high on the wall,

between the corner and a protruding dark-colored pipe, and close

to a dark gap between the tarp ceiling and wall.     He also had

installed it so that it was pointed directly at the couch where

the defendant&#x27;s sexual encounter with the victim began.         The

defendant then demonstrated his knowledge that he had

photographed the sexual encounter with the victim when, upon

being contacted again by the victim, he responded with a

photograph of the encounter.

    The jury also reasonably could find that the defendant made

statements evincing consciousness of guilt.   See MacCormack, 491

Mass. at 856 (&quot;Evidence of consciousness of guilt, while not

conclusive, may be considered in conjunction with other evidence

to establish guilt beyond a reasonable doubt&quot;).    When the victim

questioned the defendant&#x27;s conduct in &quot;secretly recording guys

that come over&quot; and raised the prospect of contacting the

police, the defendant did not state that the recording was

accidental and instead responded, &quot;Block u[.]     Bye.&quot;   The

defendant also later stated to the police, while laughing, that

he had made &quot;porno,&quot; thus inferably attempting to minimize his

conduct.
                                                                   11


    From all of this evidence, considered as a whole in the

light most favorable to the Commonwealth, the jury reasonably

could conclude beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant

acted willfully in photographing the nude victim during their

encounter.   See MacCormack, 491 Mass. at 860-861 (while &quot;no

single inference the jury reasonably could have drawn would have

been sufficient, in isolation, to establish guilt beyond a

reasonable doubt,&quot; when &quot;[c]ombined, . . . those reasonable

inferences create a &#x27;fabric of proof&#x27; that is sufficient&quot;).

Although the inferences urged by the Commonwealth were not

&quot;necessary or inescapable,&quot; they were &quot;reasonable and possible&quot;

(citation omitted).    Witkowski, 487 Mass. at 679.

    The evidence did not, as the defendant contends, &quot;tend[]

equally&quot; to support his accident theory (quotation and citation

omitted).    Rodriguez, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2483754"><a href="/opinion/2483754/commonwealth-v-rodriguez/#582" aria-description="Citation for case: Commonwealth v. Rodriguez">456 Mass. at 582</a></span><pre class="inline">.   The defendant argues

that the same evidence could result from a chain of events in

which he did not willfully photograph the victim in the nude and

instead installed the camera for security purposes, forgot about

the camera during the encounter with the victim, later

discovered the photograph, and commented to police with laughter

at accidentally making &quot;porno.&quot;   There was, however, no evidence

at trial of such an intervening discovery of an accidental

recording; instead, as discussed, on being contacted again by

the victim, the defendant sent him the photograph and then made
                                                                  12


no claim of an accident in response to the victim&#x27;s accusation

of &quot;secretly recording guys that come over.&quot;   Although no

evidence definitively disproved the accident defense, the jury

were entitled to find beyond a reasonable doubt that the

photograph was taken intentionally based on the totality of the

evidence described above and the permissible inferences

therefrom.   See Witkowski, 487 Mass. at 681 n.6 (when

&quot;conflicting inferences are possible from the evidence, it is

for the jury to determine where the truth lies&quot; [quotation and

citation omitted]).

    Our holding today does not, as the defendant claims, impose

strict liability for any accidental recording of nudity by a

home security camera.   To obtain a conviction of photographing

an unsuspecting nude person under G. L. c. 272, § 105 (b), first

par., the Commonwealth must prove that the defendant willfully,

rather than accidentally, recorded a victim whom the defendant

knew to be nude or partially nude.   Here, the Commonwealth&#x27;s

evidence -- including the defendant&#x27;s placement of the camera,

his retrieval and transmission of the photograph upon being

contacted by the victim, and his actions after being confronted

by the victim -- was sufficient to prove that the defendant

acted willfully in photographing the victim in the nude.

    b.   Evidence of intent to secretly conduct or hide

photographing.   For similar reasons, the Commonwealth&#x27;s
                                                                  13


circumstantial evidence also was sufficient to prove that the

defendant intended to &quot;secretly conduct or hide&quot; his

photographing of the victim in the nude.   G. L. c. 272,

§ 105 (b), first par.

     As discussed, the defendant installed the camera in a

location the jury reasonably could find was chosen in order to

make the camera less conspicuous to visitors:   high on the wall,

near a dark-colored pipe and a dark gap between the ceiling and

wall.    Although the camera remained visible in that location,

the jury were entitled to find that the defendant sought to

prevent others from noticing it through the manner of its

installation.

     In addition, the defendant never notified the victim of the

existence of the camera in the corner of the dimly lit room, let

alone obtaining the victim&#x27;s consent to photograph their

encounter.   The jury could infer that the defendant knew the

victim was unlikely to notice the camera in the location where

he had installed it, in a ceiling corner of the dimly lit room.

Accordingly, the jury reasonably could have found that the

defendant&#x27;s failure to alert the victim to the camera&#x27;s presence

or obtain his consent was part of a scheme to hide the

defendant&#x27;s photographing of the encounter.3

    c.   Jury instruction on willfulness.    The defendant also

argues that the judge&#x27;s instructions to the jury materially

misstated the law by permitting the jury to conclude that the

defendant acted willfully based solely on a finding that he

intended to photograph (such as by installing and operating an

around-the-clock security camera), without further finding that

he intended to photograph the victim in the nude.

    &quot;We evaluate jury instructions as a whole to determine how

a reasonable juror would understand them.&quot;   Commonwealth v.
                                                                     16


Carvajal, </pre><span class="citation no-link">496 Mass. 490</span><pre class="inline">, 500 (2025).     &quot;While trial judges are

not required to use any particular phrasing, the instructions

must convey the correct legal standard.&quot;     </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline">   Because the

defendant did not object to the instruction on willfulness at

trial, &quot;we review [his] claim to determine whether there was an

error and, if so, whether the error created a substantial risk

of a miscarriage of justice.&quot;     Commonwealth v. Kelly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9806119"><a href="/opinion/2780739/commonwealth-v-kelly/#697" aria-description="Citation for case: Commonwealth v. Kelly">470 Mass.

682, 697</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2015).

    We discern no error in the jury instruction considered as a

whole, in view of the undisputed evidence that the defendant was

with the nude victim at the time of the recording.     Consistent

with Instruction 6.530 of the Criminal Model Jury Instructions

for Use in the District Court (2021), the judge broke the

offense into discrete elements, including the element &quot;that the

defendant willfully photographed, videotaped or electronically

surveilled the alleged victim.&quot;    The judge subsequently

elaborated that, to conclude &quot;that the defendant acted willfully

in recording,&quot; the jury would have to &quot;make a decision about the

defendant&#x27;s state of mind at the time of the incident, and

whether his actions were intentionally [sic], and not accidental

or due to some inadvertence or inattentiveness.&quot;     The judge also

instructed the jury that to convict the defendant they had to

find &quot;that the alleged victim was nude or partially nude at the

time.&quot;
                                                                  17


    In the circumstances of this case, these instructions

sufficed to convey to the jury that they could not convict the

defendant without finding that he willfully photographed the

victim in the nude.   See Kelly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9806119"><a href="/opinion/2780739/commonwealth-v-kelly/#698" aria-description="Citation for case: Commonwealth v. Kelly">470 Mass. at 698</a></span><pre class="inline"> (elements of

crime &quot;cannot be divorced from the facts surrounding&quot; incident);

Commonwealth v. Dyer, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2247480"><a href="/opinion/2247480/commonwealth-v-dyer/#684" aria-description="Citation for case: Commonwealth v. Dyer">389 Mass. 677, 684</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1983) (no error where

&quot;[i]n the context of this trial, the charge as a whole was

sufficient to convey&quot; relevant intent standard).   With respect

to the element of willfulness in photographing the alleged

victim, the jury were instructed to consider &quot;the defendant&#x27;s

state of mind at the time of the incident&quot; and whether his

conduct at that time was intentional or accidental, and they

also were instructed that the alleged victim had to be &quot;nude or

partially nude at the time.&quot;   In the context of this trial,

where it was undisputed that the defendant was in the room with

the nude victim at the time the photograph was taken and thus

knew of the victim&#x27;s nudity, a reasonable jury would have

understood from the instructions as a whole that to convict the

defendant, they had to conclude that the defendant willfully

photographed the victim while the victim was in the nude.    There

was therefore no error in the instructions as to the required

to the jury in a variety of ways&quot;).

more harshly.   Recording an unsuspecting nude or partially nude

or both.   G. L. c. 272, § 105 (b), first &amp; second pars.

c. 272, § 105 (c), second par.

their use in other legal contexts and dictionary definitions.&quot;

The plain meaning of &quot;disseminate[]&quot; thus involves spreading or

    In considering the meaning of this term in the context of

§ 105 (c), we take further guidance from the statute&#x27;s structure

and purpose, see Russo, 494 Mass. at 365, including, in

particular, the graduated sentencing scheme created by the

Legislature.   Such a graduated scheme reflects the Legislature&#x27;s

&quot;apparent belief that the greater the harm to the victim, the

harsher the punishment should be for the offender.&quot;    United

States v. Gordon, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4218330"><a href="/opinion/4441077/united-states-v-gordon/#33" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Gordon">875 F.3d 26, 33</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1st Cir. 2017).     See, e.g.,

Commonwealth v. Daley, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6454694"><a href="/opinion/6580538/commonwealth-v-daley/" aria-description="Citation for case: Commonwealth v. Daley">463 Mass. 620</a></span><pre class="inline">, 625 n.4 (2012) (graduated

sentencing scheme for leaving scene of vehicle collision based

on level of harm caused by collision); Commonwealth v. Jean-

Pierre, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6462552"><a href="/opinion/6588202/commonwealth-v-jean-pierre/" aria-description="Citation for case: Commonwealth v. Jean-Pierre">65 Mass. App. Ct. 162</a></span><pre class="inline">, 165 &amp; n.4 (2005) (graduated

scheme of assault and battery offenses and penalties based on

factors including degree of harm to victim).

    Here, the fact that the Legislature set penalties for

violations of § 105 (c) twice as great as those under § 105 (b)

evinces an apparent view on the part of the Legislature that the

harm caused by &quot;disseminat[ing]&quot; the visual image of a victim is

distinctly greater than the initial harm caused by creating the

image.   Indeed, we have already observed that, while the general

purpose of § 105 is to protect both adults and minors against

invasions of their privacy, Wassilie, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7089535"><a href="/opinion/7179113/commonwealth-v-wassilie/#569" aria-description="Citation for case: Commonwealth v. Wassilie">482 Mass. at 569-570</a></span><pre class="inline">, the

&amp; Fellows of Harvard College, </pre><span class="citation no-link">490 Mass. 37</span><pre class="inline">, 56 (2022).   The

co-workers, teachers, fellow students, or random strangers on

[quotation and citation omitted]).

     We conclude, based on the ordinary meaning of

&quot;disseminate[]&quot; and the graduated scheme of which § 105 (c) is a

part, that &quot;disseminat[ion]&quot; under § 105 (c) requires dispersing

the visual image to at least one third party.   By providing for

a far greater penalty for subsequent dissemination of a visual

image created in violation of the statute, the Legislature

apparently acknowledged and sought to redress the distinct harms

to victims caused by spreading such invasive images to others.

Transmitting the image solely to the victim, while no doubt in

many cases a source of intense distress to the victim, does not

entail these same harms.7   And, to the extent there is any

ambiguity regarding whether sending an image of the victim to

the victim constitutes &quot;disseminat[ing]&quot; the image within the

meaning of this statute, we must resolve the ambiguity in the

defendant&#x27;s favor under the rule of lenity.   See Commonwealth v.

Garafalo, </pre><span class="citation no-link">495 Mass. 746</span><pre class="inline">, 761 (2025).

at 595-596.   In Ubeda, supra, the Appeals Court held that the

     In sum, we hold that a violation of § 105 (c) requires

proof that the defendant disseminated the visual image of the

victim taken in violation of § 105 (b) to a third party.

Because the Commonwealth did not present such evidence here, it

failed to meet its burden of proof.

judgment convicting the defendant under G. L. c. 272, § 105 (b),

and reverse the judgment convicting the defendant under G. L.

c. 272, § 105 (c).   We set aside the latter verdict and direct

that a judgment of not guilty enter on that charge.

        Model Jury Instruction -- Secretly Photographing
                   an Unsuspecting Nude Person

     The defendant is charged with secretly (photographing)
(videotaping) (electronically surveilling) a nude or partially
nude person.

     In order to prove the defendant guilty of this offense, the
Commonwealth must prove five things beyond a reasonable doubt:

     First: That the defendant (photographed) (videotaped)
(electronically surveilled) [the alleged victim] while [the
alleged victim] was nude or partially nude;

     Second: That the defendant did so without [the alleged
victim&#x27;s] knowledge and consent;

     Third: That the defendant acted willfully in
(photographing) (videotaping) (electronically surveilling) [the
alleged victim] while the defendant knew [the alleged victim] to
be nude or partially nude;

     Fourth: That the defendant intended to secretly conduct or
hide such activity; and

     Fifth: That [the alleged victim] had a reasonable
expectation of privacy in not being so (photographed)
(videotaped) (electronically surveilled).

     To prove the first element, the Commonwealth must prove
that the defendant (photographed) (videotaped) (electronically
surveilled) [the alleged victim]. (The term &quot;electronically
surveils&quot; means &quot;to view, obtain or record a person&#x27;s visual
image by the use or aid of a camera, cellular or other wireless
communication device, computer, telephone or other electronic
device.&quot;)

     The Commonwealth also must prove that [the alleged victim]
was nude or partially nude at the time of the (photographing)
(videotaping) (electronic surveillance). One is partially nude
when the genitals, buttocks, pubic area or female breast below a
point immediately above the top of the areola is exposed. The
&quot;areola&quot; is the area of dark-colored skin on the breast that
surrounds the nipple.
                                                                   2



     To prove the second element, the Commonwealth must prove
that [the alleged victim] did not know of or consent to the
(photographing) (videotaping) (electronic surveillance).

     To prove the third element, the Commonwealth must prove
that the defendant acted willfully in (photographing)
(videotaping) (electronically surveilling) [the alleged victim]
while the defendant knew [the alleged victim] to be nude or
partially nude. This requires you to make a decision about the
defendant&#x27;s state of mind at the time -- whether his (her)
actions were intentional and not accidental or due to
inadvertence or inattention.

     To prove the fourth element, the Commonwealth must prove
that the defendant intended to secretly conduct or hide such
activity. This element also requires you to make a decision
about the defendant&#x27;s state of mind at the time. A person&#x27;s
intent is his or her purpose or objective. In our everyday
affairs, we often must decide from the actions of others what
their intent is. You should consider all the evidence, and any
reasonable inferences you draw from the evidence, in determining
whether the defendant acted with the intent to secretly conduct
or hide his (her) activity.

     To prove the fifth element, the Commonwealth must prove
that [the alleged victim] had a reasonable expectation of
privacy in not being (photographed) (videotaped) (electronically
surveilled) at the time. Whether such an expectation is
reasonable under the circumstances of this case is to be
measured by common understanding and practices.

     If the Commonwealth proved all five elements beyond a
reasonable doubt, you should return a verdict of guilty. If the
Commonwealth failed to prove any element beyond a reasonable
doubt, you must find the defendant not guilty.
