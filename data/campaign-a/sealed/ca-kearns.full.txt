Justice Katherine M. Bidegaray delivered the Opinion of the Court.

¶1      Kenneth Michael Kearns (Ken) appeals three rulings of the Montana Eleventh

Judicial District Court, Flathead County, concerning a student loan that he agreed to pay

when he and Barbara King Kearns (Barbara) dissolved their marriage. The District Court

denied his request for additional account access, held him in contempt for nonpayment,

entered a $388,741.24 judgment in favor of Barbara, and awarded her $3,237.50 in attorney

fees.

¶2      We restate the issues as follows:

        1. Whether the District Court erred by denying Ken’s request for additional access
           to the Parent PLUS loan account.

        2. Whether the District Court erred by holding Ken in contempt for failing to make
           monthly payments on the loan after the June 2024 order.

        3. Whether the District Court erred by entering an immediately collectible money
           judgment in Barbara’s favor for the entire unpaid loan balance.

        4. Whether the District Court erred by awarding Barbara $3,237.50 in attorney
           fees.

        5. Whether Barbara is entitled to appellate attorney fees and sanctions.

We affirm the account-access ruling and the contempt determination based on the August

and September 2024 nonpayments. We vacate the full-balance judgment and remand for

further proceedings concerning the enforcement remedy.         We affirm the $3,237.50

attorney-fee award, remand for determination of reasonable appellate fees within the

applicable contractual provisions, and deny appellate sanctions.




                                             2
                 FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

¶3    Ken and Barbara executed a Marital and Property Settlement Agreement (MPSA)

on November 29, 2012. Paragraph 10 identifies marital debts by reference to schedules in

a bankruptcy proceeding. In lieu of additional maintenance, Ken agreed “to be solely

responsible for said debt in its entirety” and to “indemnify and hold [Barbara] harmless

thereto.” The parties do not dispute that the assigned debts included the Parent PLUS loan

at issue here. The December 20, 2012 Dissolution Decree incorporated the MPSA and

directed both parties to comply with it. Paragraph 19 of the MPSA prohibited modification

by a subsequent court order.

¶4    Ken resolved the other assigned debts but made no payment on the Parent PLUS

loan until after Barbara sought contempt in 2022. The parties resolved that proceeding

through an October 11, 2022 Stipulation. They acknowledged an estimated loan balance

of $342,230 and an existing federal payment pause. They agreed to seek the addition of

Ken to the account and removal of Barbara if possible. Otherwise, they would direct the

servicer to mail monthly statements to Ken. Paragraph 2 then provided “Kenneth will

make the monthly payments on the account.”

¶5    Paragraph 3 of the Stipulation stated that failure to make the monthly payment

would subject Ken to contempt and make him responsible for attorney fees incurred to

compel compliance. The Stipulation also required application of Ken’s share of the

proceeds from the sale of a business condominium to the loan. Paragraph 7 recognized a

claim against his estate for an unpaid balance if he predeceased Barbara. The parties thus



                                            3
specified ongoing payment obligations while preserving Ken’s responsibility for the

remaining debt. Barbara remained the named borrower.

¶6     Barbara again sought contempt in May 2024. After a June 5 hearing, the District

Court entered its June 17, 2024 order holding Ken in contempt. The court explained that

Aidvantage, which was servicing the Parent PLUS loan at issue, had calculated a $590.33

monthly payment after Barbara applied for income-contingent repayment and that Ken had

not paid the full amount despite notice through counsel. The court entered judgment for

delinquent and current payments and awarded enforcement-related attorney fees. It warned

that further failure to meet the monthly-payment obligation would result in a judgment for

the entire outstanding loan balance.

¶7     On June 6, the day after the hearing, Ken requested and obtained a forbearance.

Aidvantage removed it on June 10 after Barbara objected. Ken later maintained that a

representative arranged a separate hardship deferment during a July 30 call, with payments

to resume in October 2024.       He acknowledged making no payments in August or

September 2024. Ken also changed the mailing address, telephone number, and email

address on the account to his information without notifying Barbara. After Barbara stopped

receiving account communications and discovered that her tax document had gone to Ken,

she had Aidvantage restore her information and impose additional account access

restrictions.

¶8     In April 2025, Ken moved for account information, documents, reinstatement as an

authorized recipient of information, and a username and password. He invoked equitable

subrogation and asserted that he needed access to negotiate repayment terms and meet his

                                            4
obligation. Barbara opposed unrestricted access and moved to enforce the Decree or hold

Ken in contempt. Among alternative remedies, she requested a judgment for the full loan

balance that she could execute against his assets.

¶9     At the July 28, 2025 hearing, Ken testified about his health, substantial debts, and

difficulty sustaining the monthly payment. He attributed the payment amount to Barbara’s

alleged failure to recertify her income.     The District Court instead credited written

repayment-plan approvals that undermined his assertion. A January 2025 approval reduced

the monthly payment to $511.31. The court also found that Ken possessed the payment

amount and due date, and that his unauthorized account changes justified restrictions on

broader access.

¶10    The payment history showed no August or September 2024 payment and no

February 2025 posting, but two payments posted in March 2025. Ken attributed the August

and September nonpayments to the deferment and disputed that he missed the February

payment, explaining that processing delayed the posting to March. Both parties understood

the account to be in good standing at the hearing. The September 16, 2025 order

nevertheless held Ken in contempt, denied additional account access, directed Barbara to

provide the payoff amount, and ordered submission of a proposed full-balance judgment.

¶11    On October 8, 2025, the District Court entered judgment in favor of Barbara for

$388,741.24, the amount Aidvantage quoted for payoff by U.S. mail in account information

dated October 7. The court concluded that Ken was responsible for paying the loan in full

and was therefore liable to Barbara for the unpaid debt. The judgment awarded Barbara

the money, imposed ten-percent annual interest, and authorized ordinary collection

                                             5
remedies. It did not direct Barbara to apply collections to the loan or address corresponding

credit for payments to the creditor. After Ken filed a notice of nonobjection to the attorney

fees provided for in the September order and claimed in counsel’s affidavit, the court

awarded Barbara $3,237.50.

                               STANDARD OF REVIEW

¶12    Section 3-1-523(2), MCA, permits review because the contempt order includes

ancillary rulings affecting substantial rights. We examine whether the District Court acted

within its jurisdiction and whether the evidence supports contempt. We review findings of

fact for clear error and conclusions of law for correctness. In re Marriage of Steinbeisser,

</pre><span class="citation" data-id="11226031"><a href="/opinion/10759446/marriage-of-steinbeisser/" aria-description="Citation for case: Marriage of: Steinbeisser">2025 MT 289</a></span><pre class="inline">, ¶¶ 16–17, </pre><span class="citation no-link">425 Mont. 306</span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation no-link">581 P.3d 32</span><pre class="inline">. Contract interpretation presents a

legal question. In re Marriage of Harms, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2022 MT 41</span><pre class="inline">, ¶ 12, </pre><span class="citation no-link">408 Mont. 15</span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation no-link">504 P.3d 1108</span><pre class="inline">.

Whether the agreements and applicable law authorize the challenged monetary recovery

presents a legal question that we review for correctness. See In re Marriage of Harms,

¶ 12; In re Marriage of Steinbeisser, ¶ 17.

                                      DISCUSSION

¶13    1. Whether the District Court erred by denying Ken’s request for additional access
          to the Parent PLUS loan account.

¶14    Ken first argues that the District Court failed to address equitable subrogation and

enter findings sufficient for review. M. R. Civ. P. 52(a)(3) generally makes findings and

conclusions unnecessary on motions unless another rule provides otherwise. Del Duca v.

Skydancer, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="11105427"><a href="/opinion/10638840/del-duca-v-skydancer/#11" aria-description="Citation for case: Del Duca v. Skydancer">2025 MT 156, ¶ 11</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation no-link">423 Mont. 115</span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation no-link">572 P.3d 804</span><pre class="inline">. Here, the court entered

detailed findings and expressly denied the requested additional access. The pertinent


                                              6
question is whether those findings and the accompanying reasoning disclose the basis for

that ruling. See Byrum v. Andren, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="888046"><a href="/opinion/888046/byrum-v-andren/" aria-description="Citation for case: Byrum v. Andren">2007 MT 107</a></span><pre class="inline">, ¶¶ 52–55, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="888046"><a href="/opinion/888046/byrum-v-andren/" aria-description="Citation for case: Byrum v. Andren">337 Mont. 167</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="888046"><a href="/opinion/888046/byrum-v-andren/" aria-description="Citation for case: Byrum v. Andren">159 P.3d 1062</a></span><pre class="inline">.

¶15    Equitable subrogation generally permits a person who pays an obligation for which

another bears ultimate responsibility to succeed to rights associated with the satisfied claim.

It places the burden on the person who should bear it. Skauge v. Mountain States Tel. &amp;

Tel. Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="876539"><a href="/opinion/876539/skauge-v-mountain-states-telephone-telegraph-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Skauge v. Mountain States Telephone &amp; Telegraph Co.">172 Mont. 521</a></span><pre class="inline">, 524–26, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="876539"><a href="/opinion/876539/skauge-v-mountain-states-telephone-telegraph-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Skauge v. Mountain States Telephone &amp; Telegraph Co.">565 P.2d 628</a></span><pre class="inline">, 630–31 (1977). Ken does not seek to

exercise creditor rights arising from a payment. He seeks the administrative rights of the

named borrower to manage the account and negotiate repayment terms. The subrogation

principle Ken invokes concerns succession to creditor rights; it does not establish his

claimed entitlement to exercise the administrative rights of the named borrower. See Schuff

v. A.T. Klemens &amp; Son, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9510470"><a href="/opinion/885205/schuff-v-at-klemens-son/#91" aria-description="Citation for case: Schuff v. A.T. Klemens &amp; Son">2000 MT 357, ¶ 91</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9510470"><a href="/opinion/885205/schuff-v-at-klemens-son/" aria-description="Citation for case: Schuff v. A.T. Klemens &amp; Son">303 Mont. 274</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9510470"><a href="/opinion/885205/schuff-v-at-klemens-son/" aria-description="Citation for case: Schuff v. A.T. Klemens &amp; Son">16 P.3d 1002</a></span><pre class="inline">. Moreover,

although Barbara remains liable to the creditor, Ken bears responsibility for the debt as

between the former spouses.

¶16    The District Court also had a sufficient factual basis to deny unrestricted account

control. Ken changed contact information without Barbara’s consent, causing her to stop

receiving mailed and emailed communications about an account for which she remained

liable. The court found that he nevertheless had the information necessary to remit

payments and protected his access to the payoff amount. Those findings explain the refusal

to require disclosure of credentials that would enable further account changes. The

omission of the words “equitable subrogation” does not prevent review of the ruling. We

affirm the account-access ruling as a denial of the unrestricted access Ken claimed under



                                              7
subrogation. We do not read that ruling to extinguish the provisions for monthly statements

in the Stipulation or the information the September order entitles him to receive.

¶17    The record likewise supports rejecting Ken’s assertion that the access restrictions

made payment impossible.       Impracticability requires an event occurring without the

obligor’s fault whose nonoccurrence formed a basic contractual assumption, and the

obligor must have taken virtually every action within his power to perform. Lewis &amp; Clark

Cnty. v. Wirth, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6345604"><a href="/opinion/6473493/l-c-county-v-wirth/#36" aria-description="Citation for case: L &amp; C County v. Wirth">2022 MT 105, ¶ 36</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation no-link">409 Mont. 1</span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation no-link">510 P.3d 1206</span><pre class="inline">. Ken continued remitting

payments despite the restrictions, and written approvals contradicted his claim that Barbara

had failed to supply the income information necessary to establish the payment amount.

These facts do not establish his ability to pay the entire loan immediately or sustain

payments indefinitely. They do support the determination that the restrictions did not

prevent the performance at issue.

¶18    2. Whether the District Court erred by holding Ken in contempt for failing to make
          monthly payments on the loan after the June 2024 order.

¶19    We next address contempt. A contempt determination must rest on disobedience of

a definite, certain, and specific judicial command. Goodover v. Lindey’s Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="883036"><a href="/opinion/883036/goodover-v-lindeys-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Goodover v. LINDEY&#x27;S INC.">257 Mont.

38</a></span><pre class="inline">, 41–42, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="883036"><a href="/opinion/883036/goodover-v-lindeys-inc/#701" aria-description="Citation for case: Goodover v. LINDEY&#x27;S INC.">847 P.2d 699, 701</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1993). The Decree here directed compliance with the

MPSA. The parties subsequently specified monthly payments in the Stipulation, and the

June order enforced that obligation after identifying the established payment amount. The

basis for contempt is the judicially enforced payment obligation, not merely the statement

in the Stipulation that a breach would constitute contempt.




                                             8
¶20    Read together, the Stipulation and June order required continuing monthly payments

once repayment began and the amount was established. The Stipulation acknowledged the

existing federal pause before repayment commenced; it did not give Ken a continuing

option to suspend his promised performance through a separate arrangement with the

servicer. The June order described and enforced that promise. It did not amend the

Stipulation to make avoidance of federal default the measure of compliance.            Even

accepting Ken’s account of the later hardship deferment, the arrangement did not itself alter

his payment obligation to Barbara. This conclusion concerns the voluntary arrangement

Ken described, not the effect of any independently applicable federal prohibition or

mandatory suspension. See § 28-3-202, MCA.

¶21    Ken admitted that he made no August or September payments. The court found his

noncompliance willful after considering his explanations, financial circumstances, and

account changes. His admitted nonpayments support the contempt determination without

resolving whether he timely made the February payment. We therefore affirm contempt

on the August and September grounds. Our affirmance does not resolve the disputed

February payment, which the court must address if relevant to the relief it awards on

remand.

¶22    3. Whether the District Court erred by entering an immediately collectible money
          judgment in Barbara’s favor for the entire unpaid loan balance.

¶23    The full-balance judgment presents a different question. Ken preserved an objection

to that remedy. At the hearing, counsel argued that entering judgment would impose

payment terms contrary to the settlement agreement. In his opening brief, Ken challenged


                                             9
acceleration and expressly objected to an award requiring him to pay the entire debt to

Barbara. In his reply, Ken reiterated that he had not agreed to a single payment. Barbara

responded that the judgment would permit collection and use of the proceeds to pay the

loan. The permissible scope of that recovery is therefore before us, notwithstanding the

emphasis Ken placed on subrogation and impossibility. See Becker v. Rosebud Operating

Servs., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="888349"><a href="/opinion/888349/becker-v-rosebud-operating-services-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Becker v. ROSEBUD OPERATING SERVICES, INC.">2008 MT 285</a></span><pre class="inline">, ¶¶ 17–18, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="888349"><a href="/opinion/888349/becker-v-rosebud-operating-services-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Becker v. ROSEBUD OPERATING SERVICES, INC.">345 Mont. 368</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="888349"><a href="/opinion/888349/becker-v-rosebud-operating-services-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Becker v. ROSEBUD OPERATING SERVICES, INC.">191 P.3d 435</a></span><pre class="inline">.

¶24   Section 40-4-201(5), MCA, permits enforcement of an incorporated separation

agreement through remedies available to enforce a judgment, including contempt, and as

contract terms. The statute authorizes enforcement of the obligation the parties undertook.

The availability of judgment-enforcement remedies does not answer whether the

agreements entitle Barbara to immediate recovery of the entire unpaid balance. Courts

must respect the agreement and the final adjudication rather than substitute a different

bargain without a proper legal basis. In re Marriage of Harms, ¶¶ 21–25. The distinction

is especially important where, as here, paragraph 19 of the MPSA expressly prohibits later

judicial modification. See § 40-4-201(6), MCA.

¶25   The District Court relied on § 28-3-601, MCA, because the 2012 MPSA contained

no payoff deadline. But the parties did not leave the manner of performance unaddressed.

In 2022, with an identified balance exceeding $342,000, they agreed to monthly payments,

application of specified sale proceeds, and preservation of a claim against Ken’s estate.

Section 28-3-601, MCA, supplies a reasonable time for performance when the agreement

specifies none. The statute also requires immediate performance of an exactly ascertained

obligation that a party can perform instantly. In applying the reasonable-time provision,

                                            10
we consider the surrounding circumstances and the time necessary to perform what the

agreement requires, rather than impose a uniform period for performance. Summer Night

Oil Co., LLC v. Munoz, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="889712"><a href="/opinion/889712/summer-night-oil-co-llc-v-munoz/#26" aria-description="Citation for case: SUMMER NIGHT OIL CO., LLC v. Munoz">2011 MT 202, ¶ 26</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="889712"><a href="/opinion/889712/summer-night-oil-co-llc-v-munoz/" aria-description="Citation for case: SUMMER NIGHT OIL CO., LLC v. Munoz">361 Mont. 424</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="889712"><a href="/opinion/889712/summer-night-oil-co-llc-v-munoz/" aria-description="Citation for case: SUMMER NIGHT OIL CO., LLC v. Munoz">259 P.3d 778</a></span><pre class="inline">. Both provisions

require identification of the performance the parties promised. The absence of a final

payoff date in the 2012 MPSA therefore does not resolve whether, after the 2022

Stipulation, Ken owed Barbara an immediate payment equal to the entire loan balance.

The court had to consider the effect of the Stipulation before deciding what further

performance Barbara could compel.

¶26    The estate provision does not require Barbara to await Ken’s death before enforcing

his obligations. Conversely, its preservation of responsibility for the unpaid balance does

not establish that she may collect that balance immediately during his lifetime. A party

may remain responsible for an entire debt while the agreement prescribes installment

performance. The absence of a release answers whether responsibility continues; it does

not resolve when the entire monetary equivalent becomes recoverable.

¶27    Our decision in Rader v. Taylor, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9855859"><a href="/opinion/1299503/rader-v-taylor/" aria-description="Citation for case: Rader v. Taylor">134 Mont. 419</a></span><pre class="inline">, 428–31, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9855859"><a href="/opinion/1299503/rader-v-taylor/" aria-description="Citation for case: Rader v. Taylor">333 P.2d 480</a></span><pre class="inline">, 486–88

(1958), distinguishes enforcement of accrued installments from acceleration of future

performance. There, a contract for deed did not authorize a demand for the entire unpaid

purchase price upon default. Although Rader involved a different contract, the same

interpretive restraint applies: alone, a default does not itself supply a contractual or

statutory right to accelerate. Here, neither the MPSA nor the Stipulation expressly makes

all remaining payments immediately due upon a missed installment. A different basis for

presently recoverable relief therefore must support the full-balance judgment.

                                            11
¶28    The indemnity promise could provide a basis for recovery distinct from collection

of delinquent installments, but its presence does not complete the analysis. Section

28-11-314(1), MCA, permits recovery under an indemnity against liability once liability

exists. Subsection (2), by contrast, requires the indemnitee to have paid the specified

claims, demands, damages, or costs before recovering under an indemnity against those

actual losses.   These rules apply unless the parties’ agreement expresses a contrary

intention. Section 28-11-313, MCA. Thus, Barbara need not necessarily pay the creditor

before recovering from Ken, and a judgment in her favor is not categorically improper. We

therefore consider whether the later Stipulation expresses a contrary intention concerning

immediate recovery of the entire loan balance.

¶29    The District Court did not make that determination. Its conclusion that Ken is

responsible for the loan “in full and is liable to Barbara for the unpaid debt under Montana

law” joins the allocation of responsibility to an immediate monetary recovery without

explaining the legal connection. The payoff documentation establishes what would satisfy

the creditor, not necessarily the amount Barbara could presently recover from Ken under

their agreements. We may affirm a correct result despite incorrect reasoning. Dewey v.

Stringer, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2675901"><a href="/opinion/2675901/dewey-v-stringer/#16" aria-description="Citation for case: Dewey v. Stringer">2014 MT 136, ¶ 16</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2675901"><a href="/opinion/2675901/dewey-v-stringer/" aria-description="Citation for case: Dewey v. Stringer">375 Mont. 176</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2675901"><a href="/opinion/2675901/dewey-v-stringer/" aria-description="Citation for case: Dewey v. Stringer">325 P.3d 1236</a></span><pre class="inline">. But even an indemnity

against liability remains subject to a contrary intention concerning performance. Section

28-11-313, MCA. Here, with knowledge of the substantial outstanding balance, the parties

later agreed to monthly payments, application of specified sale proceeds, and preservation

of a claim against Ken’s estate.       Read together, those provisions preserve Ken’s

responsibility for the entire debt while prescribing how he must perform that obligation.

                                            12
Interpreting the monthly-payment provisions to allow immediate recovery of the entire

balance after a single missed installment would undermine the parties’ agreed method of

performance. We construe the Stipulation as qualifying the timing of recovery under the

earlier indemnity promise, not extinguishing that promise. Barbara’s continuing liability

to the creditor therefore did not, standing alone, make the entire balance immediately

recoverable from Ken notwithstanding the agreed installment performance. His missed

payments justified enforcement of that performance; they did not supply an acceleration

term that the parties omitted. See § 28-3-202, MCA; Rader, 134 Mont. at 428–31, 333 P.2d

at 486–88. We therefore conclude that the District Court lacked authority under the parties’

agreements to accelerate the entire unpaid loan balance based on Ken’s failure to make the

required monthly payments.

¶30    M. R. Civ. P. 70 does not supply the missing substantive right.           It provides

mechanisms to enforce a judgment requiring a specific act. Subsection (d), on which the

language in the September order appears to rely, concerns execution or assistance upon a

judgment or order for possession. M. R. Civ. P. 70(a), (d)-(e). The rule does not

independently authorize conversion of a promise to pay a creditor in installments into an

unconditional personal judgment for every remaining installment. Likewise, the June

warning informed Ken of the consequence the court contemplated; it did not establish that

the parties had agreed to acceleration or resolve the contractual basis for that consequence.

The June order entered judgment for then-due amounts and announced a consequence for

future noncompliance; it did not enter the full-balance judgment that Ken now challenges.



                                             13
The earlier adjudicated payment obligations remain intact, but the warning does not

dispense with review of the remedy actually imposed in 2025.

¶31    The dissolution cases do not eliminate that distinction. In In re Marriage of

Scoffield, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="882927"><a href="/opinion/882927/in-re-the-marriage-of-scoffield/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Marriage of Scoffield">258 Mont. 337</a></span><pre class="inline">, 339–40, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="882927"><a href="/opinion/882927/in-re-the-marriage-of-scoffield/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Marriage of Scoffield">852 P.2d 664</a></span><pre class="inline">, 665–66 (1993), the District Court entered

judgment in favor of a former spouse for assigned marital debts and stayed execution on

the condition that the obligor make installments. The appeal concerned which debts he had

assumed, not the power to disregard a later installment agreement. In re Marriage of

Scoffield demonstrates that an interspousal judgment can enforce assigned debt; it does not

establish that this judgment follows from nonpayment alone.

¶32    In In re Marriage of Steinbeisser, ¶ 22, we expressly distinguished modification of

a decree from the contempt sanction. We then addressed the statutory conditions for

reopening a property disposition under § 40-4-208(3), MCA, and the evidence supporting

the modified remedy. In re Marriage of Steinbeisser, ¶¶ 24–27. Here, the District Court

did not identify such a basis for reopening the disposition. Its concerns about prolonged

nonpayment, the growing balance, and the risk to Barbara support effective enforcement,

but they do not dispense with identifying the obligation and legal authority that support the

remedy selected.

¶33    Barbara defends the judgment as a means of obtaining funds to pay the loan. The

written judgment, however, permits her to collect the entire payoff amount without

directing application of collections to that obligation. At the hearing, the judge explained

that entry of the judgment would replace Ken’s monthly payments with collection through

counsel. These features underscore the substantive change in performance. We do not

                                             14
presume that Barbara would retain the proceeds without paying the creditor, and we do not

hold that a money judgment must always name the creditor as payee. But an expectation

that collections will satisfy the loan cannot substitute for establishing the right to the

judgment and defining how the relief enforces the underlying obligation.

¶34    We therefore vacate paragraph 2 of the operative September 16 order and the

resulting October 8 judgment. We remand for determination of an enforcement remedy

consistent with the MPSA, the Stipulation, and applicable law. The remaining tasks

concern the extent of uncured noncompliance, the credit due for payments already made,

and the appropriate remedy for the violations established under this Opinion. They do

not require the District Court to reconsider our construction of the agreements. See

M. R. App. P. 19(1)(c). The court may enforce Ken’s obligations through a legally

authorized remedy, but it may not accelerate the entire unpaid loan balance based on the

missed monthly payments or reinstate the vacated judgment under another label without

an independent legal basis for that relief.

¶35    The court may enforce the monthly-payment obligation and determine the amount

necessary to cure adjudicated deficiencies. That amount must account for subsequent

payments and existing judgments without counting an obligation twice. Section 27-1-303,

MCA. Because contempt rests on Ken’s separate promise to make monthly payments, the

figure need not be identical to whatever Aidvantage currently classifies as delinquent. If

the District Court orders Ken to pay amounts toward the Parent PLUS loan, it must require

payment to the creditor or direct application of those amounts to the outstanding loan

balance and provide corresponding credit for payments made. The court must distinguish

                                              15
those payments from separately authorized fees or other relief. Our decision leaves the

underlying debt allocation and the June 2024 adjudicated obligations undisturbed.

¶36    4. Whether the District Court erred by awarding Barbara $3,237.50 in attorney
          fees.

¶37    The attorney-fee award requires separate treatment. Paragraph 15 of the MPSA

requires reasonable fees to the prevailing party in an action to enforce, modify, or interpret

the agreement. Paragraph 3 of the Stipulation separately makes Ken responsible for fees

incurred when Barbara must compel compliance with his monthly-payment obligation. We

review contractual entitlement for correctness; clear fee provisions control. In re Marriage

of Fossen, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6536326"><a href="/opinion/6658894/in-re-marriage-of-fossen/#8" aria-description="Citation for case: In re Marriage of Fossen">2019 MT 119, ¶¶ 8, 11</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6536326"><a href="/opinion/6658894/in-re-marriage-of-fossen/" aria-description="Citation for case: In re Marriage of Fossen">395 Mont. 495</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6536326"><a href="/opinion/6658894/in-re-marriage-of-fossen/" aria-description="Citation for case: In re Marriage of Fossen">443 P.3d 418</a></span><pre class="inline">. Our contempt holding

preserves Barbara’s entitlement to reasonable fees for that enforcement; Ken’s

unsuccessful subrogation theory does not defeat the contractual entitlement.

¶38    The District Court awarded Barbara $3,237.50 in attorney fees after considering the

reasonableness of the requested amount under the factors identified in In re Marriage of

Mease, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9511307"><a href="/opinion/886994/in-re-the-marriage-of-mease/#58" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Marriage of Mease">2004 MT 59, ¶ 58</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9511307"><a href="/opinion/886994/in-re-the-marriage-of-mease/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Marriage of Mease">320 Mont. 229</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9511307"><a href="/opinion/886994/in-re-the-marriage-of-mease/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Marriage of Mease">92 P.3d 1148</a></span><pre class="inline">. Ken expressly notified the court

that he did not object to the fees provided for in the September 16 order and claimed in

counsel’s September 30 affidavit. Although we vacate the full-balance judgment, Barbara

prevailed in establishing Ken’s noncompliance with the monthly-payment obligation, and

the parties’ agreements authorize reasonable fees for enforcing that obligation. The

unsuccessful request for the full-balance remedy arose from the same enforcement

proceeding and does not, by itself, render the related legal work unreasonable or require

reduction of the award. Given Ken’s express nonobjection and the District Court’s


                                             16
reasonableness determination, we find no basis to disturb the $3,237.50 award. We affirm

the October 30, 2025 attorney-fee order.

¶39    5. Whether Barbara is entitled to appellate attorney fees and sanctions.

¶40    Barbara also requests appellate sanctions and contractual appellate attorney fees.

Ken has obtained relief from the full-balance judgment, and his appeal presented

substantial questions about the permissible enforcement remedy.            Sanctions under

M. R. App. P. 19(5) are unwarranted. His lack of success on account access and contempt

does not make the appeal frivolous.

¶41    Barbara invokes the contractual fee provisions as well. Her partial success requires

us to examine the scope of the contractual fee provisions and the matters on which she

prevailed. See In re Marriage of Winters, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9511291"><a href="/opinion/886971/marriage-of-winters-v-winters/" aria-description="Citation for case: Marriage of Winters v. Winters">2004 MT 82</a></span><pre class="inline">, ¶¶ 59–61, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9511291"><a href="/opinion/886971/marriage-of-winters-v-winters/" aria-description="Citation for case: Marriage of Winters v. Winters">320 Mont. 459</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9511291"><a href="/opinion/886971/marriage-of-winters-v-winters/" aria-description="Citation for case: Marriage of Winters v. Winters">87 P.3d

1005</a></span><pre class="inline">. Section 28-3-704(1), MCA, makes contractual attorney-fee rights reciprocal and

entitles the prevailing party to reasonable fees within the scope of the contractual right. A

party may prevail on the particular claim covered by a fee provision despite mixed results

elsewhere in the litigation. TransAction Network, Inc. v. Wellington Techs., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="885356"><a href="/opinion/885356/transaction-network-inc-v-wellington-technologies-inc/#24" aria-description="Citation for case: Transaction Network, Inc. v. Wellington Technologies, Inc.">2000 MT

223, ¶¶ 24-35</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="885356"><a href="/opinion/885356/transaction-network-inc-v-wellington-technologies-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Transaction Network, Inc. v. Wellington Technologies, Inc.">301 Mont. 212</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="885356"><a href="/opinion/885356/transaction-network-inc-v-wellington-technologies-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Transaction Network, Inc. v. Wellington Technologies, Inc.">7 P.3d 409</a></span><pre class="inline">. Paragraph 3 of the Stipulation separately assigns

Ken responsibility for future fees relative to compelling compliance with the

monthly-payment agreement. Barbara prevailed in establishing Ken’s violation of that

obligation and successfully defended the resulting contempt determination on appeal.

Vacating the full-balance remedy does not disturb that success. Paragraph 15 of the MPSA

provides an additional contractual basis for reasonable fees attributable to Barbara’s

successful enforcement of Ken’s continuing responsibility for the debt. She therefore

                                             17
remains entitled to reasonable fees for enforcing the monthly-payment obligation under the

parties’ agreements, subject to an assessment of the work reasonably necessary and the

results obtained. The full-balance request was a proposed remedy for the noncompliance;

our rejection of that remedy does not automatically place all work concerning it outside the

contractual fee provision. Contractual enforcement fees may include fees on appeal. See

In re Marriage of Cini, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="889616"><a href="/opinion/889616/in-re-the-marriage-of-cini/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Marriage of Cini">2011 MT 295</a></span><pre class="inline">, ¶¶ 27–28, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="889616"><a href="/opinion/889616/in-re-the-marriage-of-cini/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Marriage of Cini">363 Mont. 1</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="889616"><a href="/opinion/889616/in-re-the-marriage-of-cini/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Marriage of Cini">266 P.3d 1257</a></span><pre class="inline">. Barbara’s

entitlement therefore includes reasonable fees for defending the contempt determination

on appeal.

¶42    On remand, the court shall determine the reasonable appellate fees attributable to

enforcing the continuing monthly-payment obligation.          The court must assess the

reasonable amount in light of Barbara’s success in establishing noncompliance and her

failure to sustain the full-balance remedy. But the court need not exclude work merely

because it also concerned the full-balance remedy. When compensable and unsuccessful

matters share a common factual core or related legal theories and the court cannot

reasonably divide the work, it may assess the significance of the overall relief obtained in

relation to the hours reasonably expended. Plath v. Schonrock, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9883185"><a href="/opinion/886635/plath-v-schonrock/#37" aria-description="Citation for case: Plath v. Schonrock">2003 MT 21, ¶ 37</a></span><pre class="inline">,

</pre><span class="citation" data-id="9883185"><a href="/opinion/886635/plath-v-schonrock/" aria-description="Citation for case: Plath v. Schonrock">314 Mont. 101</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9883185"><a href="/opinion/886635/plath-v-schonrock/" aria-description="Citation for case: Plath v. Schonrock">64 P.3d 984</a></span><pre class="inline">. The court must consider the results obtained together with

the other applicable factors governing a reasonable fee, rather than reduce the award

mechanically because Barbara did not obtain every form of relief she requested. Plath,

¶¶ 36–38. The court must explain the reasonable amount awarded. Neither the contractual

entitlement nor the contempt determination makes every claimed appellate fee reasonable.

Each party shall bear his or her own costs on appeal. M. R. App. P. 19(3)(a).

                                     CONCLUSION

¶43    We affirm the account-access ruling, without diminishing the existing rights to

statements and payment information, the contempt determination based on the August and

September 2024 nonpayments, and the award of the $3,237.50 attorney-fee award. We

vacate the directive for a full-balance judgment in paragraph 2 of the September 16, 2025

order and the October 8, 2025 judgment. We remand for determination of the enforcement

remedy and reasonable appellate attorney fees consistent with this Opinion. We deny

Barbara’s request for appellate sanctions.

¶44    Affirmed in part, vacated in part, and remanded.
