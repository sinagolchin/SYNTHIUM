                        INTRODUCTION
   Mark A. Koch appealed from a document designated as
a judgment. The Nebraska Court of Appeals summarily dis-
missed the appeal for lack of jurisdiction, stating that judgment
had not been rendered. Upon further review, we conclude that
the district court rendered judgment by entering a single written
document that explicitly denied “[a]ll the relief requested” and
stated “[j]udgment is entered . . . as to each cause of action.”
Accordingly, we reverse the Court of Appeals’ dismissal, and
remand the cause to that court for further proceedings.

                       BACKGROUND
                 District Court Proceedings
   Koch filed a complaint against the City of Sargent, Nebraska
(the City), asserting numerous causes of action. He asked
the court to void all actions taken at a particular city coun-
cil meeting due to purported violations of Nebraska’s Open
Meetings Act 1 and other procedural deficiencies. The City
moved to dismiss for failure to state a claim upon which relief
could be granted.
   Following a hearing in December 2025, the court entered
an “Order Dismissing Certain Causes of Action.” It dismissed
most of the causes of action and set forth the allegations
in the five causes of action that survived the City’s motion
to dismiss.

                           ANALYSIS
                   Scope of Further Review
   Further review by this court is a matter of judicial discre-
tion. 5 In exercising our discretion, we may limit the issues
to one or more of those raised by the parties and may notice
plain error. 6 Koch assigned error to the Court of Appeals’
dismissal of his appeal, but his brief contains no discussion
of the alleged error as required by § 2-102(F)(3). We elect to
review the dismissal for plain error.
   [2,3] Plain error is error plainly evident from the record
which prejudicially affects a substantial right of a litigant and
is of such a nature that to leave it uncorrected would cause
a miscarriage of justice or result in damage to the integrity,
reputation, or fairness of the judicial process. 7 When review-
ing proceedings for plain error, appellate courts are neither
constrained by the specific arguments raised in the briefs nor
required to consider every error that may have occurred in
the lower court. 8 Here, we consider only whether the Court of
Appeals erred in determining that it lacked jurisdiction because
the district court had not rendered judgment.

                     Appellate Jurisdiction
   [4] To determine whether it has jurisdiction, an appellate
court looks to </pre><span class="citation no-link">Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 25-1911</span><pre class="inline"> (Reissue 2016) and
25-1912 (Cum. Supp. 2024). Together, these statutes gener-
ally prescribe that for an appellate court to acquire jurisdic-
tion of an appeal, the party must be appealing from a judg-
ment or decree rendered or from a final order. 9 There is no
contention that the appeal was from a final order as defined
by </pre><span class="citation no-link">Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-1902</span><pre class="inline"> (Cum. Supp. 2024); instead, the
jurisdictional question is whether the district court rendered a
final judgment.
   [5] We are guided by the definitions in </pre><span class="citation no-link">Neb. Rev. Stat.
§ 25-1301</span><pre class="inline"> (Cum. Supp. 2024). The statute defines a “judg-
ment” as “the final determination of the rights of the parties
in an action.” 10 And it provides that “[r]endition of a judgment
is the act of the court, or a judge thereof, in signing a single
written document stating all of the relief granted or denied in
an action.” 11 Based on these definitions, we have stated that
“a ‘judgment rendered,’ as required by § 25-1911, is a final
determination of the rights of the parties in an action, which
is set forth by the court in a single, signed written document
stating all of the relief granted or denied in an action.” 12

requirement of § 25-1301(2) on multiple occasions since its
addition to the statute in 2020. 13 In the background section

judgment can exist without the court explicitly stating that the
case is dismissed.

                            CONCLUSION
   As set forth above, we conclude the following:
• A “judgment rendered,” as required by § 25-1911, is a final
  determination of the parties’ rights in an action, which is set
  forth by the court in a single written document signed by the
  court stating all of the relief granted or denied in an action.
• The district court’s December 2025 order, which sustained the
  City’s motion to dismiss as to most of Koch’s causes of action
  and set forth the allegations in the causes of action that sur-
  vived dismissal, was an interlocutory order.
• The district court rendered judgment in April 2026 when it
  signed a single written document stating both that “[a]ll the
  relief requested . . . is denied” and that “[j]udgment is entered
  against [Koch] and for the [City] as to each cause of action.”
• Because Koch appealed from the April 2026 judgment within
  30 days, the Court of Appeals had appellate jurisdiction.
   We therefore reverse the Court of Appeals’ summary dis-
missal for lack of jurisdiction and remand the cause to that
court for further proceedings.
