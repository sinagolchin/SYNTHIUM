Justice Katherine M. Bidegaray delivered the Opinion of the Court.

¶1     Rhonda Purtle appeals three orders of the First Judicial District Court, Lewis and

Clark County, concerning the estate of her mother, Mary Jo Bradford. On the requests of

Purtle’s brothers, Robert Bates and Kenneth Bates (the Bateses), the District Court

removed Purtle as personal representative, directed appointment of a special administrator,

and froze financial accounts that Purtle claims passed to her outside probate. The court

removed Purtle without the statutorily required hearing. It later appointed a special

administrator without a hearing and without an adequate basis for invoking the statutory

emergency exception. Even assuming broader statutory authority to preserve the disputed

assets, the court abused its discretion by imposing a blanket account freeze without a

sufficient basis for its scope and continuing operation.

¶2     We address the following restated issues:

       1. Whether the District Court abused its discretion by removing Purtle as personal
          representative without the hearing required by § 72-3-526, MCA.

       2. Whether the Bateses have standing to seek Purtle’s removal for cause or interim
          protection before resolution of their will contest.

       3. Whether the District Court abused its discretion by appointing a special
          administrator without the hearing required by § 72-3-701(2), MCA, and whether
          the assigned investigation improperly shifted the burden of proof in the will
          contest.

       4. Whether the District Court exceeded its statutory authority or abused its
          discretion by freezing all financial accounts in which Bradford held an interest
          at death, including accounts Purtle claimed through nonprobate transfer.

We reverse and remand without deciding the validity of the will, the merits of the

financial-exploitation claim, or ownership of the disputed property.

                 FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

¶3     Bradford died on January 9, 2025, while domiciled in Lewis and Clark County.

Purtle and the Bateses survived her. Her husband, Alvin Ellis Bradford, had died on

October 5, 2023. On February 13, 2025, Purtle applied for informal probate of a will

Bradford signed on September 3, 2024, and for appointment as personal representative.

The clerk admitted the will to informal probate and issued letters appointing Purtle that

day.

¶4     Several provisions of the will underlie the dispute. The will states that Bradford is

married to Alvin and identifies Purtle under the heading “Current Children,” without

naming Robert or Kenneth. It nominates Purtle as sole executor and specifically devises

Bradford’s Helena residence to her. It leaves the residue to Alvin if he survives Bradford

by thirty days; otherwise, paragraph 13 directs division among Bradford’s children then

living, with provision for descendants of a deceased child. Paragraph 21 states that

omission of heirs “as named above” is intentional. Paragraph 26 separately expresses a

gift to Purtle of property and possessions and refers to a document titled “Bradford Family

Trust.” These provisions bear both on the challenge to Bradford’s testamentary capacity

and on the parties’ disagreement about whether the Bateses have an interest under the will.

¶5     On May 30, 2025, the Bateses filed a verified objection and counter-petition

challenging the will and Purtle’s appointment.      They asserted five counts: improper

disinheritance, lack of testamentary capacity, undue influence, financial exploitation of a

vulnerable adult, and removal of the personal representative. They relied in part on the

references to Bradford’s deceased husband and the omission of their names. They also

                                             3
alleged that Purtle had procured benefits through misconduct and could not impartially

investigate her own conduct. They requested invalidation of the will, a determination of

intestacy, forfeiture of Purtle’s benefits, appointment of an independent fiduciary, and

protection of assets.

¶6     The District Court granted the requested removal that same day, without a hearing.

It revoked Purtle’s letters; directed appointment of an independent third-party personal

representative; stayed sales, transfers, and liquidation of Bradford’s assets; and set a

scheduling conference. Beyond referring to its review of the counter-petition and “good

cause,” the court gave no explanation for removal. It did not determine that Bradford

lacked testamentary capacity, that anyone exercised undue influence or financially

exploited her, or that the will was invalid. Purtle filed her response on June 9, disputing

the allegations supporting the counter-petition and her removal.

¶7     The parties then conducted discovery under a stipulated scheduling order. Purtle

supplied an inventory identifying property she regarded as probate assets and other

property she claimed through nonprobate transfers, including an individual retirement

account and multiple-party bank accounts. Discovery also identified Dallas Davis, a

nonlawyer who had assisted Bradford in preparing the will. The Bateses questioned

whether Purtle had disclosed all assets and financial records and whether the claimed

nonprobate transfers were valid. Purtle disputed the significance of those concerns and

maintained that the nonprobate accounts belonged to her individually.

¶8     In their November 17, 2025 amended motion, the Bateses requested a new schedule,

immediate appointment of a special administrator, and a freeze of all financial accounts in

                                            4
which Bradford had held an interest. They sought a neutral fiduciary to obtain records,

investigate the allegations in their counter-petition, preserve assets, and pursue litigation

as the court might direct. Their exhibits included discovery responses, the inventory,

correspondence, and bank records for a statement period encompassing Bradford’s death.

Purtle opposed the motion. She maintained that the court had removed her without the

required hearing, that the Bateses had not established grounds for appointing a special

administrator, and that the court lacked authority in the probate proceeding to freeze

accounts she owned individually.

¶9     The Bateses replied that the unresolved questions about the assets and

Purtle’s conduct required a neutral fiduciary and immediate protection. They also invoked

§ 72-3-304, MCA, as authority to restrict an informally appointed personal representative

during a formal testacy proceeding. They characterized the earlier revocation of Purtle’s

letters as a temporary protective measure rather than removal for cause. Their attorney

submitted an affidavit addressing discovery and scheduling. The court did not hold a

hearing on the amended motion.

¶10    On December 3, 2025, the District Court granted the amended motion. Its findings

recounted the procedural history, the existence of accounts Purtle claimed as nonprobate

property, and the limited period covered by the records. The court then recited the Bateses’

contentions that allegations of incapacity, undue influence, financial exploitation, and

possible unauthorized practice of law required immediate action.          It concluded that

appointment of a special administrator was necessary to preserve the estate and secure its

proper administration, consistent with its earlier revocation of Purtle’s letters. It further

                                             5
concluded that immediate preservation measures would prevent dissipation and maintain

the status quo.

¶11    The order directed the Bateses to nominate two candidates for special administrator.

It prescribed duties that included collecting and preserving estate assets, reviewing

financial records, investigating matters in the counter-petition and motion, making

recommendations to the court, and commencing or defending actions at the court’s

direction. It also froze every financial account in which Bradford had held a legal or

equitable interest at death, whether she held the account individually, jointly, or otherwise.

The order prohibited withdrawals, transfers, closures, and beneficiary changes without

further court authorization or limited written authorization from the administrator. It

directed financial institutions to honor the freeze upon presentment. The court vacated the

scheduling order pending a preliminary report due within 120 days after appointment.

¶12    On January 15, 2026, the court appointed Seth H. Blades, CPA, and reaffirmed the

December 3 order. It held no intervening hearing and made no additional findings

supporting an emergency appointment.          Purtle served notice of entry of the three

challenged orders on January 16 and appealed on January 20. The Bateses moved to

dismiss the first two appellate issues as untimely, arguing that Purtle should have appealed

within thirty days after the May 30 order. We denied that motion on May 12, 2026, because

the Bateses had not shown service of notice of entry before January 16. That order resolved

the timeliness objection; we now consider the merits.

administrator for abuse of discretion. In re Estate of Williams, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9391730"><a href="/opinion/9396254/estate-of-g-williams/#17" aria-description="Citation for case: Estate of G. Williams">2023 MT 72, ¶¶ 17, 35</a></span><pre class="inline">,

</pre><span class="citation no-link">412 Mont. 58</span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation no-link">528 P.3d 1087</span><pre class="inline">. A court abuses its discretion when it acts arbitrarily, without

conscientious judgment, or exceeds the bounds of reason resulting in substantial injustice.

In re Estate of Thomas, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="11297538"><a href="/opinion/10830758/estate-of-sean-thomas/#14" aria-description="Citation for case: Estate of Sean Thomas">2026 MT 66, ¶ 14</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation no-link">427 Mont. 291</span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation no-link">586 P.3d 791</span><pre class="inline">. We review de

governs its decision. City of Missoula v. Girard, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9515854"><a href="/opinion/904348/city-of-missoula-v-girard/#10" aria-description="Citation for case: City of Missoula v.                                        DISCUSSION

¶14    1. Whether the District Court abused its discretion by removing Purtle as personal
          representative without the hearing required by § 72-3-526, MCA.

¶15    Purtle argues that the District Court violated § 72-3-526, MCA, by removing

her without first holding a hearing and determining whether cause existed.

Section 72-3-526(1), MCA, permits a person interested in an estate to petition for removal

for cause and directs the court, upon filing of the petition, to “fix a time and place for

hearing.” The petitioner must give notice to the personal representative and to any other


                                              7
persons the court directs. Section 72-3-526(1), MCA. Cause exists when removal would

serve the best interests of the estate or when the representative intentionally misrepresented

material facts leading to appointment, disregarded a court order, became incapable of

discharging the duties of the office, mismanaged the estate, or failed to perform an official

duty. Section 72-3-526(2), MCA. The petitioner bears the burden of establishing grounds

for removal, and the record must support those grounds. In re Estate of Robbin, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="879364"><a href="/opinion/879364/in-re-the-estate-of-robbin/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Estate of Robbin">230 Mont.

30</a></span><pre class="inline">, 33–34, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="879364"><a href="/opinion/879364/in-re-the-estate-of-robbin/#871" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Estate of Robbin">747 P.2d 869, 871</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1987).

¶16    The May 30 order revoked Purtle’s letters and directed her replacement. It did not

merely restrict particular acts pending a hearing on whether she should remain in office.

The court thus granted the requested removal on the day the Bateses filed their

counter-petition, before Purtle responded and without the hearing § 72-3-526(1), MCA,

requires. Its general reference to “good cause” neither identified facts establishing a

statutory ground for removal nor supplied the opportunity to contest those grounds

at a hearing. The power to remove a representative in the best interests of the estate

does not eliminate the notice-and-hearing requirements that govern that decision.

Section 72-3-526(1)–(2), MCA.

¶17    The Bateses defend the removal on the alternative theory that their counter-petition

and the will itself established Bradford’s incapacity, defeated the will, and eliminated

Purtle’s priority to serve. They argue that a person who described a deceased husband as

living and failed to identify two living sons could not have understood the objects of her

bounty.   This argument confuses evidence supporting a will contest with a judicial

determination resolving it. Informal probate remains conclusive until an order in a formal

                                              8
testacy proceeding supersedes it. Section 72-3-215(2), MCA. Section 72-3-310, MCA,

assigns contestants the burden of establishing lack of capacity, undue influence, and the

other grounds for invalidity specified there. The May 30 order neither determined those

issues nor superseded the informal probate of Bradford’s will.

¶18    The challenged provisions may bear on Bradford’s capacity and intentions, but the

Bateses’ interpretation of them did not relieve the court of its duty to hear the dispute before

deciding it. The formal testacy provisions themselves require the court to set a hearing and

the petitioner to give notice to the persons the statute identifies. Section 72-3-305(1)–(2),

MCA. Verification of the counter-petition did not convert disputed allegations and

inferences into judicial findings. The court instead set a scheduling conference, and the

parties later obtained deadlines for discovery and trial on the unresolved claims. We do

not decide what weight the will or other evidence should receive. We hold that the asserted

strength of the contest did not authorize the court to remove Purtle before conducting the

hearing required by § 72-3-526(1), MCA.

¶19    Recognizing that the May 30 order contains no findings resolving capacity, the

Bateses ask us to apply the doctrine of implied findings and affirm on the premise that the

District Court found the will invalid. Under that doctrine, a reviewing court may imply a

necessary finding when the record supports it, and it does not conflict with the court’s

express findings. When an express statutory requirement is at issue, specific evidence

referenced in other express findings must clearly support the implied finding. Sayler v.

Sun, </pre><span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/MT/2023/175/">2023 MT 175</a></span><pre class="inline">, ¶ 43, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9840992"><a href="/opinion/9427367/sayler-v-yan-sun/" aria-description="Citation for case: Sayler v. Yan Sun">413 Mont. 303</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9840992"><a href="/opinion/9427367/sayler-v-yan-sun/" aria-description="Citation for case: Sayler v. Yan Sun">536 P.3d 399</a></span><pre class="inline">. Here, the court identified no

evidence establishing incapacity and left the will contest for later proceedings. The Bateses

                                               9
seek more than recognition of an omitted finding supporting an adjudication the court

made; they ask us to supply the adjudication itself. The doctrine does not permit that result

or excuse the hearing required by § 72-3-526(1), MCA.

¶20    The Probate Code also answers the concern that the estate might need protection

while a removal petition awaits decision. After receiving notice of removal proceedings,

a personal representative may act only to account, correct maladministration, or

preserve the estate, except as the court otherwise orders under § 72-3-617, MCA.

Section 72-3-526(1), MCA. These restrictions preserve assets while the court determines

whether statutory grounds warrant removal. They do not terminate the appointment.

Unlike § 72-3-701(2), MCA, which expressly allows emergency appointment of a special

administrator without notice, § 72-3-526, MCA, contains no corresponding exception

allowing removal for cause without the required hearing. The court therefore could protect

the estate without deciding the removal request first and hearing it later.

¶21    The Bateses’ reliance in their District Court reply on § 72-3-304, MCA, does not

change that conclusion. They argued that the court had only suspended Purtle’s authority

during a formal testacy proceeding. Section 72-3-304, MCA, permits restrictions on the

powers of an acting representative and a request for a special administrator; it does not

transform an express revocation of letters into a temporary restriction. Whatever authority

the court had to restrain Purtle pending the contest, it exercised a different power when it

terminated her appointment. Because the court granted removal for cause without the

hearing and determination that § 72-3-526(1)–(2), MCA, require, it abused its discretion.

We therefore reverse the removal ruling.

                                             10
¶22    2. Whether the Bateses have standing to seek Purtle’s removal for cause or interim
          protection before resolution of their will contest.

¶23    Purtle also seeks a ruling that the Bateses lack standing to obtain her removal or

appointment of a special administrator unless they first prevail in their will contest. The

Bateses respond that they already defeated the will, that Purtle admitted their status as

devisees in her application, and that their improper-disinheritance count gives them an

independent claim against the estate. These arguments require us to distinguish the

interests necessary for removal for cause from those sufficient to request protection during

a will contest. The statutory definition of “interested person” expressly directs that inquiry:

its meaning varies with the particular purpose and matter involved in the proceeding.

Section 72-1-103(25), MCA.

¶24    In In re Estate of Lawlor, we held that standing to contest a will did not itself

establish standing to seek removal for cause under § 72-3-526, MCA. In re Estate of

Lawlor, ¶¶ 16–17, 20, 27. An heir who would gain from a successful contest qualified to

challenge the will, but her prospective inheritance did not establish a present property right

while the probated will remained effective. In re Estate of Lawlor, ¶¶ 16–17, 20, 27. For

removal for cause, the petitioner needed a property right in the estate, a claim against it, or

priority for appointment. In re Estate of Lawlor, ¶¶ 25–31. We expressly confined that

analysis to removal for cause and distinguished appointment proceedings associated with

a change in testacy. In re Estate of Lawlor, ¶ 21. The decision therefore does not make

success in a will contest a prerequisite to every form of relief the Probate Code authorizes

during that contest.


                                              11
¶25    The Bateses cannot establish a present intestate interest by treating the will as

already invalid; no order has resolved that issue. Section 72-3-215(2), MCA; In re Estate

of Lawlor, ¶ 27. We also reject the Bateses’ argument that Purtle admitted their status as

devisees in her application for informal probate. The application grouped the three children

under a heading identifying “heirs and devisees” and described the Bateses as sons. Section

72-3-202(2), MCA, requires an applicant to identify the spouse, children, heirs, and

devisees. Compliance with that disclosure requirement does not establish that every listed

person belongs to every category. A devisee is a person whom a will designates to receive

a devise. Section 72-1-103(12), MCA. The application establishes the family relationships

and identifies persons relevant to probate; it does not itself resolve who receives property

under the will.

¶26    The Bateses separately argue that Count I, which they labeled “improper

disinheritance,” supplies the claim against the estate that In re Estate of Lawlor requires

even if the will remains valid. They invoke the omitted-child statute, § 72-2-332, MCA,

and contend that their demand for a share differs from their claims seeking to invalidate

the will. That distinction does not make the demand a “claim” merely because they pleaded

it as a separate count. Section 72-1-103(6)(a), MCA, defines claims in terms of liabilities

of the decedent or estate, including contractual and tort liabilities, funeral expenses, and

administration expenses. A demand to inherit does not, without more, assert such a

liability. In re Estate of Lawlor, ¶ 26. A legally supported entitlement to a share may

instead bear on whether the claimant has a property right under § 72-1-103(25), MCA. The



                                            12
relevant inquiry concerns the legal basis of the asserted entitlement, not the title of the

count.

¶27      Purtle responds that § 72-2-332, MCA, cannot provide that basis because the

Bateses were adults when Bradford executed the will.           Her reading is incomplete.

Although § 72-2-332(1), MCA, addresses after-born and after-adopted children,

§ 72-2-332(3), MCA, also addresses a living child omitted solely because the testator

believed the child dead. The Bateses’ contrary assumption—that every unnamed child

receives a share unless the proponent proves an intentional omission—is also too broad.

Section 72-2-332(1)–(3), MCA, identifies circumstances in which an omitted child may

take; omission alone does not establish them. We do not decide whether the Bateses have

alleged or can prove an entitlement under § 72-2-332(3), MCA. Neither do we construe

the provisions concerning children, the residue, intentional omissions, and the separate gift

to Purtle to determine whether they receive a devise. Those unresolved questions prevent

the categorical declaration Purtle requests that the Bateses can acquire an interest only by

invalidating the will.

¶28      A separate statutory provision answers whether the Bateses may request interim

protection while they pursue the contest. Section 72-3-304, MCA, expressly permits a

petitioner who seeks a different personal representative in a formal proceeding to request

an order restraining the acting representative’s powers and appointment of a special

administrator. That authority operates during the proceeding. Requiring the petitioner to

prevail first would eliminate the protection the statute provides while the dispute remains

unresolved.     Section 72-3-319(1), MCA, likewise restricts administration, except

                                             13
preservation of the estate or as the court otherwise orders, after an informally appointed

representative receives notice of a formal proceeding concerning appointment priority or

qualification. These provisions distinguish preserving an estate during litigation from

deciding who ultimately should administer it.

¶29    That distinction applies here. The Bateses requested a determination of intestacy,

appointment of a different representative, and protection of assets pending resolution of

their challenge. They expressly invoked § 72-3-304, MCA, in their District Court reply.

Purtle does not dispute their standing to contest the will as heirs who would benefit from a

successful challenge. Their ability to pursue that formal proceeding permits them to

request the interim protection § 72-3-304, MCA, expressly provides. It does not establish

their entitlement to that protection or to removal for cause under § 72-3-526, MCA.

Applying each statute to the particular relief requested follows the distinction recognized

in In re Estate of Lawlor, ¶¶ 20–21, rather than extending will-contest standing to every

dispute concerning the estate.

¶30    We therefore reject both the assumption that filing the will contest established

standing for removal for cause and Purtle’s broader assertion that the Bateses could request

no interim protection until they prevailed. The District Court must evaluate any asserted

independent basis for removal standing under § 72-1-103(25), MCA, and In re Estate of

Lawlor, ¶¶ 25–31. It may not treat a combined disclosure heading or the label of a count

as conclusive. At the same time, § 72-3-304, MCA, permits the Bateses to request interim

relief during their formal proceeding. That provision does not dispense with the notice,



                                            14
hearing, and necessity requirements for appointment of a special administrator under

§ 72-3-701(2), MCA. We turn to those requirements.

¶31    3. Whether the District Court abused its discretion by appointing a special
          administrator without the hearing required by § 72-3-701(2), MCA, and whether
          the assigned investigation improperly shifted the burden of proof in the will
          contest.

¶32    Purtle argues that the special-administrator appointment violated § 72-3-701(2),

MCA, because the District Court held no hearing and did not establish an emergency

permitting it to dispense with one. The statute authorizes appointment on an interested

person’s petition after notice and a hearing, upon a finding that appointment is necessary

to preserve the estate or secure its proper administration, including when a general personal

representative cannot or should not act. Section 72-3-701(2), MCA. It then provides an

exception: if an emergency appears to the court, it may order appointment without notice.

Section 72-3-701(2), MCA. The Bateses maintain that the court invoked that exception by

finding “immediate preservation measures” necessary. They argue that the statute does not

require the court to use the word “emergency.”

¶33    We agree that § 72-3-701(2), MCA, does not require the court to use the words “an

emergency exists.” But the substance of the emergency determination matters. The first

sentence authorizes an appointment when necessary to preserve the estate or secure proper

administration and requires notice and a hearing before that decision. Section 72-3-701(2),

MCA. The second permits departure from that requirement when an emergency appears.

Section 72-3-701(2), MCA. A general need for a neutral administrator therefore does not,

by itself, establish the circumstances needed to appoint one before a hearing. The order


                                             15
and record must show why an apparent emergency requires appointment before the court

can hold the hearing the statute requires.       Merely describing the appointment as

“immediate” does not explain why the court must act before providing that opportunity.

Section 72-3-701(2), MCA.

¶34    The December 3 order did not make that distinction.          It recited the Bateses’

allegations concerning incapacity, undue influence, exploitation, and the preparation of the

will. It identified accounts Purtle claimed individually and the limited financial records

she had produced. But it did not identify threatened conduct or an administrative need that

required appointment before a hearing. A dispute over disclosure or ownership may justify

further inquiry and may support an appointment after a hearing. Reciting that dispute and

the requested relief, however, does not establish why an emergency permits the court to

dispense with the hearing required by § 72-3-701(2), MCA.

¶35    The Bateses contend that the bank records supply the missing urgency because

Purtle spent or withdrew more than $37,000 during the month Bradford died. Their

amended motion also describes a $10,000 in-person withdrawal as occurring the day after

Bradford’s death.    The exhibits do not establish the alleged timing of the $10,000

withdrawal. The statement period ran from December 25, 2024, through January 24, 2025,

and the aggregate debits include transfers between the identified accounts and a returned

Social Security payment. Both the transaction list and the withdrawal image date the

$10,000 withdrawal on January 7, 2025, two days before Bradford’s January 9 death. The

debit total therefore does not establish that Purtle took that amount from the estate after



                                            16
death, and the records do not explain why appointment without a hearing became necessary

in December 2025, almost eleven months after the alleged post-death transfer.

¶36    These limitations do not establish that Purtle acted properly. Conduct before

Bradford’s death may bear on the exploitation claim, and the court may need further

evidence concerning the purpose and ownership of the transactions. An emergency

appointment does not require the court to resolve that claim first. Section 72-3-701(2),

MCA. But the question here is not whether the exhibits warrant investigation; it is whether

they establish circumstances permitting the court to forgo a hearing before appointing the

investigator. The court made no finding connecting the months-old transactions to a

threatened loss or administrative harm that required appointment without a hearing. The

unresolved need to investigate cannot, standing alone, satisfy both the necessity

requirement and the distinct emergency exception in § 72-3-701(2), MCA. The Bateses

also emphasize that the estate had lacked an authorized general representative for more

than six months after the May 30, 2025 revocation. That vacancy may have supported

appointment to secure proper administration, but it did not automatically supply an

emergency under § 72-3-701(2), MCA. The court identified no impending obligation,

transaction, or other circumstance requiring it to appoint a fiduciary before a hearing. It

directed the Bateses to submit nominees and selected Blades on January 15, 2026, more

than six weeks after granting the amended motion, without an intervening hearing or

additional emergency findings. Delay does not categorically disprove an emergency.

Here, however, neither order nor the record explains why the court could omit the statutory

hearing while the appointment remained pending.

                                            17
¶37   Purtle opposed appointment and quoted the notice-and-hearing requirement in

§ 72-3-701(2), MCA. Her notice that briefing was complete did not authorize the court’s

earlier omission: she filed that notice on December 4, after the court granted the amended

motion. The January 15 order appointing Blades as fiduciary added only the identity of

the administrator and reaffirmed the December 3 directives. The District Court abused its

discretion by making the appointment without the hearing required by § 72-3-701(2),

MCA, when neither its findings nor the record adequately supported application of the

statutory emergency exception. We therefore reverse the appointment. On remand, the

court may consider a renewed request under that statute, but it must hold the required

hearing unless the circumstances support an emergency appointment.

¶38   Purtle separately argues that assigning an administrator to investigate the

counter-petition unlawfully shifts the Bateses’ burden of proving their will contest. The

Bateses answer that a special administrator may investigate and pursue estate claims.

Section 72-3-704, MCA, supports the latter proposition: it gives a court-appointed special

administrator the powers of a general personal representative, subject to the duties and

limits in the appointment order.     In In re Estate of Sauter, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="877145"><a href="/opinion/877145/matter-of-estate-of-sauter/#245" aria-description="Citation for case: Matter of Estate of Sauter">189 Mont. 244, 245</a></span><pre class="inline">,

250–51, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="877145"><a href="/opinion/877145/matter-of-estate-of-sauter/#876" aria-description="Citation for case: Matter of Estate of Sauter">615 P.2d 875, 876</a></span><pre class="inline">, 878–79 (1980), we directed appointment of a special

administrator to handle an estate claim when the general personal representative’s law

partner had represented the defendants. We allowed the general personal representative to

continue other administration. In re Estate of Sauter, 189 Mont. at 250–51, 615 P.2d at

878–79. Thus, a disputed estate claim may require independent administration before the



                                           18
court resolves its merits; the mere assignment of an investigation does not make the

appointment unlawful.

¶39    The necessary distinction concerns whose interests the administrator serves and who

must prove the pending claims. A will proponent must establish prima facie proof of due

execution; contestants must establish the grounds for invalidity assigned to them by

§ 72-3-310, MCA. Obtaining records or investigating a potential estate claim does not

itself change those burdens. A court-appointed administrator’s authority under § 72-3-704,

MCA, does not, however, replace the parties’ obligations under § 72-3-310, MCA, or

the court’s responsibility to decide the contest. Because the appointment fails under

§ 72-3-701(2), MCA, we need not decide the permissible scope of every task the

December 3 order assigned. Any renewed appointment must satisfy § 72-3-701(2), MCA.

In prescribing duties under § 72-3-704, MCA, the court must distinguish estate

administration from the proponents’ and contestants’ burdens in the will contest under

§ 72-3-310, MCA.

¶40    4. Whether the District Court exceeded its statutory authority or abused its
          discretion by freezing all financial accounts in which Bradford held an interest
          at death, including accounts Purtle claimed through nonprobate transfer.

¶41    Purtle challenges the account freeze on the ground that the accounts passed to her

outside probate and therefore lie beyond the court’s probate jurisdiction. The Bateses

respond that their financial-exploitation claim invokes § 72-2-813, MCA, which may

revoke benefits and sever survivorship interests in nonprobate property. They contend that

freezing the accounts was necessary to preserve property potentially subject to that relief.

Neither argument fully distinguishes authority to adjudicate a statutory claim affecting

                                            19
nonprobate benefits from authority to impose this particular restraint before adjudication.

The former does not automatically supply the latter.

¶42    Section 72-1-202(1)(a), MCA, grants the court jurisdiction over matters relating to

decedents’ estates, including construction of wills and determination of heirs and

successors.   Section 72-1-202(2), MCA, authorizes action necessary and proper to

administer justice in those matters. These provisions do not give a court sitting in probate

every remedy available in an ordinary civil action. In re Estate of Cooney, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2019 MT 293</span><pre class="inline">,

¶¶ 6–7, 11–16, </pre><span class="citation no-link">398 Mont. 166</span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation no-link">454 P.3d 1190</span><pre class="inline">. In In re Estate of Thomas, ¶¶ 30–34, we

applied that distinction to provisional relief: § 72-3-617, MCA, specifically authorizes

restraints on a personal representative’s conduct, whereas the preliminary-injunction

provisions of Title 27, chapter 19, MCA, govern civil actions. The restraint must fall

within a power the Probate Code expressly grants or a power necessary to carry out an

authorized probate function. Section 72-1-202(2), MCA; In re Estate of Cooney, ¶ 7.

Principles of law and equity supplement the Code unless a particular provision displaces

them. Section 72-1-104, MCA. The absence of an expressly named remedy therefore does

not end the inquiry; the court still must determine whether the requested restraint is

necessary and proper to exercise an authorized probate power.

¶43    Purtle correctly identifies a distinction between estate property and property that

passes through a nonprobate transfer. A provision for transfer on death in an account

agreement or individual retirement plan is nontestamentary. Section 72-6-111(1), MCA.

Subject to the statutory exceptions, sums in a multiple-party account belong to the

surviving party or parties; account terms that exclude survivorship produce a different

                                            20
result. Section 72-6-212(1), (3), MCA. A survivorship transfer takes effect through the

account terms and governing statutes rather than through the will. Section 72-6-214, MCA.

We do not decide whether every account Purtle identified qualifies as nonprobate property.

These provisions nevertheless foreclose treating Bradford’s interest at death, without

further analysis, as sufficient to subject every account to estate administration.

¶44    Purtle goes too far, however, when she argues that a nonprobate designation

defeats any probate inquiry.         Section 72-2-813(3)(a), MCA, revokes specified

revocable dispositions, powers, and fiduciary nominations benefiting an abuser.

Section 72-2-813(3)(b), MCA, severs the interests of the decedent and abuser in property

they held as joint tenants with survivorship rights at the time of the exploitation and

converts those interests to tenancies in common. In the absence of a qualifying conviction,

the court must determine, on an interested person’s petition and under the preponderance

standard, whether the individual would be criminally accountable for financially exploiting

the decedent. Section 72-2-813(7), MCA. The statute therefore supplies authority to

determine the effect of exploitation on covered nonprobate benefits. We do not resolve

whether the Bateses can establish the facts necessary for that relief.

¶45    The Bateses argue that the freeze was incidental to that authorized determination,

not independent equitable relief of the kind disallowed in In re Estate of Cooney. A probate

court may decide an equitable question when necessary to exercise a power the statutes

expressly grant. In re Day’s Estate, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3544104"><a href="/opinion/3564914/in-re-days-estate/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Day&#x27;s Estate">119 Mont. 547</a></span><pre class="inline">, 551–52, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3544104"><a href="/opinion/3564914/in-re-days-estate/#865" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Day&#x27;s Estate">177 P.2d 862, 865</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1947).

Even assuming § 72-1-202(2), MCA, permits a restraint necessary to preserve relief

available on the pending exploitation claim, that authority does not sustain this order. The

                                             21
court used Bradford’s former interest in an account—not the nature and extent of the

interests potentially affected by the statutory claim—to define the property restrained. It

prohibited every withdrawal, transfer, closure, and beneficiary change absent court or

administrator authorization, without distinguishing benefits subject to revocation,

survivorship interests subject to severance, or any separate ownership interests that would

remain unaffected by that relief. Section 72-2-813(3), MCA. It then left the freeze in effect

until further order while suspending the case schedule pending a report due 120 days after

the administrator’s appointment.     The court did not need to resolve exploitation or

ownership before considering protection.          But its recitals of disputed ownership,

incomplete disclosure, and possible dissipation did not explain why this categorical control

over all covered accounts was necessary for that scope and duration. On this record, the

court did not establish that the blanket restraint was necessary and proper to carry out its

authority under § 72-2-813, MCA. Section 72-1-202(2), MCA.

¶46    Purtle’s reply nevertheless overstates the absence of statutory protection when it

asserts that § 72-2-813, MCA, operates only after a final exploitation determination.

Section 72-2-813(8)(a)–(b), MCA, expressly addresses disputed property before

adjudication. A claimant may give a payor or third party the prescribed written notice

identifying the decedent, the claimant, the property, and the asserted forfeiture or

revocation. Section 72-2-813(8)(a)–(b), MCA. The statute limits potential liability under

this provision to actions taken two or more business days after actual receipt of written

notice served as § 72-2-813(8)(b), MCA, requires. Section 72-2-813(8)(a), MCA. The

payor or third party may deposit the disputed property with the probate court, which must

                                             22
hold it pending a determination. Section 72-2-813(8)(b), MCA. This procedure provides

a means to preserve disputed property held by a payor or third party without treating the

asserted forfeiture as established. The December 3 order did not direct the court to hold

property deposited under that procedure; it commanded financial institutions to freeze

covered accounts and authorized the administrator to permit transactions. Subsection (8)

does not itself authorize that directive. Section 72-2-813(8)(a)–(b), MCA. We do not,

however, treat its notice-and-deposit procedure as the exclusive source of protective

authority.   The Bateses’ broader argument depends on the court’s authority under

§ 72-1-202(2), MCA, which does not sustain this blanket restraint for the reasons just

explained.

¶47    The decisions the Bateses invoke do not authorize a broader result. In In re Estate

of Thomas, the court restrained the representative’s use and distribution of estate funds

after a hearing; the requested relief concerned the exercise of her fiduciary office. In re

Estate of Thomas, ¶¶ 9–11, 28, 30–34. Section 72-3-617(1), MCA, also permits other

orders securing proper performance of a personal representative’s duties and permits

persons with whom the representative may transact business to become parties. Those

provisions remain tied to estate administration; they do not confer a general power to

restrain a person’s use of property merely because that person serves as personal

representative. In re Estate of Thomas, ¶ 34. Here, the court did not confine the challenged

restraint to Purtle’s fiduciary conduct. It froze disputed accounts and directed financial

institutions to honor the prohibition on transactions. It also failed to set the hearing within

ten days after entering the restraint, as § 72-3-617(2), MCA, requires unless the parties

                                              23
otherwise agree. Instead, it deferred further scheduling until after the administrator’s

report. Section 72-3-617, MCA, therefore, does not sustain the order as entered. In re

Estate of Ronan, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2023 MT 197</span><pre class="inline">, ¶¶ 21–22, 28–29, </pre><span class="citation no-link">413 Mont. 468</span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation no-link">537 P.3d 868</span><pre class="inline">, likewise

concerned distribution of insurance proceeds in accordance with a testamentary disposition

and settlement agreement, not a provisional freeze of disputed personal accounts. Neither

decision allows a court to replace the applicable statutory requirements with a general

assertion that preserving the status quo would help resolve the estate.

¶48    We reverse the blanket freeze. The order does not satisfy § 72-3-617, MCA; and

§ 72-2-813(8), MCA, does not itself authorize the directive. Even assuming § 72-1-202(2),

MCA, permits a restraint necessary to preserve relief on the exploitation claim, the court

abused its discretion by imposing this categorical freeze without a sufficient basis for its

scope and continuing operation. In re Estate of Thomas, ¶ 14; § 72-1-202(2), MCA. We

do not decide whether § 72-1-202(2), MCA, would support a narrower protective order on

a different record. This holding does not validate Purtle’s claimed transfers, prevent

discovery relevant to the exploitation claim, or immunize disputed property from a remedy

the governing law authorizes. It requires the District Court to distinguish control of estate

administration from control of property Purtle claims individually and to identify the

statutory basis and required showing for any provisional relief. The parties remain free to

pursue the protection expressly available under § 72-2-813(8), MCA, and other relief for

which they establish the applicable statutory prerequisites.




                                             24
                                     CONCLUSION

¶49    The District Court violated § 72-3-526(1)–(2), MCA, by granting removal for cause

without the required hearing and determination. Its appointment of a special administrator

also failed to satisfy § 72-3-701(2), MCA, because it held no hearing and did not adequately

establish the emergency exception. The District Court also abused its discretion by

imposing the blanket account freeze without adequately justifying its scope and continuing

operation, even assuming that § 72-1-202(2), MCA, authorizes some provisional

protection. We therefore reverse the removal and replacement directives in the May 30,

2025 order, the special-administrator and account-freeze rulings in the December 3, 2025

order, and the January 15, 2026 appointment order.

¶50    On remand, the District Court shall restore Purtle’s appointment, subject to the

restrictions that apply during the pending formal proceeding. Reinstatement does not

authorize distributions that § 72-3-304, MCA, prohibits or other administration that

§ 72-3-319(1), MCA, restricts. The court may consider requests for interim protection

under the applicable statutes, including §§ 72-3-304, -617, and -701(2), MCA, but must

apply the requirements governing the particular relief. If necessary, it shall direct an

orderly accounting and transfer of estate records and property held by the special

administrator. It also shall establish an appropriate schedule for resolving the pending

claims rather than reinstate expired deadlines. The District Court shall lift the account

freeze imposed by the December 3 order. To the extent the May 30 stay would continue

that same blanket restraint, the court shall modify the stay consistently with this Opinion.



                                             25
¶51   The Bateses’ standing to contest the will does not alone establish standing to obtain

removal for cause, but they need not prevail in that contest before requesting the interim

protection § 72-3-304, MCA, permits. We leave any unresolved independent basis for

removal standing to the District Court under § 72-1-103(25), MCA, and In re Estate of

Lawlor, ¶¶ 25–31. We express no opinion on the validity or construction of the will,

whether financial exploitation occurred, ownership of the accounts, or ultimate entitlement

to estate property. We also do not foreclose appointment of a neutral fiduciary upon

satisfaction of § 72-3-701(2), MCA.

¶52   Reversed and remanded.
