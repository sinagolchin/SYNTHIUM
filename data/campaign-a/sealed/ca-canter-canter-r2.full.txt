HARRIS, Judge:

¶1     After a bench trial in this divorce case, the trial court—as
relevant here—ordered Chad Canter to pay $2,448 in monthly
child support and $8,623 in monthly alimony to his ex-wife, Jane
Canter, and it also ordered each party to pay their own attorney
fees. Both parties appeal, each challenging different parts of the
court’s orders. Chad takes issue with the court’s child support and
alimony awards, challenging the court’s decision to allow Jane’s
financial expert to testify and, in addition, asserting that the court
incorrectly assessed Jane’s expenses as well as the parties’
                           Canter v. Canter


respective incomes. 1 For her part, Jane challenges some of the
court’s orders relating to attorney fees. She acknowledges that, all
else being equal, it was not an abuse of discretion under these
circumstances for the court to have ordered each party to bear
their own fees, but she contends that the court—through some of
its other decisions regarding property division—effectively (and
in contravention of its own order) required her to pay some of
Chad’s fees without making the findings necessary to support
such an award.

¶2    We reject Chad’s arguments and affirm the court’s orders
regarding child support and alimony. But we find merit in Jane’s
arguments regarding attorney fees, and we therefore remand this
matter with instructions for the trial court to align its attorney fees
order with its property division orders.

separation, Chad—in a self-described effort to divide “the parties’
total marital checking and savings account balances”—
transferred nearly $48,000 to Jane from one of the parties’ marital
accounts.

¶15 Jane’s Accountant then testified, and he rebutted certain
aspects of Chad’s Accountant’s testimony. With regard to the
parties’ reasonable expenses, Jane’s Accountant explained that
one cannot properly compute post-separation expenses for each

of the typical expenses a household incurs during a marriage need

found that Chad’s income, for child support and alimony

amount by averaging Chad’s income over a three-year period and
by including the entirety of the 2021 bonus payment made to
2LMD as part of Chad’s income for that year. Based on that
income figure (and on the income figure for Jane to which the
parties had stipulated), the court calculated Chad’s child support
obligation as $2,448 per month, to be paid until the parties’ last
remaining minor child reached majority.

¶19 With regard to alimony, the court ordered Chad to pay

            ISSUES AND STANDARDS OF REVIEW

¶30 Both parties now appeal, challenging various aspects of the
court’s rulings. In his appeal, Chad asks us to consider two issues.
First, he takes issue with the court’s denial of his motion in limine,
in which he sought to exclude Jane’s Accountant from testifying
at trial. “We review for correctness any legal questions underlying
the admissibility of evidence, but we review for abuse of
discretion any decisions to admit or exclude evidence and
determinations regarding the admissibility of expert testimony.”
Metropolitan Water Dist. of Salt Lake &amp; Sandy v. Sorf, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9911909"><a href="/opinion/9454084/metropolitan-water-v-sorf/#28" aria-description="Citation for case: Metropolitan Water v. Sorf">2023 UT App

alimony award, with regard to the computation of both Jane’s
expenses as well as the parties’ income. As a general matter, “we
review all aspects of [a] trial court’s alimony determination for an
abuse of discretion.” Miner v. Miner, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5136789"><a href="/opinion/5308238/miner-v-miner/#11" aria-description="Citation for case: Miner v.                             ANALYSIS

                       I. Jane’s Accountant

¶33 First, Chad challenges the trial court’s order denying his
motion in limine and allowing Jane’s Accountant to testify at trial.
In particular, he claims that Jane’s Accountant “should have been
mandatorily excluded” because he had not been properly
disclosed under rule 26 of the Utah Rules of Civil Procedure and

basis—and not on a total-expenses-divided-in-half basis—and
assign a value to each line item that is commensurate with the
parties’ habits during the marriage. See Smith, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9949537"><a href="/opinion/9482924/smith-v-smith/#14" aria-description="Citation for case: Smith v. Smith">2024 UT App 28,
¶ 14</a></span><pre class="inline"> (stating that “the proper way to take the marital standard of
living into account is at step one by assessing a party’s claimed
line-item expenses in light of that standard” (cleaned up)).

¶51 But here, Chad does not challenge any of the specific line-
item amounts the trial court assigned to Jane as her reasonable
expenses. Instead—and in addition to his unpersuasive legal
arguments about the trial court’s overall approach—he asserts
generally that the court erred by basing some of those line-item
amounts on Jane’s “own unsubstantiated figures” with “no
supporting documentation.” But there is nothing wrong with the
trial court’s reliance—in part—on Jane’s testimony and financial
declaration. Indeed, we have stated that “the most common way
for a party to satisfy” the burden of proving a party’s need in light
of the marital standard of living “is for the party to provide the
court with a credible financial declaration and supporting
financial documentation to support an award of alimony.”
Krajeski v. Krajeski, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="10800273"><a href="/opinion/10333685/krajeski-v-krajeski/#46" aria-description="Citation for case: Krajeski v. Krajeski">2025 UT App 19, ¶ 46</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="10800273"><a href="/opinion/10333685/krajeski-v-krajeski/" aria-description="Citation for case: Krajeski v. Krajeski">565 P.3d 544</a></span><pre class="inline"> (cleaned
up), cert. denied, </pre><span class="citation no-link">574 P.3d 519</span><pre class="inline"> (Utah 2025). And there was other
evidence at the trial court’s disposal, including rebuttal testimony

                         Canter v. Canter


from Jane’s Accountant, that supported the trial court’s ultimate
conclusions regarding Jane’s monthly expenses. Thus, Chad has
not carried his burden of persuasion on appeal of convincing us
that any of those line items were incorrectly computed or
insufficiently supported by the evidence presented at trial.

¶52 Thus, for all of these reasons, we reject Chad’s challenges
to the trial court’s findings and conclusions regarding Jane’s
reasonable monthly expenses.

¶54 In the second step of an alimony analysis, “the court must
determine the extent to which the receiving spouse is able to meet
his or her own needs with his or her own income, and if the
receiving spouse is able to meet all his or her needs with his or her
own income, then the court should not award alimony.” Fox v.
Fox, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7797627"><a href="/opinion/7853761/fox-v-fox/#20" aria-description="Citation for case: Fox v. Fox">2022 UT App 88, ¶ 20</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7797627"><a href="/opinion/7853761/fox-v-fox/" aria-description="Citation for case: Fox v. Fox">515 P.3d 481</a></span><pre class="inline"> (cleaned up). But if the
recipient spouse cannot meet his or her own needs, then in the
third step the court must “assess whether the payor spouse’s
income, after meeting his or her needs, is sufficient to make up
some or all of the shortfall between the receiving spouse’s needs
and income.” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7797627"><a href="/opinion/7853761/fox-v-fox/" aria-description="Citation for case: Fox v. Fox">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> (cleaned up). These steps necessarily require the
court to make findings regarding the parties’ respective “earning
capacity or ability to produce income.” See Utah Code § 30-3-
5(10)(a)(ii) (2023).

far as to subpoena the company to obtain its operating
agreement and learn about the finder’s fee. The court was also
aware that the parties had stipulated to an income figure for Jane.

Chad was the one who earned the 2LMD bonus payment, and

gross annual income, for child support and alimony purposes,
was $526,531.

                         III. Attorney Fees

¶58 In her cross-appeal, Jane challenges some of the trial court’s
rulings related to attorney fees. The court ordered that—as a
general matter—both parties were to bear their own fees, and Jane
does not challenge that order. Instead, she contends that the
court—through some of its other decisions regarding property
division—effectively (and in contravention of its own fee order)
required her to pay some of Chad’s fees without making the
findings necessary to support such an award.

¶59 As we understand them, Jane’s arguments are centered on
the Zions Account, which contained marital funds that Chad
controlled in the months leading up to trial. She points out that
the evidence she unearthed post-trial demonstrated that Chad
paid his lawyers $85,000 out of that account before it was divided.
She thus argues that at least this portion—$85,000—of Chad’s
attorney fees were paid out of pre-division marital funds, while
she was forced to pay all of her attorney fees out of her share of
post-division monies. And she asserts that the trial court therefore
erred by not adopting the commissioner’s recommendation that
Chad be required to pay her $42,500, half of the amount he took
out of the parties’ marital account to pay his attorney fees. We find
merit in Jane’s position.

¶60 “In divorce cases, both the decision to award attorney fees
and the amount of such fees are within the trial court’s sound
discretion.” Miner v. Miner, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5136789"><a href="/opinion/5308238/miner-v-miner/#108" aria-description="Citation for case: Miner v. Miner">496 P.3d 242</a></span><pre class="inline">
(cleaned up). But any such award “must be based on [i] evidence
of the financial need of the receiving spouse, [ii] the ability of the
other spouse to pay, and [iii] the reasonableness of the requested
fees. And, failure to consider these factors is grounds for reversal
on the fee issue.” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5136789"><a href="/opinion/5308238/miner-v-miner/" aria-description="Citation for case: Miner v. Miner">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> (alterations in original) (cleaned up). Here,
Jane argues that the court—even while saying that both parties
were to bear their own fees—actually made a de facto attorney
fees award that effectively required her to pay $42,500 of Chad’s

fees, and she asserts that it did so without making any findings

¶61 A similar situation arose in Miner. There, the court after
trial “ordered each party to pay his or her own attorney and
expert fees,” but it “made no adjustment to account for [a] portion
of [the wife’s] attorney fees that [the husband] had already paid.”
Id. ¶ 105. The husband filed a post-trial motion asking the trial
court to make an appropriate adjustment, but the court refused.
See id. ¶ 107. The husband appealed that decision, and we agreed
with the husband, stating as follows:

       In this case, it was within the court’s discretion to
       make attorney fees awards to one party or another.
       But in order to do so, the court must first make
       adequate findings. Here, the court professed not to
       be making any award of attorney fees, and to be
       requiring each party to bear his or her own, but [the
       husband] has persuasively argued that he paid a
       significant part of [the wife’s] fees without being
       credited for that payment. If the court wishes to
       award [the wife] those fees, and require [the
       husband] to pay them, it must engage with the
       three-part test, and make the required findings. It
       cannot make such an award sub silentio, while
       asserting that its order asks both parties to bear their
       own fees.

Id. ¶ 109 (cleaned up). We remanded the case to the trial court and
instructed the court to select between “two options.” Id. ¶ 110. It
could “continue to insist that both parties bear their own fees, in
which case it need[ed] to make an adjustment to account for any
portion of [the wife’s] fees that [the husband had] paid, or at least
explain why no such adjustment [was] necessary.” Id. Or,
“[a]lternatively, it [could] explicitly make a partial award of

attorney fees to [the wife], in which case it need[ed] to make
appropriate findings” as required by the three-part test. </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline">

¶62 Similarly here, Jane has demonstrated that—due to Chad’s
use of the undivided marital Zions Account prior to trial—she has
effectively paid some of Chad’s attorney fees. Yet the court
ordered after trial, and continued to insist following post-trial
motions, that each party should bear their own attorney fees, even

                         CONCLUSION

¶63 The trial court neither erred nor exceeded its discretion in
allowing Jane’s Accountant to testify at trial. The court also
applied the correct legal framework in evaluating Jane’s alimony
claim, and Chad has not borne his burden of demonstrating that
any of the court’s factual determinations related to alimony or
child support are erroneous. We therefore reject all of Chad’s
appellate arguments and affirm the court’s orders regarding child
support and alimony.

¶64 Regarding Jane’s cross-appeal, however, we find merit in
her assertion that she was effectively compelled to pay a portion
of Chad’s attorney fees; this not only conflicted with the court’s
“bear-your-own-fees” order, but it was also unsupported by
findings necessary to sustain such an award. We therefore
remand this case to the trial court for the limited purpose of
selecting between the two options outlined above.
