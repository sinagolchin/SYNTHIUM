Justice Katherine M. Bidegaray delivered the Opinion of the Court.

¶1     Kathryn Christofferson appeals from the April 15, 2026 Amended Findings of Fact,

Conclusions of Law and Order resolving her motion to recalculate child support, entered

by the Montana Fourth Judicial District, Missoula County, after our remand for additional

findings. We restate the dispositive issues as follows:

       1. Whether the District Court erred by adopting a child-support calculation
          inconsistent with its findings and order concerning health insurance.

       2. Whether the District Court abused its discretion by declining to award earlier
          retroactive child support.

We affirm in part, reverse in part, and remand.

                 FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

¶2     Kathryn (Katie) and Colin Christofferson (Colin) married in 2010 and have two

minor children, born in 2011 and 2014. The parties separated in January 2021, and Katie

filed a petition for dissolution of marriage on June 27, 2022. In April 2023, the District

Court entered a decree dissolving the marriage.

¶3     Before entry of the decree, the parties executed a stipulated final parenting plan and

a marital and property settlement agreement. The parenting plan provided for calculation

of child support under the Montana Child Support Guidelines through the Child Support

Services Division (CSSD). It further provided that Colin would maintain health insurance

for the children, so long as it was available to him at a reasonable cost, and that beginning

with tax year 2023 the parties would divide the federal tax dependency exemptions with

Colin claiming one child and Katie claiming the other, with future rotation as specified by

the agreement.

                                             2
¶4     On October 10, 2023, CSSD issued a temporary administrative support order

requiring Colin to pay $96.50 per child per month, for a total transfer payment of $193 per

month. The accompanying worksheet assumed 210 parenting days for Katie and 155

parenting days for Colin, included imputed income for Katie, used income Colin reported

to CSSD, and credited Colin with paying the children’s health-insurance costs.

¶5     Katie requested an administrative review hearing of the CSSD calculation, but the

Office of Administrative Hearings denied her request as untimely.

¶6     On January 17, 2024, Katie filed a motion in the dissolution action requesting

recalculation of child support. She asserted that the CSSD calculation contained material

errors—including parenting days, income, medical deductions, and health-insurance

amounts—and requested back support from the date of the dissolution filing. Colin

opposed the motion and argued, in part, that the parties had agreed to proceed through

CSSD for the calculation and enforcement of child support. His February 2024 response

opposed the request for back support, and Katie renewed it in her April 2024 reply.

¶7     The parties later agreed on several inputs for a new calculation, including annual

net self-employment income of $30,000 for Katie, an average of Colin’s recent earnings,

and 134 parenting days for Colin. Applying the parties’ agreed three-year averaging

method, CSSD used annual earnings of $83,316 for Colin and calculated a total monthly

transfer payment of $853 for both children. At a January 27, 2025 conference, the District

Court requested revisions to Colin’s tax-filing status and the treatment of the earned income

tax credit. CSSD filed a revised worksheet the next day calculating a $770 monthly



                                             3
payment. That worksheet included $6,534 in annual health-insurance premiums for the

children and credited Colin for paying them.

¶8     The District Court held an evidentiary hearing on February 10, 2025. At the hearing,

the parties presented testimony and exhibits regarding their respective incomes, the

children’s health insurance, parenting time, and the prior CSSD calculations. Both parties

and CSSD attorney Patrick Quinn testified.

¶9     Katie testified that her income for 2023 was approximately $26,939 and estimated

her 2024 income would be approximately $29,000. Katie also testified regarding the

financial difficulties she experienced while the temporary CSSD support order remained

in effect. She testified that her expenses exceeded her income and that she used savings

and assistance from her parents to cover household expenses. Her parents offered their

home to Katie and the children, and her parents moved to another home. Katie paid rent

and utilities and hoped to purchase the home. The court admitted her monthly budget and

proposed calculation of back support. She acknowledged that the children’s basic needs

were met but testified that she could not provide the additional benefits they enjoyed with

Colin. She requested retroactive support reaching back to the January 2021 separation.

¶10    Colin testified that he had provided the information CSSD requested and had paid

the support required by its temporary order. He also testified that he had maintained health

insurance for the children since separation and provided for their needs during his parenting

time. He confirmed a monthly insurance premium of $592 and described fluctuating

earnings and a monthly mortgage payment of $3,861 after refinancing the marital home as

part of the dissolution. He opposed retroactive support and testified that the proposed

                                             4
increase, together with insurance premiums and his housing expense, would create

substantial financial difficulty.

¶11    The parties and the court discussed placing the children on Healthy Montana Kids

and removing the private insurance expense from the calculation. As allowed by the court,

the parties submitted proposed findings and conclusions. With his post-hearing proposal,

Colin included Exhibit D, a child-support worksheet calculation in which he assumed

Katie’s income as $30,000 per year, his income as $83,316 per year, the children being

with Katie 210 days per year and with him 155 days per year, and no child health-insurance

premium. Exhibit D also allocated both children to Katie for tax benefits. Based on that

calculation, Colin proposed he pay child support of $342 per child per month, or $684 per

month for the two children.

¶12    In its March 10, 2025 order, the District Court recited the testimony about income

and support but did not determine the essential inputs for its calculation. The court found

that no testimony indicated the children had suffered harm while Colin paid the lower

amount and that testimony indicated he supplied what they needed to maintain their

accustomed lifestyle. The court nevertheless ordered Colin to pay $684 per month in child

support for the two children, relying on Exhibit D attached to Colin’s post-trial proposed

findings, and denied back support. Exhibit D had not been admitted into evidence at the

hearing.

¶13    The worksheet on which the court relied, Exhibit D, reflected assumptions that

differed from the earlier CSSD calculations, including parenting days, tax treatment, and

health-insurance costs.     The District Court did not make findings resolving these

                                            5
discrepancies or explaining why it adopted the inputs from Colin’s post-trial submission

of Exhibit D instead of the figures reflected in the evidence presented at the hearing or in

the CSSD modified calculation.

¶14    In addition to ordering the $684 monthly payment, the District Court modified

provisions of the parties’ stipulated parenting plan. The parenting plan allocated the

children’s federal tax dependency exemptions between the parties, but the court ordered

that Katie claim both children beginning in tax year 2024 (or 2025 if necessary). The court

also altered the parenting plan’s health-insurance provision. The plan required Colin to

maintain health insurance for the children if available through his employment at a

reasonable cost, but the court instead ordered Katie to enroll the children in Healthy

Montana Kids and notify the court if the children later became ineligible. The order

provided that, to the extent these directives conflicted with the parenting plan, the order

superseded the plan.

¶15    Katie appealed. We remanded for findings and conclusions explaining the support

calculation, tax allocation, insurance arrangements, and denial of retroactive support. We

allowed the District Court to conduct further proceedings or obtain additional filings as

necessary and provided for supplemental briefing after the court entered its order.

¶16    On April 15, 2026, the District Court entered its amended findings, conclusions, and

order. It determined that 134 days most accurately represented Colin’s parenting time,

accepted annual income of $30,000 for Katie and approximately $83,000 for Colin, and

restored the allocation of one child to each parent for the child tax credit. It allocated the

earned income tax credit for both children to Katie and retained the requirement that she

                                              6
enroll the children in Healthy Montana Kids. The court also resolved the disputes over

additional family income and childcare expenses.

¶17    The court considered the $684 calculation it previously had adopted, a new $680

calculation it obtained, and the $770 calculation CSSD filed before the hearing. It selected

the $770 calculation as the most accurate because it reflected 134 parenting days for Colin

and the tax allocation the court determined would benefit the children. The amended order

described that calculation as consistent with Healthy Montana Kids coverage, although the

worksheet retained the $6,534 annual premium and corresponding credit to Colin. The

operative provision made the $770 obligation effective April 1, 2025, with credit for

support already paid.

¶18    The court again declined to award earlier support. It found that both parents had

met the children’s needs since separation and had received family assistance that benefited

the children. It acknowledged Katie’s financial difficulties but found her assertions about

Colin’s greater lifestyle speculative.    It emphasized Colin’s payment of insurance

premiums and, after the temporary order, cash support. The court also stated that Katie

waited nearly a year after the temporary order to request back support. That statement

overlooked her January 2024 motion and the ensuing briefing. Katie challenges the

amended calculation and seeks retroactive application from the date of that motion. Colin

defends the amended order and requests appellate attorney fees and costs as sanctions.

                               STANDARD OF REVIEW

¶19    We review child-support determinations, including decisions concerning retroactive

support, for abuse of discretion. In re Marriage of Miller, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2022 MT 110</span><pre class="inline">, ¶ 14, </pre><span class="citation no-link">409 Mont.

 </span><pre class="inline">                                            7
29, </pre><span class="citation no-link">511 P.3d 307</span><pre class="inline">. A district court abuses its discretion if it acts arbitrarily without

conscientious judgment or exceeds the bounds of reason resulting in substantial injustice.

In re Marriage of Miller, ¶ 14. The failure to set forth the essential and determining facts

underlying a child-support calculation is an abuse of discretion. In re Marriage of Mills,

</pre><span class="citation" data-id="2631679"><a href="/opinion/2631679/in-re-the-marriage-of-mills/#20" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Marriage of Mills">2006 MT 149, ¶ 20</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2631679"><a href="/opinion/2631679/in-re-the-marriage-of-mills/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Marriage of Mills">332 Mont. 415</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2631679"><a href="/opinion/2631679/in-re-the-marriage-of-mills/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Marriage of Mills">138 P.3d 815</a></span><pre class="inline">. We review underlying factual findings

for clear error and conclusions of law for correctness. A finding is clearly erroneous if

substantial evidence does not support it, the court misapprehends the effect of the evidence,

or review of the record leaves us convinced the court made a mistake. An exercise of

discretion resting on a clearly erroneous material finding or an erroneous application of

law also constitutes an abuse of discretion. In re Marriage of Jackson, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="11119031"><a href="/opinion/10652444/marriage-of-jackson/#8" aria-description="Citation for case: Marriage of Jackson">2025 MT 177,

¶¶ 8-9</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation no-link">423 Mont. 329</span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation no-link">573 P.3d 812</span><pre class="inline">. We do not reweigh conflicting evidence or substitute

our assessment of credibility for that of the trial court. In re Marriage of Frick, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="889887"><a href="/opinion/889887/in-re-marriage-of-frick-and-perina/#23" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Marriage of Frick and Perina">2011 MT

41, ¶ 23</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="889887"><a href="/opinion/889887/in-re-marriage-of-frick-and-perina/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Marriage of Frick and Perina">359 Mont. 296</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="889887"><a href="/opinion/889887/in-re-marriage-of-frick-and-perina/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Marriage of Frick and Perina">249 P.3d 67</a></span><pre class="inline">.

                                      DISCUSSION

¶20    1. Whether the District Court erred by adopting a child-support calculation
          inconsistent with its findings and order concerning health insurance.

¶21    The problem with the order on appeal is not that the District Court lacked authority

to address child support after CSSD acted. A district court in a dissolution proceeding

remains obligated to enter a support order consistent with § 40-4-204, MCA. A temporary

administrative order under § 40-5-225(3), MCA, remains subject to supersession by the

final district court order. Section 40-5-225(4)(b), MCA. The parenting plan contemplated

that CSSD would calculate and collect support; it did not displace that statutory framework.


                                             8
The problem is that the calculation the District Court adopted does not implement the

findings it made on remand.

¶22    Under M. R. Civ. P. 52(a), a district court must make findings that disclose the

essential and determining facts underlying its decision so that this Court can conduct

meaningful appellate review. See In re Marriage of Banka, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="889158"><a href="/opinion/889158/in-re-the-marriage-of-banka/#9" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Marriage of Banka">2009 MT 33, ¶¶ 9-10</a></span><pre class="inline">,

</pre><span class="citation" data-id="889158"><a href="/opinion/889158/in-re-the-marriage-of-banka/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Marriage of Banka">349 Mont. 193</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="889158"><a href="/opinion/889158/in-re-the-marriage-of-banka/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Marriage of Banka">201 P.3d 830</a></span><pre class="inline">. The rule serves a practical purpose: without adequate

findings, appellate review becomes speculation. In re Marriage of Banka, ¶ 9; In re

Marriage of Mills, ¶ 20.

¶23    Unlike the original order, the amended order resolves the disputes over income and

parenting days and explains the allocation of tax benefits. Katie does not ask us to disturb

those determinations in her supplemental appeal. The remaining problem is not simply an

absence of findings. The adopted worksheet contradicts the court’s decision to replace the

children’s private health insurance with Healthy Montana Kids coverage.

¶24    The accuracy of those inputs matters. Section 40-4-204(2), MCA, requires the court

to consider all relevant factors, including the financial resources of the parents, the standard

of living the children would have enjoyed had the marriage not been dissolved, the cost of

day care, and the parenting plan. The court must apply the uniform Guidelines, and the

resulting amount is presumptively adequate and reasonable. Section 40-4-204(3)(a), MCA.

When the court varies from the presumptive Guidelines amount, § 40-4-204(3)(a)-(b),

MCA, requires the court to find, by clear and convincing evidence, that application of the

standards and Guidelines would be unjust to the child or a party or inappropriate in the



                                               9
particular case, explain its reasons, and identify the amount that would otherwise be

ordered under the Guidelines.

¶25    The Guidelines require the court to supplement the primary support allowance with

specified expenses, including the actual cost of adding the children to an existing health

insurance policy or obtaining a child-only policy. Admin. R. M. 37.62.123(1)(b). The

court must credit each parent for the supplemental expense that parent pays. Admin. R. M.

37.62.123(3). Thus, the calculation must reflect both the expense and the parent who bears

it. In In re Marriage of Stevens, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="889816"><a href="/opinion/889816/in-re-the-marriage-of-stevens/#18" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Marriage of Stevens">2011 MT 106, ¶¶ 18-20</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="889816"><a href="/opinion/889816/in-re-the-marriage-of-stevens/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Marriage of Stevens">360 Mont. 344</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="889816"><a href="/opinion/889816/in-re-the-marriage-of-stevens/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Marriage of Stevens">253 P.3d 877</a></span><pre class="inline">,

we reversed a calculation that failed to account for the children’s insurance costs that the

father paid. Conversely, a court need not credit a parent for insurance costs the parent does

not incur. In re Marriage of Frick, ¶¶ 38-41.

¶26    The amended order and adopted worksheet do not agree on this essential input. The

order requires Healthy Montana Kids coverage and provides for allocation of private

insurance premiums only if the children later lose eligibility. The worksheet nonetheless

adds $6,534 in annual private premiums to the children’s supplemental needs at line 12b

and credits Colin for that expense at line 23. Those entries assume an expense that the

ordered coverage eliminates.      The fact that the worksheet accurately reflects other

determinations does not resolve this contradiction.

¶27    Although the District Court found the $770 calculation “the most accurate,” the

court did not explain why that worksheet continued to credit Colin with the children’s

private insurance expense after the order replaced that coverage. Its reasons for selecting

134 parenting days and the tax allocation do not resolve the insurance discrepancy. And

                                             10
the court did not enter variance findings under § 40-4-204(3), MCA, to support a departure

from a correctly calculated presumptive amount. Colin’s reliance on Midence v. Hampton,

</pre><span class="citation" data-id="887475"><a href="/opinion/887475/marriage-of-midence-v-hampton/#15" aria-description="Citation for case: Marriage of Midence v. Hampton">2006 MT 294, ¶ 15</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="887475"><a href="/opinion/887475/marriage-of-midence-v-hampton/" aria-description="Citation for case: Marriage of Midence v. Hampton">334 Mont. 388</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="887475"><a href="/opinion/887475/marriage-of-midence-v-hampton/" aria-description="Citation for case: Marriage of Midence v. Hampton">147 P.3d 227</a></span><pre class="inline">, does not cure that omission. The court

selected $770 as the accurate guideline calculation, not as an identified and justified

variance. Deference to its factual findings does not permit us to affirm a calculation that

contradicts them.

¶28    We do not hold that a district court may never consider post-hearing proposed

calculations. But where the court does so, the resulting order still must rest on findings

that identify the material facts the court actually found and explain how those facts support

the ordered amount. In In re Marriage of Frick, ¶¶ 14-15, the court entered findings and

then directed the parties to submit proposed child-support calculations.          Here, the

dispositive defect is substantive, not evidentiary: the worksheet the District Court adopted

does not implement the Healthy Montana Kids insurance arrangement the District Court

ordered. We therefore need not decide Katie’s separate contention that the court could not

consider the $770 worksheet because no party formally offered it as an exhibit at the

hearing.

¶29    Colin also argues that Katie should not obtain more support than she requested

below and that she failed to object to post-hearing submissions. Katie requested an

accurate calculation and contested the treatment of health-insurance costs. The parties

discussed removing those costs when they considered Healthy Montana Kids coverage.

Her agreement to submit proposed findings did not concede the accuracy of Colin’s

worksheet, and the particular inconsistency now before us arose when the court adopted its

                                             11
amended order. Her challenge seeks application of the Guidelines to the facts the court

found, not enforcement of a particular figure divorced from those facts.

¶30    2. Whether the District Court abused its discretion by declining to award earlier
          retroactive child support.

¶31    The District Court had authority to award retroactive child support. A district court

has discretion regarding retroactive support, but that discretion must rest on an adequate

record and a legally sufficient rationale. See In re Marriage of Miller, ¶¶ 14, 26; In re

Marriage of Banka, ¶ 9. Section 40-5-225(4)(b), MCA, expressly provides for crediting

payments under a temporary administrative order if the final order applies retroactively; it

does not require every final order to apply retroactively. We likewise have recognized that

a court may revise a temporary support obligation retroactively after hearing the evidence.

Stoneman v. Drollinger, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="885302"><a href="/opinion/885302/stoneman-v-drollinger/#50" aria-description="Citation for case: Stoneman v. Drollinger">2000 MT 274, ¶ 50</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="885302"><a href="/opinion/885302/stoneman-v-drollinger/" aria-description="Citation for case: Stoneman v. Drollinger">302 Mont. 107</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="885302"><a href="/opinion/885302/stoneman-v-drollinger/" aria-description="Citation for case: Stoneman v. Drollinger">14 P.3d 12</a></span><pre class="inline">, overruled in part

on other grounds by In re Marriage of Funk, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="890243"><a href="/opinion/890243/in-re-the-marriage-of-funk/#25" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Marriage of Funk">2012 MT 14, ¶ 25</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="890243"><a href="/opinion/890243/in-re-the-marriage-of-funk/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Marriage of Funk">363 Mont. 352</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="890243"><a href="/opinion/890243/in-re-the-marriage-of-funk/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Marriage of Funk">270 P.3d

39</a></span><pre class="inline">. Whether to do so remains a matter of judicial discretion. In re Marriage of Frick, ¶ 44.

¶32    We first address the finding that Katie waited nearly a year after the temporary order

to request back support. Katie’s January 2024 motion sought back support from the

dissolution filing date, while later arguments referenced other periods. Colin opposed the

request in February, and Katie renewed it in April. The omission of retroactivity from a

later list of issues did not make November 2024 the first notice of the request. The court

heard evidence and decided the issue on its merits. We therefore do not affirm based on

waiver, lack of notice, or the inaccurate finding of delay.




                                             12
¶33    Katie argues that the disparity between the temporary payment and the later

calculations, together with her financial hardship, required a retroactive award. She

distinguishes cases affirming denial of retroactivity because the custodial parent already

had received substantial financial benefits. In Midence, ¶ 21, the father had paid more than

the children needed under the mother’s expense calculations. In In re Marriage of Miller,

¶ 27, substantial housing assistance and continuing monthly payments supported the

finding that an additional retroactive award would produce a financial windfall. In In re

Marriage of Franks, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8028446"><a href="/opinion/8069946/in-re-the-marriage-of-franks/#71" aria-description="Citation for case: In re the Marriage of Franks">275 Mont. 66, 71</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8028446"><a href="/opinion/8069946/in-re-the-marriage-of-franks/#715" aria-description="Citation for case: In re the Marriage of Franks">909 P.2d 712, 715</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1996), the mother’s use of

income-producing marital property supported denial of support reaching back through an

eleven-year separation. Katie maintains that she received no comparable benefit.

¶34    Those cases identify circumstances supporting the particular decisions under

review; they do not establish that overpayment or a financial windfall is the only

permissible basis for declining retroactive support. In In re Marriage of Frick, the mother

sought retroactive support based on the father’s asserted lack of financial involvement and

her own difficulty maintaining employment without losing public benefits. The parties

disputed the extent of past support, and the father had incurred substantial travel costs to

respond to the dissolution petition. We affirmed the denial of retroactive support in light

of the disputed contributions and financial hardship both parties experienced. In re

Marriage of Frick, ¶¶ 43-44.       The inquiry therefore requires consideration of the

circumstances, not simply comparison of the temporary payment with a later calculation.

¶35    Katie also relies on Welch v. Welch, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="883488"><a href="/opinion/883488/marriage-of-welch-v-welch/#505" aria-description="Citation for case: Marriage of Welch v. Welch">273 Mont. 497, 505</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="883488"><a href="/opinion/883488/marriage-of-welch-v-welch/#137" aria-description="Citation for case: Marriage of Welch v. Welch">905 P.2d 132, 137</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1995).

There, we affirmed an award applying an increased obligation retroactively because the

                                            13
children’s expenses existed before the court entered its order. We rejected the father’s

objection that the resulting debt would be burdensome when the mother already had borne

the additional expenses. Welch establishes that an obligor’s reluctance to incur debt does

not defeat an otherwise warranted retroactive award. It does not make retroactivity

mandatory whenever the final obligation exceeds temporary support; the decision

expressly recognizes the court’s discretion. Welch, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="883488"><a href="/opinion/883488/marriage-of-welch-v-welch/#505" aria-description="Citation for case: Marriage of Welch v. Welch">273 Mont. at 505</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="883488"><a href="/opinion/883488/marriage-of-welch-v-welch/#137" aria-description="Citation for case: Marriage of Welch v. Welch">905 P.2d at 137</a></span><pre class="inline">.

¶36    The District Court recognized that discretion and considered the circumstances of

both households. It acknowledged that Katie had struggled financially, used savings, and

received assistance from her parents. It also found that Colin had maintained the children’s

insurance and supported them during his parenting time, including before the temporary

order, and had paid the required cash support after that order. It considered his fluctuating

income, increased housing expense, and the financial effect of an additional accumulated

obligation. The court found that both parents had met the children’s needs, did not find

that Colin received regular additional family income, and rejected as speculative Katie’s

assertions about the greater lifestyle he could provide. These findings addressed actual

past support and the financial circumstances of both parents, not merely compliance with

the temporary order.

¶37    Katie’s budget and testimony could support a different exercise of discretion. They

do not permit us to disregard the findings the court made after considering that evidence.

We do not treat her share of the marital estate as child support paid by Colin, or assistance

from her parents as an automatic substitute for his obligation. We do not hold that an

obligor’s housing expense takes priority over support. Neither compliance with the

                                             14
temporary order nor the absence of bad faith would alone defeat the request. Rather, as in

In re Marriage of Frick, the court weighed competing evidence concerning support and

financial hardship and concluded that an additional retroactive obligation was not

warranted. Our review asks whether that determination exceeded the bounds of reason,

not whether we would have reached the same result in the first instance.

¶38    The court’s statement that the children had not suffered harm also requires context.

Child support is not limited to avoiding deprivation of food or shelter. The statute requires

consideration of the standard of living the children would have enjoyed had the marriage

continued, along with the resources and needs of the children and their parents. Section

40-4-204(2), MCA; In re Marriage of Jackson, ¶ 11. A parent need not prove that children

suffered harm before the court may award retroactive support. Here, however, the court

did not stop with the absence of harm. It considered the parents’ contributions, their

financial circumstances, family assistance, and the evidence concerning the children’s

standard of living. We affirm that individualized assessment, not a rule that meeting basic

needs alone defeats retroactivity.

¶39    We also reject Colin’s argument that adding the $592 monthly insurance premium

identified in the findings to his $193 cash payment establishes that he paid more than the

$770 guideline amount. The $770 reflected in the worksheet is a net transfer payment that

already credits Colin for his $592 out-of-pocket insurance premium expense. Therefore,

comparing Colin’s actual outlay of $785 to the $770 net transfer payment double-counts

the insurance expense as a credit. Adding the premium to the cash payment and comparing

that sum with the net transfer payment improperly treats the insurance payment as an

                                             15
additional credit. Colin’s total obligation under the worksheet would actually be $1,362

(the $770 transfer payment plus the $592 premium he pays directly). The court could

consider insurance Colin actually provided when assessing the support the children

received, but those payments do not establish that $193 equaled a correctly calculated $770

transfer obligation. Our affirmance does not depend on that comparison.

¶40    The erroneous finding concerning Katie’s delay does not require another remand.

We must disregard errors that do not affect substantial rights. M. R. Civ. P. 61. An

erroneous finding unnecessary to support the decision does not require reversal. In re

Marriage of Anderson, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="879263"><a href="/opinion/879263/in-re-the-marriage-of-anderson/#481" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Marriage of Anderson">220 Mont. 477, 481</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="879263"><a href="/opinion/879263/in-re-the-marriage-of-anderson/#14" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Marriage of Anderson">717 P.2d 11, 14</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1986). The amended order

identified Colin’s actual contributions as its most important consideration, separately found

that both parents had met the children’s needs, and evaluated the asserted disparity in their

resources. Although Finding of Fact 38 referred to delay, the court separately concluded

in Conclusion of Law 10        that earlier support was unwarranted because of those

contributions and its assessment of the evidence, without identifying delay as a reason.

Those reasons address the merits of the request regardless of when Katie raised it. They

are reasons the District Court itself gave, not findings we supply on appeal. Considering

the amended order as a whole, the inaccurate chronology was not necessary to its

discretionary determination and does not undermine the independent grounds supporting

that determination.

¶41    The discrepancy in the ongoing insurance calculation does not require a different

result on retroactivity.   The calculation must change because the ordered coverage

eliminates an expense the worksheet continues to credit. The earlier period involved

                                             16
insurance Colin actually provided. The District Court did not have to treat that contribution

as nonexistent when deciding whether to impose an earlier retroactive obligation.

Conversely, affirming that discretionary decision does not validate the $770 calculation for

periods governed by the new arrangement. The amount due under the final order and

whether to extend that obligation into an earlier period present distinct questions.

¶42    Finally, the amended order incorrectly combined the public-domain citation for

In re Marriage of Frank, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6536339"><a href="/opinion/6658903/frank-v-frank/" aria-description="Citation for case: Frank v. Frank">443 P.3d 527</a></span><pre class="inline">, with the reporter

citation for In re Marriage of Franks, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8028446"><a href="/opinion/8069946/in-re-the-marriage-of-franks/" aria-description="Citation for case: In re the Marriage of Franks">275 Mont. 66</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8028446"><a href="/opinion/8069946/in-re-the-marriage-of-franks/" aria-description="Citation for case: In re the Marriage of Franks">909 P.2d 712</a></span><pre class="inline">. The former concerns

maintenance. In re Marriage of Frank, ¶ 1. The latter addresses retroactive child support

and the applicable abuse-of-discretion review. In re Marriage of Franks, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8028446"><a href="/opinion/8069946/in-re-the-marriage-of-franks/#70" aria-description="Citation for case: In re the Marriage of Franks">275 Mont. at

70-71</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8028446"><a href="/opinion/8069946/in-re-the-marriage-of-franks/#714" aria-description="Citation for case: In re the Marriage of Franks">909 P.2d at 714-15</a></span><pre class="inline">. The citation error does not change the governing law or the

court’s express recognition that it could award retroactive support.

¶43    The District Court considered the support the children actually received, the

financial circumstances of both parents, and Katie’s evidence of hardship, and explained

why it declined to impose an earlier retroactive obligation.         Its supported findings,

independent of the erroneous finding concerning delay addressed above, provide a

sufficient basis for that decision. Although the evidence could support a different result,

our review does not permit us to substitute our judgment for a reasonable exercise of the

District Court’s discretion. See In re Marriage of Frick, ¶¶ 43-44. On this record, the

District Court did not act arbitrarily, fail to exercise conscientious judgment, or exceed the

bounds of reason resulting in substantial injustice. In re Marriage of Miller, ¶ 14. We



                                             17
therefore conclude that the District Court did not abuse its discretion by declining to award

child support retroactive to a date before April 1, 2025.

¶44    Because we reverse only the support calculation, we do not direct the District Court

to adopt any specific support figure now or direct an award of earlier retroactive support.

The record includes a post-hearing CSSD worksheet attached as Exhibit C to Colin’s

proposed findings that uses 134 parenting days, one child tax credit for each parent, the

earned income tax credit for both children to Katie, and no children’s insurance premium,

producing $957 per month. The court did not adopt that calculation or resolve its

differences from the other worksheets. Determination of the correct support amount

remains for the District Court on remand. Our affirmance of the denial of earlier support

does not validate the $770 calculation for periods governed by the new insurance

arrangement.

¶45    Finally, we deny Colin’s request for appellate attorney fees and costs as sanctions

under M. R. App. P. 19(5). That rule permits sanctions for an appeal that is frivolous,

vexatious, brought for harassment or delay, or taken without substantial or reasonable

grounds. Katie’s appeal raises substantial and reasonable grounds, and she has established

reversible error in the support calculation.

                                      CONCLUSION

¶46    The child-support calculation in the amended order does not implement the findings

and health-insurance arrangement the District Court adopted. We reverse the April 15,

2026 Amended Findings of Fact, Conclusions of Law and Order insofar as it fixes child

support at $770 per month and remand for further proceedings consistent with this Opinion.

                                               18
On remand, the District Court shall determine the correct presumptive support amount

under the Guidelines consistently with its unchallenged determinations concerning income,

parenting days, tax treatment, and health-insurance coverage, and make any required

variance findings if it departs from that amount. The parties shall have an opportunity to

address the corrected calculation before the court enters its order. The court may take

additional evidence if necessary to determine the actual insurance expense for the relevant

period.

¶47    The corrected obligation shall apply from April 1, 2025, the effective date specified

in the operative provision of the amended order. The court shall credit support already

paid and account for any actual insurance expense during the relevant period without

allowing a credit for premiums no parent incurred. Correction of the amount due from that

effective date does not reopen the request to extend the obligation to January 17, 2024, or

an earlier date. Our affirmance of the denial of earlier retroactive support does not postpone

correction of the appealed award until entry of the order on remand.

¶48    Affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded.
