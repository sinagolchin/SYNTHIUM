Justice Beth Baker delivered the Opinion of the Court.

¶1     Appellant Gateway Conservation Alliance (Gateway) appeals the Eighteenth

Judicial District Court’s dismissal of its complaint for failure to exhaust administrative

remedies. We conclude that Gateway may challenge the constitutionality of the statute

authorizing the DEQ permit at issue only after completing its administrative appeal before

the Board of Environmental Review. We therefore affirm.

                 FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

¶2     In March 2024, the Montana Department of Environmental Quality (“DEQ”)

approved Opencut Permit #3462 (“Permit 3462”). TMC, Inc., which applied for the permit

in May 2023, plans to mine clay, gravel, and sand at the “Black Site” in Gallatin County,

Montana, for twenty years.

¶3     Opencut mines must conform to The Opencut Mining Act (“the Act”), codified at

Title 82, chapter 4, part 4, MCA. The Act is intended to “provide adequate remedies for

the protection of the environment[] . . . from degradation and . . . to prevent . . . [the]

degradation of natural resources.” Section 82-4-402(1), MCA. DEQ is responsible under

the Act for determining whether to grant or deny opencut mining permits.

Section 82-4-422(1)(a), MCA.       During the 2021 Montana Legislative Session, the

Legislature passed House Bill 599 revising opencut mining laws to “provid[e] less stringent

applications for certain opencut operations[.]”

¶4     The contested case provisions of the Montana Administrative Procedure Act

(“MAPA”) apply to permits issued under the Act. Section 82-4-427(4), MCA. A person

seeking to appeal approval of a permit application must submit a written request to the

                                             3
Board of Environmental Review (“Board”) within 30 days of DEQ’s decision.

Section 82-4-427(1)(a), MCA. Gateway filed a notice of appeal with the Board in

March 2024. Gateway claimed that Permit 3462 violates the Act’s intentions, ground

water requirements, plan of operation requirements, and the requirement that DEQ verify

information submitted by the applicant. Gateway requested that the Board find that DEQ

violated statutory requirements and set aside the approval of Permit 3462.

¶5     Four months after filing its administrative appeal, Gateway filed a complaint against

DEQ in Gallatin County District Court, while the appeal was pending before the Board.1

Gateway asserted that the HB 599 amendments to the Act violated the right to a clean and

healthful environment under Article II, Section 3 of the Montana Constitution. Gateway

sought a declaration that HB 599 is unconstitutional, a permanent injunction against its

enforcement, and vacatur of Permit 3462. TMC intervened with leave of court.

¶6     DEQ moved to dismiss Gateway’s constitutional challenge to HB 599 because

Gateway did not exhaust its administrative remedies in accordance with MAPA. The

District Court agreed and granted the DEQ’s motion to dismiss. Gateway now appeals.

This Court granted the Montana Contractors Association leave to file an amicus curiae

brief supporting DEQ’s position.

                                     DISCUSSION

¶8     Section 2-4-702(1)(a), MCA, allows judicial review in a contested agency case after

the exhaustion of all available administrative remedies. A party generally may not raise

any question not raised in front of the agency without a showing of good cause.

Section 2-4-702(1)(b), MCA. The statute provides, however, that “[a] party who proceeds

before an agency under the terms of a particular statute may not be precluded from

questioning the validity of that statute on judicial review[.]” Section 2-4-702(1)(b), MCA.

And an agency action “is immediately reviewable if review of the final agency decision

would not provide an adequate remedy.” Section 2-4-701, MCA.

¶9     The District Court looked to City of Great Falls v. Int’l Ass’n of Fire Fighters,

</pre><span class="citation" data-id="10757937"><a href="/opinion/10291349/great-falls-v-assoc-of-firefighters/" aria-description="Citation for case: Great Falls v. Assoc. of Firefighters">2024 MT 302</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation no-link">419 Mont. 262</span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation no-link">560 P.3d 621</span><pre class="inline"> (City of Great Falls), for guidance in

considering DEQ’s motion to dismiss. The District Court explained that although City of

Great Falls recognized a facial constitutional challenge to the administrative scheme as an

exception to exhaustion requirements, the exception was not present in Gateway’s case.



                                            5
¶10    In City of Great Falls, the City imposed a new drug and alcohol policy on all

employees, including union members subject to a collective bargaining agreement. City of

Great Falls, ¶ 3. The City failed to obtain the consent of the unions and denied demands

for collective bargaining regarding the new policy. City of Great Falls, ¶ 3. The unions

began filing individual labor practice complaints with the Montana Board of Personnel

Appeals (“MBPA”). City of Great Falls, ¶ 4. The MBPA consolidated the complaints for

a contested case administrative hearing and adjudication. City of Great Falls, ¶ 5. The

agency hearing examiner issued a decision in favor of the unions. City of Great Falls, ¶ 6.

Rather than file exceptions to the proposed decision and order for final MBPA review, the

City petitioned the district court for judicial review under § 2-4-701, MCA. City of Great

Falls, ¶ 7. In district court, the City argued that assertions of error for pure questions of

law are exempt from administrative remedy as required by § 2-4-702(1)(a), MCA. City of

Great Falls, ¶ 7. The City also argued that waiting for review of a final agency decision

by a “non-judicial board” would not remedy the asserted errors of law and would “not be

in the interests of judicial economy.” City of Great Falls, ¶ 7. The unions responded that

the City could not circumvent the exhaustion requirement and distinguished the case from

prior cases that allowed exception from administrative exhaustion. City of Great Falls,

¶ 7. The district court dismissed the City’s petition for failure to exhaust administrative

remedies in accordance with the remedy provided under the requirements of the Unfair

Labor Practices Act. City of Great Falls, ¶ 7.

¶11    On appeal, this Court reasoned that “a sweeping jurisprudential pure question of

law, or narrower pure question of constitutional law, exception to § 2-4-702(1)(a), MCA,

                                             6
would irreconcilably conflict with the authorized scope of the final agency review remedy

expressly provided by [MAPA] . . . .” City of Great Falls, ¶ 28. A facial constitutional

challenge must contest the administrative procedure to escape the exhaustion requirement.

City of Great Falls, ¶¶ 28, 30. We clarified that a constitutional issue accompanied by

assertions of error regarding correctness of an agency decision does not qualify for the

exception to the exhaustion doctrine.         City of Great Falls, ¶ 23; see generally

Shoemaker v. Denke, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="887045"><a href="/opinion/887045/shoemaker-v-denke/" aria-description="Citation for case: Shoemaker v. Denke">2004 MT 11</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="887045"><a href="/opinion/887045/shoemaker-v-denke/" aria-description="Citation for case: Shoemaker v. Denke">319 Mont. 238</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation no-link">89 P.3d 4</span><pre class="inline"> (exception applies only to

pure constitutional law questions not dependent on fact-based determinations in the

jurisdiction of the agency to decide) (abrogated in part by City of Great Falls).

¶12    Under § 82-4-427(1), MCA, “a person whose interests are or may be adversely

affected by a final decision of the department to approve or disapprove a permit

application . . . is entitled to a hearing before the board[.]” Gateway acknowledges that it

was required to proceed under this statute but asserts that because its District Court

complaint presents a “facial constitutional challenge,” it permissibly brought that claim

independently. Gateway argues that exhaustion is not required because the District Court

and the Board are considering different legal and factual questions and because exhaustion

would be futile, as the Board has no authority to resolve a facial constitutional challenge.

¶13    DEQ responds that Gateway’s complaint does not raise a pure question of

constitutional law that challenged the administrative scheme. It instead challenges the

statutory basis for the permit. DEQ argues that, in accordance with MAPA, Gateway

cannot evade its contested case proceeding before the Board by filing a separate action in

District Court because the request for declaratory relief is not independent of an agency

                                             7
action. TMC and the Montana Contractors Association likewise urge this Court to follow

the rationale of City of Great Falls and affirm the District Court’s grant of DEQ’s motion

to dismiss.

¶14    Gateway’s appeal to the Board advanced various alleged violations of the Act

specific to TMC’s permit application and the Black Site. Gateway requested that the Board

set aside approval of Permit 3462 as unlawful and void.                 Gateway’s District Court

complaint similarly recounts the alleged errors specific to TMC’s application and the Black

Site in support of its request to declare HB 599 unconstitutional and vacate Permit 3462.2

Gateway pleaded that its “constitutional rights to a clean and healthful environment and

adequate remedies to prevent unreasonable environmental harms was infringed by []

DEQ’s permitting of the Black [Site] . . . .” Both in its appeal to the Board and in its

complaint before the District Court, Gateway requested vacatur of Permit 3462 because of

its alleged unconstitutional or otherwise unlawful issuance.

¶15    The District Court reasoned that “to have an adequate picture of the constitutional

implications of the challenged statute, the [c]ourt needs facts that pertain to that question[]”

presented to the agency.        As discussed in City of Great Falls, constitutional issues

accompanied by assertions of error by an agency do not qualify for the exception to the

exhaustion doctrine as a pure question of law. City of Great Falls, ¶ 23. Gateway’s

¶16    Gateway points out that the Board does not have the authority to decide

constitutional issues in a declaratory judgment action. Gateway argues that, due to the

jurisdiction conferred to the District Court and the doctrine of separation of powers,

with the timing of when it may exercise that jurisdiction. DEQ argues that the Uniform

Declaratory Judgment Act (“UDJA”) cannot be invoked to escape MAPA requirements.

¶17    Immediate review of an agency action is available “if review of the final agency

decision would not provide an adequate remedy.” Section 2-4-701, MCA. As stated

above, parties who have “exhausted all administrative remedies available within the

agency” and are “aggrieved by a final written decision in a contested case [are] entitled to

judicial review[.]” Section 2-4-702(1)(a), MCA. The administrative remedy exhaustion

requirement   “prevents    courts   from   ‘premature . . . entangl[ement] . . . in   abstract

disagreements over [regulatory] policies’ . . . .” N. Star Dev., LLC v. Mont. Pub. Serv.

Board’s decision. We agree with Gateway that it did not waive its right to go to the district

court by appealing DEQ’s permitting decision to the Board. But because Gateway filed its

complaint without a final Board decision, administrative remedies have not been

exhausted.

¶18    Gateway attempts to liken its situation to Held v. State, </pre><span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/MT/2024/312/">2024 MT 312</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation no-link">419 Mont.

case. Gateway also cites Mont. Env’t Info. Ctr. v. facial challenges to proceed in the absence of an administrative proceeding. DEQ responds

that Held is distinct from the current case because it involved a constitutional challenge

that was independent from agency action.

¶19    Gateway’s reliance on Held and MEIC is misplaced. Held did not involve a

challenge to a specific permit. Held, ¶ 53. It instead involved a challenge to the Montana

Environmental Policy Act, untethered to an ongoing agency action. Held, ¶ 5. MEIC also

did not involve a pending administrative review; the plaintiffs there challenged an

amendment to the Water Quality Act that excluded “certain activities from review pursuant

to the act’s nondegradation policy[.]” MEIC, ¶ 37. Both of these cases thus presented a

procedural posture distinct from the current case. MEIC, ¶ 17. In MEIC, we did not disturb

the premise that parties are entitled to bring a direct action to enforce fundamental rights,

and we need not do so here. MEIC, ¶¶ 28, 45. In this case, both actions seek invalidation



                                             10
of the same permit. Gateway’s pending administrative case requires it to comply with

MAPA requirements before filing in the District Court.

¶20    Section 2-4-702(1)(b), MCA, creates an exception to the exhaustion requirement for

a party to question the validity of a particular statute. But the statute makes clear the

required procedure and process. A party challenging an agency decision subject to MAPA

first must bring its claims to the agency and obtain a final ruling. Once that final agency

decision is issued, the aggrieved party may seek judicial review of (1) all questions raised

before the agency, (2) the validity of the statute underlying the agency’s actions, and

(3) upon a showing of “good cause,” “any other” question it did not first raise in the

contested case proceeding. Section 2-4-702(1)(b), MCA. Therefore, though Gateway is

correct that it does not have to litigate its constitutional claim to the Board, it does first

have to exhaust its contested case proceeding on the permit. If the Board rules adversely,

Gateway may petition for judicial review, at which time it may present the constitutional

claim.3

¶21    As City of Great Falls explained, this honors both the judiciary’s exclusive authority

“to determine, interpret, or apply applicable statutory or constitutional law” and the

Legislature’s express constitutional authority to “provide for direct review by the

district court of decisions of administrative agencies.”            City of Great Falls, ¶ 28;

Mont. Const. art. VII, § 4(2). “As an express procedural prerequisite for judicial review of

¶22    As we further clarified, an exception to MAPA’s exhaustion requirement may be

found “only when the matter at issue involves a pure question of constitutional law, not

accompanied by an issue regarding a fact-based determination within the pertinent

jurisdiction of the agency to decide.” City of Great Falls, ¶ 23. In this action, Gateway

challenges the Permit—its constitutional arguments are “accompanied by” issues regarding

determinations within the Board’s jurisdiction. Section 2-4-702(1)(b), MCA, expressly

preserves Gateway’s ability to raise its constitutional challenge on judicial review of any

adverse Board decision.

¶23    This Court long has recognized that “courts should avoid constitutional issues

whenever possible.” In re License Revocation of Gildersleeve, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="884282"><a href="/opinion/884282/in-re-the-license-revocation-of-gildersleeve/#485" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the License Revocation of Gildersleeve">283 Mont. 479, 485</a></span><pre class="inline">,

</pre><span class="citation" data-id="884282"><a href="/opinion/884282/in-re-the-license-revocation-of-gildersleeve/#709" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the License Revocation of Gildersleeve">942 P.2d 705, 709</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1997) (citation omitted). The District Court reasoned that if it issued

“a ruling that agreed with [Gateway] that the statutory scheme is facially an environmental

violation of constitutional proportions” and then the Board found no evidence of damage,

“it would operate as a reversal of the facial challenge.” We agree with the District Court.

Tethered as it is to Permit 3462 at issue, Gateway’s constitutional challenge may be mooted

if the Board invalidates Permit 3462 on other grounds. This process thus harmonizes with

our oft-invoked principle that the courts will not reach constitutional issues except as a last

resort. The exhaustion of administrative remedies “allows an agency to make a factual

                                               12
record and correct its own errors within its specific expertise.” City of Great Falls, ¶ 17

(internal quotations omitted; citation omitted).       If the Board agrees with Gateway’s

arguments in the administrative appeal, consideration of the constitutional claims would

be unnecessary to grant Gateway the relief it seeks.

Section 2-4-702(1)(b), MCA, expressly permits Gateway to challenge the statute on

argument that proceeding in front of the Board would be futile is incorrect.

¶26    The District Court additionally addressed its concern with piecemeal litigation.

Gateway argues that first requiring a permit at the administrative level gives the agency a

Dep’t of Env’t Quality, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="889652"><a href="/opinion/889652/jefferson-county-v-department-of-environmental-quality/#32" aria-description="Citation for case: Jefferson County v. and facilitates efficiency in the ongoing administrative action.
