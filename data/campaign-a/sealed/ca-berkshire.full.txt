                 1. Jurisdiction: Appeal and Error. A jurisdictional question which does
                    not involve a factual dispute is determined by an appellate court as a
                    matter of law.
                 2. Motions to Dismiss: Appeal and Error. A district court’s denial of a
                    motion to dismiss is reviewed de novo.
                 3. Evidence: Appeal and Error. A trial court has the discretion to deter-
                    mine the relevancy and admissibility of evidence, and such determina-
                    tions will not be disturbed on appeal unless they constitute an abuse of
                    that discretion.
                 4. Judges: Words and Phrases. A judicial abuse of discretion exists
                    only when the reasons or rulings of a trial judge are clearly untenable,
                    unfairly depriving a litigant of a substantial right and denying just
                    results in matters submitted for disposition.
                 5. Torts: Equity. A breach of fiduciary duty is a tort claim that lies in
                    equity.
                 6. Equity: Appeal and Error. On appeal from an equity action, an appel-
                    late court decides factual questions de novo on the record and, as to
                    questions of both fact and law, is obligated to reach a conclusion inde-
                    pendent of the trial court’s determination.
                 7. Equity: Evidence: Appeal and Error. In an appeal of an equity action,
                    where credible evidence is in conflict on a material question of fact, an
                    appellate court considers and may give weight to the fact that the trial
                    court heard and observed the witnesses and their manner of testifying
                    and accepted one version of the facts rather than another.

 8. Attorney Fees: Appeal and Error. On appeal, a trial court’s decision
    awarding or denying attorney fees will be upheld absent an abuse of
    discretion.
 9. Jurisdiction: Appeal and Error. Before reaching the legal issues
    presented for review, it is the duty of an appellate court to determine
    whether it has jurisdiction over the matter before it.
10. Jurisdiction: Judgments: Final Orders: Appeal and Error. To deter-
    mine whether it has jurisdiction, an appellate court looks to </pre><span class="citation no-link">Neb. Rev.
    Stat. §§ 25-1911</span><pre class="inline"> (Reissue 2016) and 25-1912 (Cum. Supp. 2024).
    Together, these statutes generally prescribe that for an appellate court
    to acquire jurisdiction of an appeal, the party must be appealing from a
    judgment or decree rendered or from a final order.
11. Judgments: Final Orders: Words and Phrases. A “judgment rend­
    ered,” as required by Neb. Rev. Stat § 25-1911 (Reissue 2016), is a final
    determination of the rights of the parties in an action, which is set forth
    by the court in a single, signed written document stating all of the relief
    granted or denied in an action.
12. Rules of the Supreme Court: Pleadings: Jurisdiction. A party may
    challenge the court’s subject matter jurisdiction under Neb. Ct. R. Pldg.
    § 6-1112(b)(1) (rev. 2025) by presenting either a facial challenge or a
    factual challenge.
13. ____: ____: ____. In a facial challenge under Neb. Ct. R. Pldg.
    § 6-1112(b)(1) (rev. 2025), the party asserts the allegations of the com-
    plaint are insufficient to establish the court’s jurisdiction over the sub-
    ject matter of the case. When a facial challenge is presented, the court
    will look only to the complaint to determine whether the plaintiff has
    sufficiently alleged a basis of subject matter jurisdiction.
14. ____: ____: ____. In a factual challenge under Neb. Ct. R. Pldg.
    § 6-1112(b)(1) (rev. 2025), the party asserts there is no jurisdiction
    over the subject matter of the case notwithstanding the allegations of
    the complaint. When a factual challenge is presented, the court may
    consider and weigh evidence outside of the pleadings to answer the
    jurisdictional question.
15. Trial: Evidence: Appeal and Error. To preserve a claimed error in
    admission of evidence, a litigant must make a timely objection which
    specifies the ground of the objection to the offered evidence.
16. Trial: Evidence. An objection to the admission of evidence is generally
    not timely unless it is made at the earliest opportunity after the ground
    for the objection becomes apparent.
17. Limitations of Actions: Pleadings: Waiver. The statute of limitations is
    an affirmative defense that is waived if the defendant fails to plead it.

18. Partnerships: Records: Accounting. A managing partner having exclu-
    sive control of a partnership business not only controls production,
    sales, and distribution of products but is responsible for keeping all
    records and books of account, and he or she is required to account to the
    partnership for any benefit or profit derived from any transaction con-
    nected with the formation, operation, or liquidation of the partnership or
    from any use by him or her of its property.
19. Corporations: Accounting: Proof. Although the burden is ordinarily
    upon the party seeking an accounting to produce evidence to sustain the
    accounting, when another person is in control of the books and has man-
    aged the business, that other person is in the position of a trustee and
    must make a proper accounting.
20. Attorney Fees. Attorney fees and expenses may be recovered in a civil
    action only where provided for by statute or when a recognized and
    accepted uniform course of procedure has been to allow recovery of
    attorney fees.

                     I. INTRODUCTION
   This appeal raises a number of questions regarding juris-
diction, ownership interests in a partnership, damages for
breach of fiduciary duty, and related matters. For the reasons
set forth below, we conclude that we have jurisdiction over
the appeal and all matters raised in it. We also conclude
that the district court did not err in ruling that Richard N.
Berkshire (Rick) no longer has an ownership interest in
Berkshire Partnership, LP, in requiring Rick to pay damages
for his breach of fiduciary duties while he was the managing

                       II. BACKGROUND
              1. Parties and Properties Involved
   Rick, Leslie J. Berkshire, and Laurie B. Meyers are all chil-
dren of Joanne Berkshire and Robert H. Berkshire (Bob). Paul
Meyers is Laurie’s husband. The other named parties are the
grandchildren of Joanne and Bob.
   Berkshire Partnership was formed in 1988 and converted
into a limited partnership in 2005. Its original general part-
ners were Bob, Rick, Leslie, and Laurie. At least some of the
partnership interests of Bob and Laurie were later transferred,
and limited partners were added. However, this appeal centers
primarily on Rick, Leslie, and Laurie.
   Rick was the managing partner of Berkshire Partnership
from its inception, although Bob remained involved in the
partnership’s operations until his death in February 2011.
Before Bob died, the family would meet each year around
Christmas to review financial matters. However, those meet-
ings ceased after Bob’s death, and Leslie and Laurie would
subsequently testify that they did not receive any “annual
updates” or “year-end summaries” for the years between 2010
and 2015. They eventually received information covering the
2010 through 2015 period in 2016, and they received infor-
mation about 2016 and 2017 in 2018. The information they
ultimately received was, in their opinion, “disorganized” and
incomplete. This factor, coupled with certain withdrawals that
Rick made from Joanne’s accounts, over which he then had
power of attorney, contributed to his removal as the managing
partner in 2019. Rick was replaced by Paul, who was added
as a general partner through an assignment and transfer of
membership interest from Laurie, although Rick disputed the
validity of the assignment and his replacement.
   Berkshire Partnership owned between 85 and 100 per-
cent of Berkshire Family Building, LLC. Angela Burmeister

dispute is determined by an appellate court as a matter of law. 2

reviewed de novo. 3

evancy and admissibility of evidence, and such determinations
will not be disturbed on appeal unless they constitute an abuse
of that discretion. 4 A judicial abuse of discretion exists only
when the reasons or rulings of a trial judge are clearly unten-
able, unfairly depriving a litigant of a substantial right and
denying just results in matters submitted for disposition. 5
   [5-7] We have previously opined that a breach of fiduciary
duty is a tort claim that lies in equity. 6 On appeal from an
equity action, an appellate court decides factual questions de
novo on the record and, as to questions of both fact and law, is
obligated to reach a conclusion independent of the trial court’s
determination. 7 However, in an appeal of an equity action,
where credible evidence is in conflict on a material question of
fact, an appellate court considers and may give weight to the
fact that the trial court heard and observed the witnesses and
their manner of testifying and accepted one version of the facts
rather than another. 8

                          V. ANALYSIS
                         1. Jurisdiction
   [9] Before reaching the legal issues presented for review,
it is the duty of an appellate court to determine whether it
has jurisdiction over the matter before it. 10 In this case, the
parties have raised several jurisdictional issues that we must
address before we reach the merits of the appeal. Two of those
issues pertain to Rick’s counterclaims and the timeliness of his
appeal. Two other issues pertain to the district court’s exercise
of subject matter jurisdiction over the case or specific matters
within the case. Where the lower court lacks subject matter
jurisdiction to adjudicate the merits of a claim, issue, or ques-
tion, we, too, would lack the power to determine the merits
of the claim, issue, or question presented to the lower court. 11
Rick also raises several other jurisdictional issues as second-
ary arguments supporting specific assignments of error, which
we discuss later in the opinion. Ultimately, for the reasons set
forth below, we conclude that we have jurisdiction over this
appeal and all the issues raised in it.
