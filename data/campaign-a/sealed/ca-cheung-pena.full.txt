BRODY, Chief Justice.
       This appeal addresses the period within which a judgment creditor must renew an
unsatisfied judgment. Leanne Cheung obtained a judgment in 2006 against Raymundo G. Pena,
her former attorney who has since been disbarred. That judgment was renewed in 2011 and again
in 2016.
       When Cheung moved to renew her judgment in 2016, the district court granted the motion
but also recognized an error in the judgment amount and entered an amended judgment reflecting
the correct amount. Cheung filed a motion to renew the judgment again in 2025, and the district
court denied that motion on timeliness grounds. Cheung challenges that decision on appeal.
       When Cheung’s judgment was issued in 2006, Idaho Code section 10-1111(1) required a
judgment creditor to renew a judgment every five years. I.C. § 10-1111(1) (2004). In 2016, the
Idaho Legislature changed the renewal period to ten years, but the enacting clause of that
amendment expressly provided that the ten-year period applies “only to judgments issued on and
after July 1, 2015.” Act of Mar. 30, 2016, ch. 269, § 2, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2016 Idaho Sess. Laws 724</span><pre class="inline">, 724. This


                                                 1
critical limitation was not codified in the Idaho Code. This case is an important reminder for all
practitioners that “the Idaho Code is merely evidence of the laws enacted by the legislature as set
forth in the session laws.” Peterson v. Peterson, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2657057"><a href="/opinion/2657057/debra-peterson-v-dept-of-hw/#90" aria-description="Citation for case: Debra Peterson v. Dept of H&amp;W">156 Idaho 85, 90</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2657057"><a href="/opinion/2657057/debra-peterson-v-dept-of-hw/#1249" aria-description="Citation for case: Debra Peterson v. Dept of H&amp;W">320 P.3d 1244, 1249</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2014)
(emphasis added). In other words, “[t]he Idaho Code is not the law.” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2657057"><a href="/opinion/2657057/debra-peterson-v-dept-of-hw/" aria-description="Citation for case: Debra Peterson v. Dept of H&amp;W">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> The session laws are the
law.
       Today, we hold that the 2016 amendment unambiguously applies section 10‑1111(1)’s
ten‑year renewal period exclusively to judgments finally concluding a controversy “on or after
July 1, 2015.” Act of Mar. 30, 2016, ch. 269, § 2, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2016 Idaho Sess. Laws 724</span><pre class="inline">, 724. Cheung’s
judgment was subject to the five‑year renewal period because it was issued prior to July 1, 2015,
and its subsequent renewal and amendment after that date do not permit her to avail herself of the
more generous renewal period. Neither the renewal nor amendment of the judgment finally
concluded her litigation with Pena. Accordingly, we affirm the district court’s denial of Cheung’s
motion to renew the judgment.

          As a “pure question[] of law,” this Court freely reviews a district court’s “construction and
application of legislative acts.” State, Dep’t of Health &amp; Welfare v. Doe (2022-32), </pre><span class="citation no-link">171 Idaho 677</span><pre class="inline">,
680, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9637450"><a href="/opinion/9421753/idhw-v-john-doe-2022-32/#718" aria-description="Citation for case: IDHW v. John Doe (2022-32)">525 P.3d 715, 718</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2023).
                                             III. ANALYSIS
   A. The district court correctly concluded that Senate Bill 1362’s ten-year renewal period
      does not govern Cheung’s judgment by virtue of its renewal in 2016.
          Cheung obtained a judgment against Pena in 2006, which she renewed in 2011 and again
in 2016. When she moved to renew the judgment a third time in 2025, about nine years after it had
last been renewed, the district court denied Cheung’s motion, concluding that the judgment was
subject to a five-year renewal period and could no longer be renewed because it had expired in
2021.
          The district court began its analysis with a recitation of the relevant legislative action
amending Idaho Code section 10-1111. It observed that the current version of the statute provides
for a ten-year renewal period, which superseded the prior five-year period as of July 1, 2015, by
the terms of the 2016 amendment. Act of Mar. 30, 2016, ch. 269, § 2, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2016 Idaho Sess. Laws 724</span><pre class="inline">,



                                                   3
724. The legislature again amended section 10-1111 in 2018. On this occasion, it replaced the term
“renewed judgment” with “an order renewing judgment,” Act of Mar. 26, 2018, ch. 284, § 1, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2018
Idaho Sess. Laws 672</span><pre class="inline">, 672, to make clear “that the entry of an order renewing judgment . . .
maintains the original judgment’s date and collection priority,” Statement of Purpose, S.B. 1225,
64th Leg., 2d Reg. Sess. (Idaho 2018).
       From this, the district court first concluded that the ten-year renewal period could not
displace the original five-year period governing Cheung’s 2006 judgment simply because the
judgment was renewed in 2016, even if renewal occurred after the 2016 amendment’s July 1, 2015,
effective date. It reasoned that the 2016 amendment’s effective date applied to the entry of the
original judgment, not to the entry of an order renewing that judgment. Relying on this Court’s
decision in Smith v. Smith, </pre><span class="citation no-link">164 Idaho 46</span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4283661"><a href="/opinion/4506408/smith-v-smith/" aria-description="Citation for case: Smith v. Smith">423 P.3d 998</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2018), the district court ruled that the
judgment here was subject to the prior five-year renewal period in perpetuity, since the date of its
initial entry preceded July 1, 2015.
       Furthermore, the district court concluded that the amended judgment entered in 2016 was
not a “new” judgment governed by the ten-year renewal period applicable to judgments entered
on or after July 1, 2015. It reasoned that the 2016 amended judgment merely rectified “clerical
mistakes” and that its only aim was “to make the judgment . . . speak the truth” rather than “to
make it say something other than what originally was pronounced.” In the district court’s view,
the 2018 amendment to section 10-1111 made “clear that the legislature did not intend for orders
renewing judgments to act as new judgments,” and the court appeared to assume the same was true
of judgments simultaneously renewed and amended. Accordingly, the district court ruled that the
judgment expired on December 11, 2021, and denied Cheung’s motion to renew.
       Cheung finds error in the district court’s reasoning. She insists that section 10-1111(1) is
ambiguous, without identifying the specific words or clauses that are reasonably susceptible to
more than one meaning. Then, instead of grappling with any ambiguity, Cheung leapfrogs to a
discussion of legislative history and public policy to support her contention that the 2016 renewal
brought her judgment under the 2016 amendment’s ten-year renewal period, rather than the five-
year period applicable to the original 2006 judgment. Pena disagrees. He relies on a footnote in
this Court’s opinion in Smith, 164 Idaho at 48 n.1, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4283661"><a href="/opinion/4506408/smith-v-smith/" aria-description="Citation for case: Smith v. Smith">423 P.3d at 1000</a></span><pre class="inline"> n.1, to support the contention
that the “issuance of a renewed judgment does not . . . convert the requirement of renewal every
five years to the ten-year renewal period.”


                                                 4
       Stated differently, Pena argues that the 2016 amendment unambiguously applies its ten-
year renewal period exclusively to original judgments entered on or after July 1, 2015:
               SECTION 2. An emergency existing therefor, which emergency is hereby
       declared to exist, this act shall be in full force and effect on and after its passage
       and approval, and retroactively to July 1, 2015, and shall apply only to judgments
       issued on and after July 1, 2015, by a court of competent jurisdiction.
Act of Mar. 30, 2016, ch. 269, § 2, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2016 Idaho Sess. Laws 724</span><pre class="inline">, 724 (emphasis added). At bottom,
this case turns on the meaning of “issued,” as it is used in the 2016 amendment’s enacting clause.
Cheung implicitly maintains that, when she renewed her judgment in 2016, it “issued” anew and,
therefore, became subject to the ten-year renewal period going forward. Pena, by contrast, asserts
that a judgment “issues” only once—the date the original judgment is entered, thereby finally
concluding the controversy—and that Cheung’s 2016 renewal necessarily did not have this
conclusive effect.
       Pena advances the more reasonable interpretation, even if it is derived without direct
reference to the plain language of the enacting clause. On many occasions, we have explained that
the objective of statutory interpretation is to give effect to the “plain, express meaning of the
provision in question.” State v. Yager, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2639065"><a href="/opinion/2639065/state-v-yager/#689" aria-description="Citation for case: State v. Yager">139 Idaho 680, 689</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2639065"><a href="/opinion/2639065/state-v-yager/#665" aria-description="Citation for case: State v. Yager">85 P.3d 656, 665</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2004) (citation
omitted). It follows that “[t]he starting point for any statutory interpretation is the literal wording
of the statute,” from which “the court will give the statute’s language its plain, obvious and rational
meaning.” Martin v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2639180"><a href="/opinion/2639180/martin-v-state-farm-mutual-automobile-insurance/#246" aria-description="Citation for case: Martin v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance">138 Idaho 244, 246</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2639180"><a href="/opinion/2639180/martin-v-state-farm-mutual-automobile-insurance/#603" aria-description="Citation for case: Martin v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance">61 P.3d 601, 603</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2002).
       When a judgment “issues,” it is “put forth officially.” Issue, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th
ed. 2024); Issue, Webster’s Third New International Dictionary (1967) (“[T]o be or have a
consequence or final outcome.”). That is to say, the issuance of judgment marks the point at which
“the court has finally decided the controversy.” Restatement (First) of Judgments § 1 (1942).
“Issuance” does not refer to non-concluding acts like “renewal,” which merely asks the court to
restate its earlier decision in order to facilitate enforcement. Therefore, Cheung’s interpretation is
unreasonable. The meaning of “issue” cannot be stretched to encompass the renewal of a judgment
because the term denotes the act by which the court “puts forth” its final decision, ending the
lawsuit, and finally determining the parties’ rights. See Watson v. Watson, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="874534"><a href="/opinion/874534/watson-v-watson/#217" aria-description="Citation for case: Watson v. Watson">144 Idaho 214, 217</a></span><pre class="inline">,
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="874534"><a href="/opinion/874534/watson-v-watson/#854" aria-description="Citation for case: Watson v. Watson">159 P.3d 851, 854</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2007). The term “renewal” does not perform that function. Thus, Cheung’s
reading is inconsistent with the plain meaning of “issued.”




                                                  5
       We conclude that the 2016 amendment’s enacting clause unambiguously refers to original
judgments when it speaks of “judgments issued on and after July 1, 2015.” In this context, original
judgments are those that finally resolved the controversy and were entered on or after that date.
The ten-year renewal period does not apply to original judgments entered before July 1, 2015, even
if those judgments were renewed after that date.
       This reading is supported by our caselaw. While we have never squarely addressed the
proper reading of the 2016 amendment’s enacting clause, at least two of our cases have done so
incidentally. In Alpha Mortgage Fund II v. Drinkard, the respondent argued that a judgment
entered in 2010 was ineligible for renewal because the appellant-judgment creditor failed to record
the judgment. </pre><span class="citation no-link">169 Idaho 446</span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2625158"><a href="/opinion/2625158/yaekle-v-andrews/" aria-description="Citation for case: Yaekle v. Andrews">169 P.3d 200</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2021). This Court rejected this argument and applied
section 10-1111(1)’s plain language with ease:
               Based on the statutory language in effect when this case commenced in
       2010, the motion to renew must have been made within five years. The district court
       entered the Original Judgment on February 23, 2010. The Original Judgment was
       renewed on February 12, 2015. Alpha’s motion to renew was filed on February 10,
       2020, less than five years after the 2015 Judgment was last renewed. Consequently,
       the district court did not err when it granted Alpha’s motion to renew the Original
       Judgment.
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="2625158"><a href="/opinion/2625158/yaekle-v-andrews/" aria-description="Citation for case: Yaekle v. Andrews">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> at 452–53, 497 P.3d at 206–07. Similarly, in Smith, the appellant argued that he was not barred
by res judicata from challenging the respondent’s renewal of a 1991 judgment. In holding that res
judicata did preclude the appellant’s challenge to the renewal, this Court explained the effect of
the 2016 amendment:
       Idaho Code section 10-1111 was amended in 2016 to extend the renewal of a
       judgment lien to ten years, as opposed to five years, as was in place at the time the
       judgments were entered in this case. S.B. 1362, S.L. 2016, ch. 269, § 1. The ten-
       year renewal time applies only to judgments issued on and after July 1,
       2015. Id. Thus, the judgments in this case continue to be subject to a five-year
       renewal period.
164 Idaho at 48 n.1, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4283661"><a href="/opinion/4506408/smith-v-smith/" aria-description="Citation for case: Smith v. Smith">423 P.3d at 1000</a></span><pre class="inline"> n.1. To be sure, this Court’s observations in both Alpha
Mortgage and Smith regarding the application of section 10-1111(1) after the 2016 amendment
were “not necessary to decide the issue[s] presented” and likely amount to dicta. State v. Garcia,
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="7803519"><a href="/opinion/7859653/state-v-garcia/#714" aria-description="Citation for case: State v. Garcia">170 Idaho 708, 714</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7803519"><a href="/opinion/7859653/state-v-garcia/#584" aria-description="Citation for case: State v. Garcia">516 P.3d 578, 584</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2022) (citation omitted). Nonetheless, both cases provide
persuasive support for the conclusion that the 2016 amendment’s ten-year renewal period applies
only to original judgments entered on and after July 1, 2015, as Pena contends.



                                                   6
       Moreover, even if the enacting clause were ambiguous, Pena’s interpretation is supported
by the public policy and legislative history underlying Idaho Code section 10‑1111. See Doe I v.
Boy Scouts of Am., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5174758"><a href="/opinion/5342126/doe-v-boy-scouts-of-america/#430" aria-description="Citation for case: Doe v. Boy Scouts of America">148 Idaho 427, 430</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5174758"><a href="/opinion/5342126/doe-v-boy-scouts-of-america/#497" aria-description="Citation for case: Doe v. Boy Scouts of America">224 P.3d 494, 497</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009). The 2018 amendment replaced
section 10-1111(1)’s references to “renewed judgment” with “order renewing judgment.” Act of
Mar. 26, 2018, ch. 284, § 1, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2018 Idaho Sess. Laws 672</span><pre class="inline">, 672. As the Statement of Purpose explains,
this amendment aimed to “clarif[y] that the entry of an order renewing judgment which is recorded
in the same manner as the original judgment maintains the original judgment’s date . . . .”
Statement of Purpose, S.B. 1225, supra (emphasis added). This clarification accords with Idaho’s
longstanding rule that judgment priority is fixed by the date of recording the original judgment,
and that subsequent judgment creditors obtain priority only by recording first. See Golub v.
Kirk‑Hughes Dev., LLC, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3133261"><a href="/opinion/3133261/alan-golub-v-kirk-hughes-development/" aria-description="Citation for case: Alan Golub v. Kirk-Hughes Development">158 Idaho 73</a></span><pre class="inline">, 76–77, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3133261"><a href="/opinion/3133261/alan-golub-v-kirk-hughes-development/" aria-description="Citation for case: Alan Golub v. Kirk-Hughes Development">343 P.3d 1080</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1083–84 (2015). Thus, the
legislature intended that an order renewing judgment would not cause a new judgment to “issue”
or create a new priority date but instead continue the original judgment’s priority.
       In sum, the 2016 amendment unambiguously provides that its ten-year renewal period
applies to original judgments—those that finally concluded a lawsuit—entered on or after July 1,
2015. Judgments issued prior to that date will remain subject to Idaho Code section 10-1111(1)’s
prior five-year renewal period. That is so even if an original judgment entered prior to July 1, 2015,
was subsequently renewed after that date, as was Cheung’s judgment. Consequently, we conclude
that the district court correctly applied the five-year renewal period to Cheung’s 2006 judgment.
And it correctly determined that Cheung’s judgment expired on December 11, 2021—five years
after its most recent renewal on December 12, 2016—rendering it unenforceable.
   B. The district court correctly concluded that Senate Bill 1362’s ten-year renewal period
      does not govern Cheung’s judgment by virtue of its amendment in 2016 pursuant to
      Rule 60(a).
       In the alternative, Cheung relies on the district court’s entry of an amended judgment in
2016 to obtain the more favorable ten-year renewal period provided by Senate Bill 1362. The
district court found this argument unavailing as well. It reasoned that the 2016 amended judgment
simply ensured that Cheung’s judgment reflected the amount indicated in this Court’s remittitur
following Pena’s appeal from the order of the Commission. By the district court’s reading, its 2016
amended judgment did not disturb the original judgment’s finality or materially alter the rights and
obligations of the parties. Thus, in its view, there is no basis to conclude that the amendment to



                                                  7
the judgment effected an “issuance” of the judgment that would render the 2016 legislative
amendment’s ten‑year renewal period applicable.
        Cheung views the matter differently. She reasons that the district court’s 2016 amended
judgment “substantively changed the legal obligations of the parties” by reducing the amount of
the judgment by some $11,000. Because the amendment produced this purportedly substantive
effect, Cheung contends it constituted “a new judgment within the meaning of Idaho Code
[section] 10-1111.” And since that new judgment “issued” in 2016—after July 1, 2015—then, in
her view, it is governed by the 2016 amendment’s ten-year renewal period.
        The relevant inquiry here concerns the applicability of Rule 60(a) of the Idaho Rules of
Civil Procedure. Rule 60(a) permits a trial court to “correct a clerical mistake or a mistake arising
from oversight or omission whenever one is found in a judgment, order, or other part of the record.”
I.R.C.P. 60(a). We have described clerical errors as those “in which the type of mistake or omission
is mechanical in nature, which is apparent in the record and which does not involve a legal decision
or judgment by an attorney.” Silsby v. Kepner, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2597461"><a href="/opinion/2597461/silsby-v-kepner/#411" aria-description="Citation for case: Silsby v. Kepner">140 Idaho 410, 411</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2597461"><a href="/opinion/2597461/silsby-v-kepner/#29" aria-description="Citation for case: Silsby v. Kepner">95 P.3d 28, 29</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2004) (citation
modified) (quoting Dursteler v. Dursteler, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1244094"><a href="/opinion/1244094/dursteler-v-dursteler/#597" aria-description="Citation for case: Dursteler v. Dursteler">112 Idaho 594, 597</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1244094"><a href="/opinion/1244094/dursteler-v-dursteler/#818" aria-description="Citation for case: Dursteler v. Dursteler">733 P.2d 815, 818</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Ct. App.
1987)). It follows that the trial court may correct these mistakes on its own initiative, as the district
court did here. See I.R.C.P. 60(a).
        By contrast, judicial errors involve “instances where the court changes its mind, either
because it made a legal or factual mistake in making its original determination, or because on
second thought it has decided to exercise its discretion in a manner different from the way it was
exercised in the original determination.” Silsby, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2597461"><a href="/opinion/2597461/silsby-v-kepner/#412" aria-description="Citation for case: Silsby v. Kepner">140 Idaho at 412</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2597461"><a href="/opinion/2597461/silsby-v-kepner/#30" aria-description="Citation for case: Silsby v. Kepner">95 P.3d at 30</a></span><pre class="inline"> (emphasis omitted)
(quoting Blanton v. Anzalone, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="484923"><a href="/opinion/484923/blanton-v-anzalone/" aria-description="Citation for case: Blanton v. Anzalone">813 F.2d 1574</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1577 n.2 (9th Cir. 1987)). Judicial and other errors
“of a more substantial nature” are appropriately rectified by a motion brought under either Rule
59(e) or Rule 60(b). Id. at 411, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2597461"><a href="/opinion/2597461/silsby-v-kepner/" aria-description="Citation for case: Silsby v. Kepner">95 P.3d at 29</a></span><pre class="inline"> (quoting Dursteler, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1244094"><a href="/opinion/1244094/dursteler-v-dursteler/#597" aria-description="Citation for case: Dursteler v. Dursteler">112 Idaho at 597</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1244094"><a href="/opinion/1244094/dursteler-v-dursteler/#818" aria-description="Citation for case: Dursteler v. Dursteler">733 P.2d at
818</a></span><pre class="inline">); see also Bias v. State, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3156600"><a href="/opinion/3156600/mitchell-james-bias-v-state/#706" aria-description="Citation for case: Mitchell James Bias v. State">159 Idaho 696, 706</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3156600"><a href="/opinion/3156600/mitchell-james-bias-v-state/#1060" aria-description="Citation for case: Mitchell James Bias v. State">365 P.3d 1050, 1060</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Ct. App. 2015) (explaining
the difference between relief under Rule 59(e) and Rule 60(b)). Critically, Rules 59(e) and 60(b)
result in the vacatur of the original, erroneous judgment, erasing its conclusive effect on the
litigation and determination of the parties’ rights and obligations. See I.R.C.P. 59(e), 60(b); Andrus
v. Irick, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2619291"><a href="/opinion/2619291/andrus-v-irick/" aria-description="Citation for case: Andrus v. Irick">87 Idaho 471</a></span><pre class="inline">, 475–77, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2619291"><a href="/opinion/2619291/andrus-v-irick/" aria-description="Citation for case: Andrus v. Irick">394 P.2d 304</a></span><pre class="inline">, 305–07 (1964) (describing the effect of the district
court’s order granting Rule 60(b) relief on an original judgment).




                                                   8
        Here, when the district court entered the original judgment in 2006, it expressly stated that
it was acting pursuant to the opinion issued by this Court resolving Pena’s appeal. The opinion
ordered Pena to pay Cheung $155,641.26, reflecting attorney fees arising exclusively from Pena’s
representation in the worker’s compensation matter. Cheung v. Pena, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2633465"><a href="/opinion/2633465/cheung-v-pena/#36" aria-description="Citation for case: Cheung v. Pena">143 Idaho 30, 36</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2633465"><a href="/opinion/2633465/cheung-v-pena/#423" aria-description="Citation for case: Cheung v. Pena">137 P.3d
417, 423</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2006). Despite this, the district court’s 2006 judgment included approximately $11,000
in fees incurred outside the worker’s compensation matter, producing an incorrect judgment
amount of $166,896.23. In 2016, the district court corrected this error by entering an amended
judgment pursuant to Rule 60(a), revising the amount to conform to the remittitur. The district
court’s treatment of this as a clerical error was sound. The mistake is straightforward: the court
carried the wrong dollar figure from the remittitur into the judgment. That is precisely the kind of
mechanical error that Rule 60(a) exists to correct—it reflects no legal judgment or factual
determination by the court, only a failure to accurately record what this Court’s opinion required.
Cf. Dursteler, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1244094"><a href="/opinion/1244094/dursteler-v-dursteler/#598" aria-description="Citation for case: Dursteler v. Dursteler">112 Idaho at 598</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1244094"><a href="/opinion/1244094/dursteler-v-dursteler/#819" aria-description="Citation for case: Dursteler v. Dursteler">733 P.2d at 819</a></span><pre class="inline"> (affirming district court’s entry of amended
judgment pursuant to Rule 60(a) to include mandatory post-judgment interest it should have
included in original judgment).
        We acknowledge Cheung’s argument, that the error implicated “the substantive content
prescribing the rights and obligations of the parties,” is compelling. On its face, the amended
judgment reduced Pena’s obligation by nearly $11,000. Even so, our caselaw makes clear that
correcting clerical errors under Rule 60(a) may involve revisions to the judgment’s expression of
the parties’ rights and obligations without intruding on the territory reserved to Rules 59(e) and
60(b). See Donaldson v. Henry, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3409227"><a href="/opinion/3413867/donaldson-v-henry/#476" aria-description="Citation for case: Donaldson v. Henry">63 Idaho 467, 476</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3409227"><a href="/opinion/3413867/donaldson-v-henry/" aria-description="Citation for case: Donaldson v. Henry">121 P.2d 445</a></span><pre class="inline">, 448–49 (1941); Dursteler, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1244094"><a href="/opinion/1244094/dursteler-v-dursteler/#598" aria-description="Citation for case: Dursteler v. Dursteler">112
Idaho at 598</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1244094"><a href="/opinion/1244094/dursteler-v-dursteler/#819" aria-description="Citation for case: Dursteler v. Dursteler">733 P.2d at 819</a></span><pre class="inline">; accord, e.g., Baker v. Cts. at Bayshore I Condo. Ass’n, Inc., </pre><span class="citation no-link">279
So. 3d 799</span><pre class="inline">, 802 (Fla. Dist. Ct. App. 2019) (affirming correction of a foreclosure judgment that
incorrectly described the real property and treating the error as a scrivener’s error). These cases
instruct that a scrivener’s error, even when it affects the judgment’s statement of the parties’ rights
and obligations, does not displace the court’s actual disposition of the case as set out in this Court’s
opinion.
        In sum, the district court’s 2016 amended judgment corrected a clerical error. This Court
had expressly held that Pena must forfeit $155,641.26 in fees to Cheung. By entering judgment for
$166,896.23 instead, the district court simply failed to record the correct award—a scrivener’s
error properly rectified under Rule 60(a). Because the error was clerical rather than judicial, it did


                                                   9
not require vacating the original judgment under Rules 59(e) or 60(b). So, the 2016 amended
judgment did not disturb the original judgment’s finality or its governance over the parties’ rights
and obligations; it merely restated, accurately this time, what the original judgment should have
said. It was therefore not a judgment that independently “finally decided the controversy.”
Restatement (First) of Judgments § 1 (1942). The 2006 judgment remains the operative one.
       This conclusion controls the statutory question. Because the 2016 amendment did not
displace the original judgment, the 2006 judgment remains the one “issued” within the meaning
of Senate Bill 1362’s enacting clause. And since its date of issuance precedes July 1, 2015,
Cheung’s judgment remains subject to the five-year renewal period in effect at the time of its entry.
See I.C. § 10-1111(1) (2004). Accordingly, we conclude that Cheung’s judgment expired on
December 11, 2021, five years after its most recent renewal on December 12, 2016. Cheung’s
motion to renew the judgment in 2025 was therefore untimely.
                                         IV. CONCLUSION
       Based on the foregoing reasoning and authorities, we affirm the district court’s decision.
