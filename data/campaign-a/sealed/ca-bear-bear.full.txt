BRODY, Chief Justice.
       This partition action concerns real property located in Eagle, Idaho, owned by former
spouses Joshua Brent Bear (“Joshua”) and Kristie Ann Bear (“Kristie”). A Montana court
dissolved the Bears’ marriage and distributed the Eagle property as part of the property settlement
agreement incorporated into the divorce decree, awarding it to Joshua and Kristie as tenants in
common. Later, Kristie brought this partition action in Ada County district court to force a sale of
the property. In this appeal, we are asked to decide whether Idaho’s obligation to give full faith
and credit to the Bears’ Montana divorce decree precludes an Idaho court from judicially
partitioning the Eagle property because of a provision in the divorce decree giving the Montana
                                                 1
court exclusive jurisdiction over the “enforcement, modification, or interpretation” of the Bears’
property settlement agreement. We conclude that the Full Faith and Credit Clause of the United
States Constitution obliges us to honor the decree’s reservation of jurisdiction to the Montana
court. We therefore affirm the district court’s dismissal of Kristie’s partition action, albeit on
alternative grounds than those articulated by the district court.
                       I.      FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND
       Kristie and Joshua were married in 2007 in Coeur d’Alene, Idaho. The couple has three
children, who at the time of the Bears’ divorce, were thirteen, ten, and seven years of age. Kristie
brought a Petition for Dissolution (divorce) in Montana state court in December 2021. She averred
that the family had resided in Montana for more than ninety days prior to the date of filing the
Petition. In the Petition, Kristie claimed that the marriage was “irretrievably broken.” The Bears
later mutually executed a Marital and Property Settlement Agreement (“Agreement”) and a
Parenting Plan, which Kristie submitted to the Montana court along with the Petition. The Bears
agreed to the merger of the Agreement into the Montana court’s decree of divorce:
                                  DECREE OF DISSOLUTION
               IT IS HEREBY ORDERED, ADJUDGED, AND DECREED:
               ....
               6. The issues of property and debt distribution and all other affairs of the
       marriage are determined pursuant to the [Agreement] filed herein.
               7. The [Agreement] thereto filed herein is incorporated into this decree by
       reference. The parties are ordered to perform and comply with the terms and
       conditions contained in the [Agreement]. Terms of the [Agreement] are enforceable
       by all remedies available for enforcement of a judgment, including contempt, and
       are enforceable as contract terms.
Relevant to this case, the Agreement distributed the Bears’ community real property as follows:
               1. The following real property shall be immediately listed for sale and sold:
                   2815 Rustler Drive, Missoula, Montana
                   202 Shoreline Drive, #3, Polson, Montana
               Upon sale closing the net sale proceeds shall be divided equally between
       the parties after payment of . . . mortgage, any sales commission, and taxes.
            The following real property shall be immediately titled as tenant’s [sic] in
       common, each with an undivided ½ interest, and shall be sold upon the mutual

                                                  2
        agreement of the parties with the net sale proceeds divided equally between the
        parties after payment of any sales commissions mortgage or other obligations and
        taxes:
                    724 Lone Brook, Eagle, Idaho
Furthermore, the Agreement contains an “enforcement” clause providing that the Montana court
retains exclusive jurisdiction over subsequent actions arising from its terms:
               The parties hereby designate the Montana Fourth Judicial District Court,
        Missoula County, as the court having exclusive jurisdiction of the enforcement,
        modification, or interpretation of this [A]greement.
        During the divorce proceedings, Kristie relocated from Montana to Idaho. One month after
the decree issued, Joshua quitclaimed a one‑half interest in the Eagle residence to her. That same
day, he emailed Kristie inviting her to live separately with the children in the Eagle residence and
stated that he intended to live there with the children if she declined his offer. Kristie appears not
to have responded to the offer. Joshua later confirmed that he would move into the Eagle residence
after the lessees vacated the property at the end of August 2022. Kristie, however, understood the
Agreement to require Joshua to sell the Eagle residence once the lease expired.
        Kristie later brought a personal injury action in district court in Ada County against Joshua
for acts of violence that she alleged he committed during the marriage. Kristie also filed a petition
in Ada County to modify the parenting plan adopted by the Montana court in the divorce decree.
Kristie subsequently brought this partition action. In the complaint, she alleges that Joshua has
excluded her from the Eagle residence, despite their tenancy in common, and asks the court to
order the sale of the property and to facilitate the equitable distribution of the proceeds. She further
requests that the court “award[] her credit against the net sale proceeds of the Eagle Property for
50% fair market rent that she has not received from [Joshua].”
        Kristie moved for partial summary judgment, arguing that Joshua was unable to
demonstrate the existence of an issue of material fact as to either her right to partition the property
as a tenant in common or as to whether “the Eagle [residence] cannot be physically partitioned
without great prejudice to the owners.” In opposition, Joshua argued that the district court lacked
jurisdiction to adjudicate the partition action by virtue of the Agreement’s enforcement clause,
which was adopted and incorporated into the Montana court’s divorce decree. He further argued
that the Agreement reflects Kristie’s waiver of the right to seek partition of the Eagle residence
because it provides that the residence must be sold on “mutual agreement of the parties.” Thus,
Joshua asked the district court to grant him summary judgment as the non-moving party.

       The district court held a hearing to consider Kristie’s motion for summary judgment. After
hearing the parties’ arguments, the district court denied Kristie’s motion and ruled that it lacked
subject matter jurisdiction to partition the Eagle residence. More precisely, the district court
reasoned that the Montana court retained exclusive jurisdiction over the “enforcement,
modification, or interpretation” of the Agreement, precluding an Idaho court from partitioning
property subject to its terms:
       But I don’t think that I get to decide those issues because, I agree, that this is a case
       where the Montana [c]ourt reserved exclusive jurisdiction. Were I to compel the
       sale of this property and then make a determination as to how the assets are divided
       depending upon who made what payments and who was entitled to credit for
       mortgage payments or use of the property, I think at that point I am clearly invading
       the purview of the original [c]ourt.

               [The Montana court] reserved, as I suggested, to itself enforcement,
       modification, or interpretation of the agreement. And I think that the issue before
       the [c]ourt is a request to interpret the language of that agreement, to the extent they
       can, one party to force the sale of the property in a particular period of time. I think
       that’s consistent with the Olsen v. Olsen decision at </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9542916"><a href="/opinion/1161792/locey-v-farmers-insurance-co-of-idaho/" aria-description="Citation for case: Locey v. Farmers Insurance Co. of Idaho">115 Idaho 25</a></span><pre class="inline">.
The district court granted summary judgment in favor of Joshua and dismissed the action without
prejudice. Applying the same reasoning, the district court denied Kristie’s motion for
reconsideration. Kristie timely appealed.
                                  II.       STANDARD OF REVIEW
       This Court reviews a lower court’s decision on summary judgment under the same standard
applied by the lower court. Brewer v. Wash. RSA No. 8 Ltd. P’ship, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9506729"><a href="/opinion/874447/brewer-v-washington-rsa-no-8-ltd-partnership/#738" aria-description="Citation for case: Brewer v. Washington RSA No. 8 Ltd. Partnership">145 Idaho 735, 738</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9506729"><a href="/opinion/874447/brewer-v-washington-rsa-no-8-ltd-partnership/#863" aria-description="Citation for case: Brewer v. Washington RSA No. 8 Ltd. Partnership">184 P.3d
860, 863</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2008) (citing Kolln v. Saint Luke’s Reg’l Med. Ctr., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9547326"><a href="/opinion/1172713/kolln-v-saint-lukes-regional-medical-center/#327" aria-description="Citation for case: Kolln v. Saint Luke&#x27;s Regional Medical Center">130 Idaho 323, 327</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9547326"><a href="/opinion/1172713/kolln-v-saint-lukes-regional-medical-center/#1146" aria-description="Citation for case: Kolln v. Saint Luke&#x27;s Regional Medical Center">940 P.2d 1142,
1146</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1997)). To prevail on a summary judgment motion, the movant must “show[] that there is
no genuine dispute as to any material fact” such that they are “entitled to judgment as a matter of
law.” I.R.C.P. 56(a). It follows that we review questions of law at the summary judgment stage
freely, drawing our own conclusions from the evidence. Callies v. O’Neal, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="874356"><a href="/opinion/874356/callies-v-oneal/#846" aria-description="Citation for case: Callies v. O&#x27;NEAL">147 Idaho 841, 846</a></span><pre class="inline">,
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="874356"><a href="/opinion/874356/callies-v-oneal/#135" aria-description="Citation for case: Callies v. O&#x27;NEAL">216 P.3d 130, 135</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009) (quoting Lettunich v. Key Bank Nat’l Ass’n, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2520409"><a href="/opinion/2520409/lettunich-v-key-bank-national-assn/#366" aria-description="Citation for case: Lettunich v. Key Bank National Ass&#x27;n">141 Idaho 362, 366</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2520409"><a href="/opinion/2520409/lettunich-v-key-bank-national-assn/#1108" aria-description="Citation for case: Lettunich v. Key Bank National Ass&#x27;n">109
P.3d 1104, 1108</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2005)). The application of the Full Faith and Credit Clause of the United States
Constitution is one such question of law subject to free review. See Markin v. Grohmann, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="880181"><a href="/opinion/880181/ron-markin-v-thomas-wolff-grohmann/#227" aria-description="Citation for case: Ron Markin v. Thomas Wolff Grohmann">153
Idaho 223, 227</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="880181"><a href="/opinion/880181/ron-markin-v-thomas-wolff-grohmann/#730" aria-description="Citation for case: Ron Markin v. Thomas Wolff Grohmann">280 P.3d 726, 730</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2012).




                                                  4
                                         III.    ANALYSIS
   A. The district court erred by concluding that it lacked subject matter jurisdiction over
      Kristie’s partition action, but its error was harmless because the partition action was
      properly dismissed based on full faith and credit principles.
       The district court determined that the enforcement clause of the Agreement deprived it of
subject matter jurisdiction to partition the Eagle residence, given that the Agreement defines the
terms of the Bears’ possession of that property and reserves to the Montana court exclusive
jurisdiction over any action to enforce, modify, or interpret those terms. Kristie insists this was
error. She argues that subject matter jurisdiction “does not turn on” language in a foreign divorce
decree; rather, “Idaho district courts unequivocally have subject matter jurisdiction over partition
cases involving Idaho real estate.” Relying on this Court’s decision in Olsen v. Olsen, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5173593"><a href="/opinion/5341105/olsen-v-olsen/" aria-description="Citation for case: Olsen v. Olsen">115 Idaho
105</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5173593"><a href="/opinion/5341105/olsen-v-olsen/" aria-description="Citation for case: Olsen v. Olsen">765 P.2d 130</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1988), Joshua counters that the district court lacked jurisdiction to entertain a
partition action because doing so would constitute an impermissible collateral attack on the
Montana divorce decree and the Agreement it incorporated. We agree with Kristie that the
Agreement does not deprive an Idaho court of subject matter jurisdiction over a partition action.
Nevertheless, we affirm the district court’s dismissal because the Agreement governs the parties’
respective rights in the Eagle residence and giving the Agreement full faith and credit necessarily
precludes the relief Kristie seeks through this partition action.
       The Idaho Constitution vests the “judicial power” in the Supreme Court and the district
courts, among others. Idaho Const. art. V, § 2. And it specifically gives Idaho’s district courts
“original jurisdiction in all cases, both at law and in equity.” Id. art. V, § 20 (emphasis added).
This language is broad; it reflects the fact that state courts, unlike our federal counterparts, are
courts of general jurisdiction. Allen v. Campbell, </pre><span class="citation no-link">169 Idaho 613</span><pre class="inline">, 618, </pre><span class="citation no-link">499 P.3d 1103</span><pre class="inline">, 1108 (2021);
McCormick v. Smith, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5170300"><a href="/opinion/5338422/mccormick-v-smith/#494" aria-description="Citation for case: McCormick v. Smith">23 Idaho 487, 494</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5170300"><a href="/opinion/5338422/mccormick-v-smith/#1001" aria-description="Citation for case: McCormick v. Smith">130 P. 999, 1001</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1913). Consequently, there will be few
occasions where an Idaho district court lacks subject matter jurisdiction; “the only limitation likely
. . . to arise with any frequency is preemption by federal law.” Allen, 169 Idaho at 618, 499 P.3d
at 1108.
       Idaho’s district courts possess subject matter jurisdiction to adjudicate partition actions. To
begin, a partition action is one sounding in equity, and it therefore falls within the jurisdiction
conferred on district courts by Article V, section 20. Nordgaarden v. Kiebert, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9637439"><a href="/opinion/9421744/nordgaarden-v-kiebert/#889" aria-description="Citation for case: Nordgaarden v. Kiebert">171 Idaho 883, 889,
892</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9637439"><a href="/opinion/9421744/nordgaarden-v-kiebert/#492" aria-description="Citation for case: Nordgaarden v. Kiebert">527 P.3d 486, 492, 495</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2022) (citation omitted). Furthermore, partition is a cause of action
expressly recognized by Idaho Code section 6-501. Thus, these “statutory and constitutional
                                                  5
provisions, when taken together, demonstrate that the district court, as a court of general
jurisdiction, had the authority to hear this matter when filed because it was in fact dealing with
the partition of real property under the authority granted by Idaho Code section 6-501.” Troupis v.
Summer, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="874326"><a href="/opinion/874326/troupis-v-summer/#80" aria-description="Citation for case: Troupis v. Summer">148 Idaho 77, 80</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="874326"><a href="/opinion/874326/troupis-v-summer/#1141" aria-description="Citation for case: Troupis v. Summer">218 P.3d 1138, 1141</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009).
       Subject matter jurisdiction does not disappear even when other principles of law prevent
the court from considering the case at hand. In Olsen, Mr. Olsen brought a partition action “seeking
to obtain the value of his interest in” real property he owned with his ex-wife as joint tenants. 115
Idaho at 106, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5173593"><a href="/opinion/5341105/olsen-v-olsen/#131" aria-description="Citation for case: Olsen v. Olsen">765 P.2d at 131</a></span><pre class="inline">. He sought to partition the property despite language in the divorce
decree awarding Ms. Olsen possession of the property until she remarried or “removed herself
from the residence.” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5173593"><a href="/opinion/5341105/olsen-v-olsen/" aria-description="Citation for case: Olsen v. Olsen">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> at 105–06, 765 P.2d at 130–31. The district court rejected Ms. Olsen’s
contention that the parties’ rights in the property were res judicata as a result of the divorce decree
and ordered her to buy out Mr. Olsen’s interest in the property. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5173593"><a href="/opinion/5341105/olsen-v-olsen/#106" aria-description="Citation for case: Olsen v. Olsen">Id. at 106</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5173593"><a href="/opinion/5341105/olsen-v-olsen/#131" aria-description="Citation for case: Olsen v. Olsen">765 P.2d at 131</a></span><pre class="inline">. This
Court reversed, holding that the district court “had no jurisdiction to consider anew a sale of the
residence” given that “[t]he divorce decree was a valid and final personal judgment and
extinguished Mr. Olsen’s claim for the sale of the residence, except as provided in the decree.” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5173593"><a href="/opinion/5341105/olsen-v-olsen/#107" aria-description="Citation for case: Olsen v. Olsen">Id.
at 107</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5173593"><a href="/opinion/5341105/olsen-v-olsen/#132" aria-description="Citation for case: Olsen v. Olsen">765 P.2d at 132</a></span><pre class="inline">.
       Unfortunately, Olsen confuses the term “jurisdiction” with the preclusive effect of a final
judgment. See </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5173593"><a href="/opinion/5341105/olsen-v-olsen/" aria-description="Citation for case: Olsen v. Olsen">id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> This confusion is not new; on several occasions we have wrestled with the
“imprecise use of the term ‘jurisdiction.’” State v. Brown, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6115750"><a href="/opinion/6248554/state-v-brown/#443" aria-description="Citation for case: State v. Brown">170 Idaho 439, 443</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6115750"><a href="/opinion/6248554/state-v-brown/#863" aria-description="Citation for case: State v. Brown">511 P.3d 859, 863</a></span><pre class="inline">
(2022); see also State v. Moore, </pre><span class="citation no-link">175 Idaho 906</span><pre class="inline">, 908, </pre><span class="citation no-link">572 P.3d 204</span><pre class="inline">, 206 (2025) (per curiam) (“This
Court has repeatedly noted that phrases like ‘the court lacks jurisdiction’ have been used
inconsistently, sometimes precisely and sometimes imprecisely, by both courts and parties.’”
(citations omitted)). While Olsen held that the district court had no “jurisdiction” to reconsider the
distribution of the Olsen’s community property, 115 Idaho at 107, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5173593"><a href="/opinion/5341105/olsen-v-olsen/#132" aria-description="Citation for case: Olsen v. Olsen">765 P.2d at 132</a></span><pre class="inline">, the true
meaning of its holding, as Justice Bistline notes in a concurring opinion, is that, when the divorce
decree “became wholly final, . . . it was not subject to modification in that action or in any other
action,” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5173593"><a href="/opinion/5341105/olsen-v-olsen/#108" aria-description="Citation for case: Olsen v. Olsen">id. at 108</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5173593"><a href="/opinion/5341105/olsen-v-olsen/#133" aria-description="Citation for case: Olsen v. Olsen">765 P.2d at 133</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Bistline, J., concurring). In other words, the district court was
precluded from modifying the final divorce decree in a collateral partition action, even if, as a
general matter, district courts have subject matter jurisdiction to partition real property.
       Joshua’s argument suffers from the same flaw. He insists that the Montana divorce decree
deprived the district court of jurisdiction to entertain Kristie’s partition action. The district court

                                                  6
erred in accepting this argument. Article V, section 20 of the Idaho Constitution and Idaho Code
section 6-501 confer enduring subject matter jurisdiction on Idaho’s district courts to adjudicate
partition actions. That grant of jurisdiction, however, does not answer whether a court may grant
relief in a particular case. Here, the relief Kristie seeks is precluded by a prior judgment on the
merits between the same parties. See D.A.R., Inc. v. Sheffer, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1195309"><a href="/opinion/1195309/dar-inc-v-sheffer/#144" aria-description="Citation for case: D.A.R., Inc. v. Sheffer">134 Idaho 141, 144</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1195309"><a href="/opinion/1195309/dar-inc-v-sheffer/#605" aria-description="Citation for case: D.A.R., Inc. v. Sheffer">997 P.2d 602,
605</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2000).
       The district court’s error on this score is ultimately harmless because full faith and credit
principles required it to dismiss Kristie’s partition action. The divorce decree provides that the
Agreement is “incorporated into th[e] decree by reference” and that the “issues of property and
debt distribution . . . are [to be] determined pursuant to” that Agreement. The Agreement, in turn,
designates the Montana court “as the court having exclusive jurisdiction of the enforcement,
modification, or interpretation of this [A]greement.” By virtue of its incorporation into the divorce
decree—a final judgment of a sister state entitled to full faith and credit under the Constitution—
we are required to honor the reservation of any dispute involving “enforcement, modification, or
interpretation” to the Montana court.
       Kristie contests this conclusion. She urges us to read the Agreement merely as a contract
rather than as the terms of a sister‑state judgment. Under her reading, the Agreement’s
“enforcement” provision operates as a forum‑selection clause, which she contends is
unenforceable in Idaho. And, in any event, Kristie argues that judicial partition of the Eagle
residence has nothing to do with the Bears’ divorce. Because she seeks “a partition of her separate
property under general property laws that would govern any tenant‑in‑common dispute,” she
reasons that partition of the Eagle residence does not “involve the enforcement, modification, or
interpretation of the [Agreement].” In other words, Kristie frames this partition action as an effort
to enforce her rights as the possessor of real property in Idaho rather than any rights created by the
Agreement.
       Joshua counters that the Agreement is not a contract, but rather a final judgment. In his
view, “[a]n Idaho partition order compelling sale” would run counter to the terms of the Montana
divorce decree and would thus impermissibly “alter a judgment term.” Like the district court,
Joshua casts the problem as one of subject matter jurisdiction. In truth, however, the limitation he
identifies arises from the Full Faith and Credit Clause, which requires Idaho courts to honor the
terms of the Montana divorce decree. We thus begin by explaining why principles of full faith and

                                                  7
credit govern this dispute. We then turn to the language of the enforcement provision and conclude
that Kristie’s partition action would necessarily require an Idaho court to interpret, enforce, or
modify its distribution of the Bears’ marital real property—functions expressly reserved to the
Montana court.
       The Full Faith and Credit Clause of the United States Constitution provides that “Full Faith
and Credit shall be given in each State to the public Acts, Records, and judicial Proceedings of
every other State.” U.S. Const art. IV, § 1. As Chief Justice Marshall explained, the Clause
guarantees “that the judgment of a state court should have the same credit, validity and effect, in
every other court of the United States, which it had in the state where it was pronounced.” Hampton
v. McConnel, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="85230"><a href="/opinion/85230/hampton-v-mconnel/#235" aria-description="Citation for case: Hampton v. M&#x27;connel">16 U.S. (3 Wheat.) 234, 235</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1818). We have held that a sister-state judgment is
entitled to full faith and credit in Idaho when (1) the rendering court properly exercised personal
and subject-matter jurisdiction consistent with due process and its own state-law requirements; (2)
the judgment is final; and (3) it was decided on the merits. Andre v. Morrow, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9584070"><a href="/opinion/1251811/andre-v-morrow/#460" aria-description="Citation for case: Andre v. Morrow">106 Idaho 455, 460</a></span><pre class="inline">,
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="9584070"><a href="/opinion/1251811/andre-v-morrow/#1360" aria-description="Citation for case: Andre v. Morrow">680 P.2d 1355, 1360</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1984).
       When these elements are satisfied, foreign state judgments are essentially given res judicata
effect in Idaho courts, see Stewart E. Sterk, The Muddy Boundaries Between Res Judicata and Full
Faith and Credit, </pre><span class="citation no-link">58 Wash. &amp; Lee L. Rev. 47</span><pre class="inline">, 62 (2001), except as against allegations that the
sister-state court lacked jurisdiction in rendering the judgment, Andre, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9584070"><a href="/opinion/1251811/andre-v-morrow/#459" aria-description="Citation for case: Andre v. Morrow">106 Idaho at 459</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9584070"><a href="/opinion/1251811/andre-v-morrow/#1359" aria-description="Citation for case: Andre v. Morrow">680 P.2d
at 1359</a></span><pre class="inline">. Although the Clause’s command tends to limit “the status of the States as independent
sovereigns” by requiring submission to another State’s potentially “hostile policies,” it
simultaneously promotes “the practical operation of the federal system” by forbidding collateral
attacks on foreign judgments among state courts. Estin v. Estin, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9420188"><a href="/opinion/104568/estin-v-estin/#546" aria-description="Citation for case: Estin v. Estin">334 U.S. 541, 546</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1948); see
Fauntleroy v. Lum, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="96865"><a href="/opinion/96865/fauntleroy-v-lum/#238" aria-description="Citation for case: Fauntleroy v. Lum">210 U.S. 230, 238</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1908).
       A foreign divorce decree is a variety of judgment entitled to full faith and credit in Idaho
courts. See Beesley v. Beesley, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9622786"><a href="/opinion/1405968/beesley-v-beesley/#537" aria-description="Citation for case: Beesley v. Beesley">114 Idaho 536, 537</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9622786"><a href="/opinion/1405968/beesley-v-beesley/#696" aria-description="Citation for case: Beesley v. Beesley">758 P.2d 695, 696</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1988) (first citing U.S.
Const. art. IV, § 1; then citing Andre, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9584070"><a href="/opinion/1251811/andre-v-morrow/" aria-description="Citation for case: Andre v. Morrow">106 Idaho 455</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9584070"><a href="/opinion/1251811/andre-v-morrow/" aria-description="Citation for case: Andre v. Morrow">680 P.2d 1355</a></span><pre class="inline">; and then citing Mitchell v.
Pincock, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9572088"><a href="/opinion/1230487/mitchell-v-pincock/" aria-description="Citation for case: Mitchell v. Pincock">99 Idaho 56</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9572088"><a href="/opinion/1230487/mitchell-v-pincock/" aria-description="Citation for case: Mitchell v. Pincock">577 P.2d 343</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1978)). Here, the parties do not dispute that the Bears’ divorce
decree was a final judgment on the merits issued in the proper exercise of the Montana court’s
jurisdiction. But Kristie urges this Court to treat the Agreement the decree incorporates by
reference as a mere contract. Specifically, she contends that the Agreement’s enforcement clause
is nothing more than a contractual forum‑selection provision that should be given no effect by an

                                                 8
Idaho court pursuant to Idaho Code section 29-110(1). We are unpersuaded. By virtue of its
incorporation into the Montana court’s divorce decree, the Agreement’s enforcement clause is a
judgment term entitled to full faith and credit in Idaho. See Mont. Code Ann. § 40‑4‑201(4)(a); In
re Marriage of Lorge, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9507958"><a href="/opinion/878648/in-re-the-marriage-of-lorge/#117" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Marriage of Lorge">675 P.2d 115, 117</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Mont. 1984). Idaho courts are therefore obliged to respect
the Montana court’s reservation of “exclusive jurisdiction of the enforcement, modification, or
interpretation of th[e] [A]greement.”
        We conclude that Kristie’s attempt to partition the Eagle residence effectively asked the
Idaho district court to enforce, modify, or interpret the Agreement in contravention of its
enforcement clause. The Bears offer competing interpretations of the Agreement’s language
addressing their ownership of the Eagle residence. On the one hand, Kristie reads the Agreement
as establishing the Bears’ ownership of the Eagle residence under the default rules of tenancies in
common—principally, that the sale of the entire property requires the unanimous consent of the
co‑tenants. 20 Am. Jur. 2d Cotenancy &amp; Joint Ownership § 96; see Washburn-Wilson Seed Co. v.
Alexie, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3409659"><a href="/opinion/3414204/washburn-wilson-seed-co-v-alexie/#730" aria-description="Citation for case: Washburn-Wilson Seed Co. v. Alexie">54 Idaho 727, 730</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3409659"><a href="/opinion/3414204/washburn-wilson-seed-co-v-alexie/#991" aria-description="Citation for case: Washburn-Wilson Seed Co. v. Alexie">35 P.2d 990, 991</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1934). Yet those default rules, Kristie reasons, also
bestow on her the right to vindicate her individual ownership interest in the property through a
partition action brought in accordance with Idaho Code section 6-501. Under section 6-501, Kristie
may unilaterally effectuate the sale of the entire property “if it appears that a partition cannot be
made without great prejudice to the owners.” I.C. § 6-501. On this view, then, the phrase “upon
the mutual agreement of the parties” simply restates a baseline rule of property law but does not
limit Kristie’s right to partition the Eagle residence.
        On the other hand, Joshua understands the Agreement itself—not Idaho’s default rules—
as the source of the parties’ rights in the Eagle residence. In his view, the Agreement both created
their tenancy in common and established the conditions under which the property could be sold.
Thus, he interprets the phrase “upon the mutual agreement of the parties” not as a restatement of
property law, but as a negotiated limitation on the parties’ partition rights. Under that reading,
mutual agreement is a condition precedent to any sale of the property, foreclosing this partition
action in which Kristie explicitly asked the district court to force a sale of the Eagle residence over
Joshua’s objection.
        Because the partition action cannot be adjudicated without resolving this dispute, an Idaho
court would necessarily undertake the very interpretive task the Bears’ divorce decree reserves to
the Montana court. Moreover, undertaking that inquiry would risk giving the Montana judgment

                                                   9
less effect than it has in Montana—whether by misinterpreting its terms, by effectively reopening
or modifying a property disposition incorporated into the divorce decree, or even by correctly
interpreting the Agreement but doing so in a manner that amounts to enforcing its terms, a task the
Agreement likewise reserves exclusively to the Montana court. Full faith and credit does not
deprive Idaho courts of jurisdiction over partition actions; rather, it precludes courts from
exercising that jurisdiction in a way that would contravene a sister‑state judgment. In short, the
Montana court must determine the parties’ rights in the Eagle residence under the Agreement, and
full faith and credit requires the Ada County district court to respect that allocation of authority
when it ultimately partitions the property. Thus, we affirm the district court’s dismissal of Kristie’s
partition action.
        To summarize, the district court had jurisdiction over the partition action, but it may not
exercise that jurisdiction in a manner that contradicts the terms of the Bears’ Montana divorce
decree. The Full Faith and Credit Clause requires Idaho courts to give the Bears’ divorce decree—
and the Agreement it incorporates—the preclusive effect of a final judgment. Because Kristie’s
partition action necessarily seeks to enforce, modify, or interpret the Agreement, she must pursue
that relief in the Montana court before coming to an Idaho court to obtain an order partitioning the
property in accordance with the decree.
    B. We decline to award attorney fees to Joshua.
        As the prevailing party, Joshua requests an award of attorney fees on appeal. We decline
to make such an award. This Court may award reasonable attorney fees to the prevailing party if
it finds that the case was “brought, pursued, or defended frivolously, unreasonably or without
foundation.” I.C. § 12-121. A case does not meet section 12-121’s standard so long as it raises
“fairly debatable issues.” Evans v. Wright, </pre><span class="citation no-link">174 Idaho 330</span><pre class="inline">, 341, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="11199303"><a href="/opinion/10732718/evans-v-wright/#602" aria-description="Citation for case: Evans v. Wright">554 P.3d 591, 602</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2024).
However, when this Court determines that only specific issues raised on appeal are not fairly
debatable—rather than the appeal as a whole—it may apportion fees to reflect only those
unmeritorious claims. Idaho Mil. Hist. Soc’y, Inc. v. Maslen, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2678800"><a href="/opinion/2678800/idaho-military-historical-society-inc-v-maslen/#632" aria-description="Citation for case: Idaho Military Historical Society, Inc. v. Maslen">156 Idaho 624, 632</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2678800"><a href="/opinion/2678800/idaho-military-historical-society-inc-v-maslen/#1080" aria-description="Citation for case: Idaho Military Historical Society, Inc. v. Maslen">329 P.3d 1072,
1080</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2014).
        In the present case, the district court concluded that it lacked subject matter jurisdiction to
hear Kristie’s partition action. The district court erred in so concluding, and Kristie’s appeal
therefore raises a fairly debatable claim of error on that score, even if there are sound alternative
grounds for dismissal. Moreover, much of this appeal turns on whether the Bears’ property

                                                  10
settlement agreement is properly read as a foreign judgment or a contract. Because the Agreement
reflects qualities of both a judgment and a contract, its proper classification is fairly debatable;
both parties’ arguments are serious, legally reasonable, and founded in the law and factual record
of the case. We therefore decline to award attorney fees against Kristie.
                                      IV.      CONCLUSION
       Based on the foregoing reasoning and authorities, we affirm the district court’s order
granting summary judgment to Joshua and dismissing this action without prejudice. We decline to
make an award of attorney fees against Kristie. However, as the prevailing party, Joshua is entitled
to costs as a matter of course pursuant to Idaho Appellate Rule 40(a).
