   Papik, J.
   Under Nebraska law, holographic wills need not be wit-
nessed, but the signature, the material provisions, and, with
some limited exceptions, an indication of the date of sign-
ing, must be in the handwriting of the testator. But what if
a purported holographic will is a joint will and the material
provisions of that joint will are not in the handwriting of the
person who has died but in that of the other person making
the joint will? Does the joint will qualify as a valid holo-
graphic will of the decedent? This case presents that question.
As we will explain, the material provisions of such a will are
not in the handwriting of its testator and thus it is not a valid
holographic will.

                       BACKGROUND
Probate Proceedings.
   This appeal arises out of probate proceedings for the estate
of Douglas G. Todd. After Douglas died in January 2025, his
son Chadd B. Todd filed an application for informal probate of
a will Douglas executed in 1984. In the will, Douglas directed
that his sons would receive all his property.
   Later, however, Judy Sheets filed an objection to the appli-
cation for informal probate. Judy asserted that the 1984 will
was not Douglas’ last will. Instead, Judy claimed that Douglas
had executed a two-page holographic will in February 2021
that left his estate to her. Consistent with that claim, Judy
also filed a petition to have the purported holographic will
admitted for probate. She attached the purported holographic
will to her petition. We have reprinted a picture of those
pages here:

   The county court sustained Chadd’s motion for summary

of material fact and that the purported holographic will was
not valid as a matter of law for two reasons. First, the county
court concluded that the document was not a valid holographic
will because the material provisions of the purported will were
not in Douglas’ handwriting. Alternatively, the county court
concluded that the purported holographic will did not express
testamentary intent.

                  STANDARD OF REVIEW
   [1,2] An appellate court affirms a lower court’s grant of sum-
mary judgment if the pleadings and admitted evidence show
that there is no genuine issue as to any material facts or as to
the ultimate inferences that may be drawn from the facts and
that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.
Bar at the Yard v. Friends Family, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="11344378"><a href="/opinion/10876877/bar-at-the-yard-v-friends-family/" aria-description="Citation for case: Bar at the Yard v. Friends Family">321 Neb. 606</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation no-link">35 N.W.3d
813</span><pre class="inline"> (2026). An appellate court reviews the lower court’s grant
of summary judgment de novo, viewing the record in the light
most favorable to the nonmoving party and drawing all reason-
able inferences in that party’s favor. See </pre><span class="citation no-link">id.</span><pre class="inline">
   [3] Statutory interpretation presents a question of law that
an appellate court resolves independently of the trial court.
State v. Strawn, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="10924925"><a href="/opinion/10458337/state-v-strawn/" aria-description="Citation for case: State v. Strawn">318 Neb. 859</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation no-link">19 N.W.3d 761</span><pre class="inline"> (2025).
                            ANALYSIS
Judy’s Position: 2021 Document Is Valid
Holographic Will Even Though Material
Provisions Are in Her Handwriting.
   It is Judy’s position that the 2021 document was a valid will
of Douglas. Because Douglas executed a valid will in 2021,
Judy argues, it superseded his 1984 will and the county court
should have admitted it for probate.
   The 2021 document was not executed with all of the for-
malities ordinarily required of Nebraska wills. See </pre><span class="citation no-link">Neb. Rev.
Stat. § 30-2327</span><pre class="inline"> (Reissue 2016). At a minimum, the document
was not “signed by at least two individuals each of whom wit-
nessed either the signing or the testator’s acknowledgment of
the signature or of the will.” </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline"> Judy readily admits this but
argues that the 2021 document was a valid holographic will
pursuant to </pre><span class="citation no-link">Neb. Rev. Stat. § 30-2328</span><pre class="inline"> (Reissue 2016).

   Section 30-2328 recognizes an exception to the usual
requirement that wills bear the signature of two witnesses.
That statute provides, in relevant part: “An instrument which
purports to be testamentary in nature but does not comply
with [§ 30-2327] is valid as a holographic will, whether or
not witnessed, if the signature, the material provisions, and an
indication of the date of signing are in the handwriting of the
testator . . . .” § 30-2328.
   The county court concluded that the 2021 document did not
qualify as a valid holographic will under § 30-2328 because
its material provisions were not in Douglas’ handwriting.
Although Judy admits that the material provisions in the 2021
document were entirely in her handwriting, she argues that
the document still qualifies as a valid holographic will under
§ 30-2328. Judy points out that § 30-2328 requires that the sig-
nature and material provisions be in the handwriting of the
“testator.” Judy argues that she was a “testator” for purposes
of § 30-2328 because the Nebraska Probate Code generally
defines “[t]estator” as “the maker of a will,” </pre><span class="citation no-link">Neb. Rev. Stat.
§ 30-2209</span><pre class="inline">(49) (Reissue 2016), and she, along with Douglas,
were the makers of a joint will.
   Judy’s argument that she is a “testator” of the purported
holographic will requires us to discuss the nature of joint wills.
We do so now.
Joint Wills.
   The term “joint will” is used to refer to a testamentary
instrument executed by two or more parties in which the
parties set forth how their joint or separate property should
be disposed of upon death. See, Powell v. American Charter
Fed. Sav. &amp; Loan Assn., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2134859"><a href="/opinion/2134859/powell-v-american-charter-federal-savings-loan-assn/" aria-description="Citation for case: Powell v. mer. It is a misnomer because a joint will actually contains
two wills. As one court put it, “a joint will, is, in effect, the
separate will of each person signing it as [a] testator.” Olive v.
Biggs, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9561567"><a href="/opinion/1205431/olive-v-biggs/#455" aria-description="Citation for case: Olive v. 2021 Document Was Not Valid Holographic
Will of Douglas.
   Once it is understood that a joint will contains multiple,
separate wills and that each separate will therein is probated
independently upon the death of each testator, Judy’s argument
unravels. If Judy is correct that the 2021 document was a joint
will, then the document contained both her will and Douglas’
will. But even if wills for both Douglas and Judy were embod-
ied in the 2021 document, the only will of the two that could
be offered for probate in Douglas’ estate matter was Douglas’
will. At the risk of stating the obvious, it was Douglas who had
died, and it was Douglas’ property that was subject to disposi-
tion in the probate proceedings for his estate. Judy may have
been a testator of her separate will that was also embodied in

the 2021 document, but only Douglas could be the testator of
the will that Judy offered for probate.
   Because only Douglas could be the testator of the will Judy
offered in his probate proceedings, it did not qualify as a valid
holographic will. As discussed above, the material provisions
of holographic wills must be in the handwriting of the testator.
There is no dispute that Judy handwrote the material provisions
of the 2021 document. Thus, the 2021 document was not a
valid holographic will of Douglas.

Our Decision Is Consistent With Other
Courts and With Purpose of
Holographic Will Statutes.
   Although it appears relatively few courts have encountered
the exact question before us today and even fewer have exten-
sively discussed it, our decision is consistent with the weight
of existing authority. A handful of courts have concluded, or at
least suggested, that when the material provisions of a docu-
ment purporting to be a joint will are entirely in one person’s
handwriting, it is not a valid holographic will of the other
party to the purported joint will, even if the other party has
signed it.
   Judy cites one such case in her brief, In re Cole’s Will, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3675006"><a href="/opinion/3928372/in-re-will-of-cole/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Will of Cole">171
N.C. 74</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3675006"><a href="/opinion/3928372/in-re-will-of-cole/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Will of Cole">87 S.E. 962</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1916). In that case, a husband prepared
a document purporting to be a joint will of him and his wife.
With the exception of the wife’s signature, the document was
entirely in the husband’s handwriting. When the husband
died, the document was offered for probate as his holographic
will. The North Carolina Supreme Court concluded that the
document was correctly admitted for probate as a holographic
will of the husband but added that “this could not be done
in this case as to the wife, as it does not comply, and could
not comply, with the requirements of the statutory provisions
as to a holographic will, for two people could not write such
[a] will.” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3675006"><a href="/opinion/3928372/in-re-will-of-cole/#75" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Will of Cole">Id. at 75</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3675006"><a href="/opinion/3928372/in-re-will-of-cole/#963" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Will of Cole">87 S.E. at 963</a></span><pre class="inline">.
