Justice Jim Rice delivered the Opinion of the Court.

¶1    Claimant and Appellant YC Properties, LLC (YC Properties) appeals the August 19,

2025 Order on Motions for Summary Judgment entered by the Water Court of the State of

Montana, which granted the motions of Sharon Connolly (Connolly), Bernard J. Spaan

(Spaan), and Patrick O. Connell (Connell) (together, Objectors or Appellees), for summary

judgment. We address the following issues:

      1. Whether the Water Court erred by considering post-1973 conduct to make its
         determination of abandonment.

      2. Whether the Water Court erred by concluding unauthorized water use is the
         functional equivalent of an intent to abandon a water right.

¶2    We reverse and remand for further proceedings.

                 FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

¶3    Landowners Charles E. and Alma R. Vining (together the Vinings) filed Statement

of Claim 76H 147812-00 with the Montana Department of Natural Resources (DNRC) on

March 30, 1982. The statement of claim was for two groundwater wells on the Vinings’

property in Ravalli County for the stated purpose of “commercial beaver pens.” Notably,

the property owned by the Vinings also contained a pond.

¶4    In January 1994, and prior to the entry of a preliminary decree, a claims examiner

from the DNRC met with Alma Vining to discuss claim 76H 147812-00. After this

meeting, the DNRC generated an implied claim for the second well in the statement of

claim. This implied claim was identified as 76H 214439-00. Neither claim 76H 147812-

00 nor implied claim 76H 214439-00 included the pond on the property.



                                             2
¶5       Prior to the Vinings’ ownership, the property and claimed water rights were held by

Sam A. Downing (Downing). Downing’s declaration of vested water right was recorded

on December 31, 1963, which identified the wells and described their beneficial use as

“domestic, stock water, and irrigation.” These wells would become the sources of the two

claims at issue.

¶6       The Water Court issued the Preliminary Decree on January 14, 1998, which

included claims 76H 147812-00 and 76H 214439-00 in the West Subbasin of the Bitterroot

River 76HF (Basin 76HF). The Preliminary Decree recognized both of the Vinings’ claims

as beneficially used for commercial beaver pens. With respect to claim 76H 214439-00,

the Preliminary Decree described the flow rate as 100.00 gallons per minute (gpm) and the

volume as 161.30 acre-feet per year (AF)1 with a point of diversion in the SWNWSE of

Section 35, T6N, R21W, Ravalli County. With respect to claim 76H 147812-00, the

Preliminary Decree noted that the point of diversion was located in the SENWSE of

Section 35, T6N, R21W, Ravalli County and listed the flow rate as 100.00 gpm with the

volume of 162.22 AF. The Preliminary Decree referenced the “feasible volume” for claim

76H 147812-00 was limited to 161.74 AF.2 One individual objected to a potential volume

issue, but later withdrew his objection.



1
  Section 85-20-1101(2), MCA, defines acre-feet as “the amount of water necessary to cover 1
acre to a depth of 1 foot and is equivalent to 43,560 cubic feet of water.”
2
    An issue remark on the decree abstract for Claim 76H 147812-00 stated:

      THE CLAIMED VOLUME EXCEEDS MAXIMUM FEASIBLE VOLUME BASED
      ON THE FLOW RATE AND PERIOD OF USE, THE MAXIMUM VOLUME
      POSSIBLE IS 161.74 ACRE-FEET PER YEAR.
                                                3
¶7     To resolve the issue remark, the Water Court consolidated 76H 147812-00 into case

76HF-660. On January 28, 2005, Alma Vining filed an affidavit requesting that the volume

be reduced from 162.22 AF to 39.77 AF. A Master’s Report and an order adopting the

Master’s Report were entered in February of 2005, changing the volume to 39.77 AF.

¶8     Sometime in the 1990s, the Vinings stopped using the wells for beaver pens and

instead started using the water for livestock and irrigation purposes. In 1995, the Vinings

leased the property to a third party for the purpose of maintaining livestock. The Vinings

did not apply for nor receive a permit or authorization from the DNRC to change the use

of the appropriation rights from commercial beaver pens to irrigation and livestock. The

Vinings sold the property with both wells to YC Properties in 2020. The DNRC water

rights database thus lists YC Properties as the owner of both claim 76H 147812-00 and

claim 76H 214439-00.

¶9     On February 15, 2023, YC Properties filed a motion with the Water Court to amend

both claims, stating, “[t]he purpose of this motion is to add a historic groundwater pond

(0.68 surface acres and 10 feet deep, capacity of 2.72 AF) that is part of the historical use

of these wells[.]” The motion offered that water was historically pumped from the wells

to the pond and aerial photos taken in 1954 confirm the pond’s historic existence.

Accompanying the motion to amend was an affidavit from Alma Vining’s son, Robert

Vining, which stated the pond had existed for many years and had been used in the beaver

pen system. He further stated that “I remember that it would stay relatively full without

water from the wells, but water from the wells was needed to provide enough flow to supply

the down-gradient beaver pens.”

                                             4
¶10       Notice of the motion to amend the claims was published and, in response, Appellees

filed objections. The Water Court issued a scheduling order on December 7, 2023, and on

April 24, 2024, Objector Connell moved for a preliminary injunction seeking to prevent

“any and all uses of water” under 76H 148812-00 and 76H 214439-00 or from the pond,

alleging the claims had been abandoned. YC Properties moved to dismiss Connell from

the case, arguing he lacked standing to object. The Water Court issued an order denying

both pending motions. In the meantime, discovery was conducted and witness and exhibit

lists were exchanged.

¶11       Connell then moved for summary judgment, arguing that “YC Properties LLC water

rights 76H 147812-00 and 76H 214439-00 [were] abandoned under 85-2-404(2), MCA[.]”

YC Properties opposed the motion and filed a cross-motion for summary judgment. In

response to Connell’s motion, YC Properties argued that because no final decree had yet

been entered on Basin 76HF, the abandonment provisions of § 85-2-404(2), MCA, were

not applicable.3 In its own motion for summary judgment, YC Properties argued that “there

is no dispute as to the pre-1973 existence of YC’s storage pond and the undisputed facts

show that YC or its predecessors did not abandon its water rights.” Objectors Spaan and

Connolly also filed a joint motion requesting summary judgment, arguing there was no

genuine issue of material fact regarding whether YC Properties had abandoned its water

rights.



3
  Subsections (1) and (2) of the statute, addressing abandonment, “do not apply to existing
rights until they have been finally determined in accordance with part 2 of this chapter.”
Section 85-2-404(6), MCA.
                                              5
¶12       The Water Court issued its Order on Motions for Summary Judgment (Order) on

August 19, 2025.       The Water Court noted that Connell had incorrectly relied upon

§ 85-2-404, MCA, for his abandonment objection, but that the Water Court nonetheless

had jurisdiction to adjudicate the abandonment challenge under § 85-2-227, MCA, and the

common law. Under that authority, the Water Court found that YC Properties and its

predecessors had not used the water rights for thirty years because they ceased using the

water for commercial beaver pen operations in the mid-1990s, thus constituting “non-use”

of the water and triggering a rebuttable presumption of an intent to abandon the water

rights.    Rejecting YC Properties’ argument that its beneficial uses of irrigation and

livestock care could not constitute “non-use,” the Water Court reasoned that because YC

Properties and its predecessors did not file a statement of claim for either livestock or

irrigation use and did not apply for nor receive a permit or authorization from the DNRC

to change the purpose of the water rights from commercial use to stock or irrigation use,

any use of the water for those purposes was “unauthorized.” The Water Court reasoned

that, “when unauthorized water use occurs for a sufficiently long period of time, the

unauthorized use becomes the functional equivalent of intent to abandon a water right.”

(Emphasis added.) The Water Court stated that YC Properties had “offer[ed] no evidence

of commercial water use after the mid-1990s,” that is, for commercial beaver pens, and

therefore did not rebut the presumption that it intended to abandon its water rights. Thus,

the Water Court held that YC Properties had abandoned its water rights and granted

summary judgment in favor of Appellees, dismissing YC Properties’ water claims.

¶13       YC Properties appeals.

                                      DISCUSSION

¶16    1. Whether the Water Court erred in considering post-1973 conduct to make its
          determination of abandonment.

¶17    YC Properties contends the Water Court erred by considering its failure to obtain

authorization from the DNRC to change its water rights usage as evidence of abandonment

“because [the Water Court] lacked jurisdiction to adjudicate or enforce alleged post-1973

changes under the Montana Water Use Act” (MWUA). YC Properties argues the DNRC

holds the authority to adjudicate post-1973 changes to water rights, including whether YC

Properties was eligible to change the use of its rights from commercial beaver pens to


                                             7
irrigation and stock use pursuant to § 85-2-302, MCA. YC Properties thus contends the

Water Court impermissibly acted as the enforcing entity of the MWUA.

¶18    Appellees Connolly and Spaan answer that “YC improperly conflates DNRC[’]s

jurisdiction to enforce the MWUA to a prohibition against the Water Court’s reliance on

compliance with the Act as evidence of abandonment.” Citing § 85-2-227(2), MCA

(“[r]elevant evidence under this part may include admissible evidence arising before or

after July 1, 1973”), Connolly and Spaan argue that “[t]he Water Court is statutorily

authorized to consider all admissible evidence arising after July 1, 1973 in abandonment

cases.”

¶19    The Water Court has exclusive jurisdiction over matters concerning the

“determination and interpretation of existing water rights.” Section 3-7-501, MCA. An

“existing water right means a right to the use of water that would be protected under the

law as it existed prior to July 1, 1973.” Section 85-2-102(13), MCA; see also Heavirland

v. State, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1086503"><a href="/opinion/1086503/heavirland-v-state/#17" aria-description="Citation for case: Heavirland v. State">2013 MT 313, ¶¶ 17-27</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1086503"><a href="/opinion/1086503/heavirland-v-state/" aria-description="Citation for case: Heavirland v. State">372 Mont. 300</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1086503"><a href="/opinion/1086503/heavirland-v-state/" aria-description="Citation for case: Heavirland v. State">311 P.3d 813</a></span><pre class="inline"> (holding the protection of

existing water rights does not preclude applying post-1973 judicial precedent). “The

determination and interpretation of existing water rights includes, without limitation, the

adjudication of total or partial abandonment of existing water rights occurring at any time

before the entry of the final decree.” Section 3-7-501(4), MCA (emphasis added).

¶20    Importantly, whether a water right has been abandoned is a question of fact that

depends on “‘the evidence of the conduct, acts, and intent of the parties claiming the

usufruct of the water.’” Heavirland, ¶ 31 (quoting Power v. Switzer, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6639575"><a href="/opinion/6757014/power-v-switzer/#529" aria-description="Citation for case: Power v. Switzer">21 Mont. 523, 529</a></span><pre class="inline">,

</pre><span class="citation" data-id="6639575"><a href="/opinion/6757014/power-v-switzer/#34" aria-description="Citation for case: Power v. Switzer">55 P. 32, 34</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1898)). Thus, § 85-2-227(3), MCA, authorizes the Water Court to “determine

                                            8
all or part of an existing water right to be abandoned based on a consideration of all

admissible evidence that is relevant, including, without limitation, evidence relating to acts

or intent occurring in whole or in part after July 1, 1973.”

¶21    As the exclusive authority on the determination of existing water rights, the Water

Court was well within its jurisdiction when it entertained relevant evidence of post-1973

conduct in its consideration of whether the water claims 76H 147812-00 and 76H 214439-

00 had been abandoned. While YC Properties is correct to the extent that it argues the

Water Court is not, as a matter of law, authorized to decide whether YC Properties violated

the MWUA, the Water Court recognized this limitation, noting that “[t]he [c]ourt’s

jurisdiction limits its determination of [the] motion to amend based on the water rights as

they existed as of July 1, 1973. The authority to address post-1973 changes to a water right

is outside the Water Court’s jurisdiction, as is fashioning a remedy for alleged illegal water

use.” The Water Court stated that it “analyzes Connell’s argument under the authority of

§ 85-2-227, MCA[,]” and thereby properly exercised its exclusive jurisdiction pursuant to

§ 3-7-501(4), MCA.

¶22    In its task of determining whether YC Properties or the Vinings had abandoned

the claims, the Water Court was permitted to consider all the relevant facts.

Section 85-2-227(3), MCA. Without limitation, such record facts here included: the

statement of claim indicated the purpose of 76H 147812-00 and 76H 214439-00 as for

commercial beaver pens; the Vinings ceased using both claims for beaver pens in the mid-

1990s; the Vinings subsequently began using water pursuant to both claims for livestock

and irrigation; the Vinings did not file a statement of claim for livestock and irrigation

                                              9
purposes; and neither YC Properties nor the Vinings applied to the DNRC for the authority

to change the use from commercial beaver pens to irrigation and livestock. In other words,

the Water Court was permitted to admit evidence about post-1973 conduct into the factual

record and, pursuant to usual evidentiary rules and considerations, rely on that evidence to

determine the issue of abandonment.

¶23      Therefore, we hold the Water Court did not err by considering the post-1973

conduct of YC Properties and its predecessors in adjudicating the issue of abandonment.

¶24      2. Whether the Water Court erred by concluding unauthorized water use is the
            functional equivalent of an intent to abandon a water right.

¶25      Appellees ask that we affirm the Water Court’s order and hold that “sufficiently

long periods of unauthorized use, without change authorization, is the functional equivalent

of nonuse, triggering the rebuttable presumption that the unauthorized user intended to

abandon it[s] water rights.” Their argument relies heavily on the order entered by the Water

Court in In re Zier, No. 43N-3, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2016 Mont. Water LEXIS 5</span><pre class="inline"> (Mont. Water Ct. Nov. 22,

2016).

¶26      YC Properties contends the Water Court’s ruling sets a dangerous precedent that

“fundamentally expand[s] abandonment doctrine,” which will “create[] a chilling policy

where abandonment could be found when use and intent are present but complete

regulatory compliance is not.” YC Properties suggests that if courts can infer non-use from

these circumstances, “any technical noncompliance would silently morph into forfeiture,

even while water is actively being used[.]”




                                              10
¶27    For a water right to be abandoned, two concurrent elements must be present:

(1) non-use and (2) an intent to abandon. Twin Creeks Farm &amp; Ranch, LLC v. Petrolia

Irrigation Dist., </pre><span class="citation no-link">2020 MT 80</span><pre class="inline">, ¶ 14, </pre><span class="citation no-link">399 Mont. 431</span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation no-link">461 P.3d 91</span><pre class="inline"> (citation omitted).

Determining whether a water right has been abandoned “requires weighing all the relevant

factual circumstances of the case.” Heavirland, ¶ 32.

¶28    Consistent with the requisite elements, we have recognized that non-use of water

alone does not constitute abandonment. 79 Ranch v. Pitsch, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9507591"><a href="/opinion/878149/79-ranch-inc-v-pitsch/#432" aria-description="Citation for case: 79 Ranch, Inc. v. Pitsch">204 Mont. 426, 432</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9507591"><a href="/opinion/878149/79-ranch-inc-v-pitsch/#218" aria-description="Citation for case: 79 Ranch, Inc. v. Pitsch">666 P.2d

215, 218</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1983) (“In determining the abandonment of a water right, this Court has often

recognized the rule that mere nonuse of the water does not constitute abandonment.”).

Rather, it must also be proven that the claimant intended to abandon his or her water right.

79 Ranch, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9507591"><a href="/opinion/878149/79-ranch-inc-v-pitsch/#432" aria-description="Citation for case: 79 Ranch, Inc. v. Pitsch">204 Mont. at 432</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9507591"><a href="/opinion/878149/79-ranch-inc-v-pitsch/#218" aria-description="Citation for case: 79 Ranch, Inc. v. Pitsch">666 P.2d at 218</a></span><pre class="inline"> (“To prove abandonment, one has to prove

the other party intended to abandon his water right.”) (citations omitted). An intent to

abandon “need not be proved directly, but may be inferred from all the circumstances of

the case.” Skelton Ranch, Inc. v. Pondera Cnty. Canal &amp; Reservoir Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9800287"><a href="/opinion/2682629/skelton-ranch-inc-v-pondera-county-canal-reservoir-co/#52" aria-description="Citation for case: Skelton Ranch, Inc. v. Pondera County Canal &amp; Reservoir Co.">2014 MT 167,

¶ 52</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9800287"><a href="/opinion/2682629/skelton-ranch-inc-v-pondera-county-canal-reservoir-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Skelton Ranch, Inc. v. Pondera County Canal &amp; Reservoir Co.">375 Mont. 327</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9800287"><a href="/opinion/2682629/skelton-ranch-inc-v-pondera-county-canal-reservoir-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Skelton Ranch, Inc. v. Pondera County Canal &amp; Reservoir Co.">328 P.3d 644</a></span><pre class="inline"> (citation and internal quotations omitted). “The

circumstances must be such as to justify an inference of intention to abandon; in other

words, to leave the property to be taken by any other person who chooses to do so.”

Featherman v. Hennessy, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8021546"><a href="/opinion/8063731/featherman-v-hennessy/#540" aria-description="Citation for case: Featherman v. Hennessy">42 Mont. 535, 540-41</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8021546"><a href="/opinion/8063731/featherman-v-hennessy/#753" aria-description="Citation for case: Featherman v. Hennessy">113 P. 751, 753</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1911).

¶29    While not conclusive, non-use of water can be evidence of an intention to abandon

and give rise to a presumption of abandonment. 79 Ranch, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9507591"><a href="/opinion/878149/79-ranch-inc-v-pitsch/#432" aria-description="Citation for case: 79 Ranch, Inc. v. Pitsch">204 Mont. at 432</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9507591"><a href="/opinion/878149/79-ranch-inc-v-pitsch/#218" aria-description="Citation for case: 79 Ranch, Inc. v. Pitsch">666 P.2d at

218</a></span><pre class="inline">. Specifically, “a long period of continuous nonuse raises the rebuttable presumption

of an intention to abandon, and shifts the burden of proof onto the nonuser to explain the

                                            11
reasons for nonuse.” 79 Ranch, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9507591"><a href="/opinion/878149/79-ranch-inc-v-pitsch/#432" aria-description="Citation for case: 79 Ranch, Inc. v. Pitsch">204 Mont. at 432-33</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9507591"><a href="/opinion/878149/79-ranch-inc-v-pitsch/#218" aria-description="Citation for case: 79 Ranch, Inc. v. Pitsch">666 P.2d at 218</a></span><pre class="inline">. As the Water Court

noted, the period of non-use that is necessary to raise the rebuttable presumption has been

developed by case law. See Holmstrom Land Co. v. Meagher Cnty. Newlan Creek Water

Dist., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9507136"><a href="/opinion/876900/holmstrom-land-co-v-meagher-county-newlan-creek-water-district/#424" aria-description="Citation for case: Holmstrom Land Co. v. Meagher County Newlan Creek Water...">185 Mont. 409, 424</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9507136"><a href="/opinion/876900/holmstrom-land-co-v-meagher-county-newlan-creek-water-district/#1069" aria-description="Citation for case: Holmstrom Land Co. v. Meagher County Newlan Creek Water...">605 P.2d 1060, 1069</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1979) (stating 75 years of non-use is

“clear evidence” of abandonment); 79 Ranch, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9507591"><a href="/opinion/878149/79-ranch-inc-v-pitsch/#431" aria-description="Citation for case: 79 Ranch, Inc. v. Pitsch">204 Mont. at 431</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9507591"><a href="/opinion/878149/79-ranch-inc-v-pitsch/#217" aria-description="Citation for case: 79 Ranch, Inc. v. Pitsch">666 P.2d at 217</a></span><pre class="inline"> (stating

40 years of non-use “is strong evidence of an intent to abandon a water right, and, in effect,

raises a rebuttable presumption of abandonment”); In re Adjudication of Water Rights of

Clark Fork River, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="882481"><a href="/opinion/882481/in-re-the-adjudication-of-the-existing-water-rights-of-clark-fork-river/#16" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Adjudication of the Existing Water Rights of...">254 Mont. 11, 16</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="882481"><a href="/opinion/882481/in-re-the-adjudication-of-the-existing-water-rights-of-clark-fork-river/#1123" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re the Adjudication of the Existing Water Rights of...">833 P.2d 1120, 1123</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1992) (approximately 23 years

of non-use raised a rebuttable presumption of abandonment); Skelton Ranch, ¶ 56 (a period

of non-use ranging from 18 to 29 years was sufficient to raise a presumption of

abandonment); and Smith v. Hope Mining Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6639066"><a href="/opinion/6756527/smith-v-hope-mining-co/#438" aria-description="Citation for case: Smith v. Hope Mining Co.">18 Mont. 432, 438</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6639066"><a href="/opinion/6756527/smith-v-hope-mining-co/#634" aria-description="Citation for case: Smith v. Hope Mining Co.">45 P. 632, 634</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1896)

(stating 9 years of non-use is “certainly very potent evidence, if it stood alone, of an

intention to abandon”).

¶30    In an abandonment dispute, “[t]he objector bears the initial burden of showing a

long period of continuous non-use of the claimed water right.” Skelton Ranch, ¶ 53

(citation omitted). Once non-use has been factually established, the burden shifts to the

claimant to provide evidence explaining or excusing the prolonged non-use of the

particular water rights. 79 Ranch, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9507591"><a href="/opinion/878149/79-ranch-inc-v-pitsch/#432" aria-description="Citation for case: 79 Ranch, Inc. v. Pitsch">204 Mont. at 432-33</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9507591"><a href="/opinion/878149/79-ranch-inc-v-pitsch/#218" aria-description="Citation for case: 79 Ranch, Inc. v. Pitsch">666 P.2d at 218</a></span><pre class="inline"> (“In effect, such a

long period of continuous nonuse raises the rebuttable presumption of an intention to

abandon, and shifts the burden of proof onto the nonuser to explain the reasons for

¶31    YC Properties argued to the Water Court that because its water rights had been

continuously used for the recognized beneficial purposes of livestock care and irrigation,

the Appellees could not prove prolonged non-use, and it maintains this position on appeal,

arguing “Montana law is clear that changing the beneficial use of a right does not constitute

abandonment so long as the appropriator continues to put the water to a ‘beneficial use.’”

Connell answers that this Court should affirm the Water Court because “YC Properties,

and its predecessor property owners either disregarded, or ignored Montana Law[,]”

resulting in abandonment.       Connolly and Spaan answer that “compliance with the

[MWUA] is a requirement for continued beneficial use of water when determining

abandonment.” They proffer that “the Court has the opportunity to establish a clear rule

that a water user’s unauthorized use, without change authorization, subjects that user to the

same risk of abandoning its right as if it did not use the water at all.”

¶32    Under the common law, when the owner of the water right abandons or ceases to

use the water for its beneficial use, the right ceases. 79 Ranch, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9507591"><a href="/opinion/878149/79-ranch-inc-v-pitsch/#431" aria-description="Citation for case: 79 Ranch, Inc. v. ¶33    In reliance on its reading of Zier, the Water Court rejected YC Properties’ beneficial

use argument. It reasoned that the use of the water for irrigation and livestock purposes by

YC Properties and its predecessors was impermissible because they failed to apply for and

obtain authority from the DNRC to change the water’s use from commercial beaver pens.

Consequently, because the original statement of claim did not authorize irrigation and

livestock, and because YC Properties or the Vinings did not amend the statement of claim

to authorize irrigation and livestock use, such uses were “unauthorized.” Thus, the Water

Court concluded that, “when unauthorized water use occurs for a sufficiently long period

of time, the unauthorized use becomes the functional equivalent of intent to abandon a

water right.” In essence, the Water Court held that a long period of water use inconsistent

with the statement of claim, or which has not been authorized by the DNRC, constitutes,



                                               14
or raises a presumption of, intentional non-use of the water as a matter of law, without

considering other evidence.

¶34    We disagree with aspects of the Water Court’s reasoning, as well as the congruent

argument made by Connolly and Spaan that we should adopt a new rule declaring “a water

user’s unauthorized use, without change authorization, subjects that user to the same risk

of abandoning its right as if it did not use the water at all.” This rendering of “non-use” is

too narrow under the common law. Not using water in accordance with an existing

statement of claim is not factually the same thing as not using the water at all, and the

common law’s expansive inquiry into the circumstances requires consideration of both of

these facts for purposes of determining whether an existing right has been abandoned. “To

constitute abandonment there must be a concurrence of act and intent—the relinquishment

of possession and the intent not to resume it for a beneficial use.” Thomas v. Ball, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8023683"><a href="/opinion/8065750/thomas-v-ball/#167" aria-description="Citation for case: Thomas v. Ball">66 Mont.

161, 167</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8023683"><a href="/opinion/8065750/thomas-v-ball/#599" aria-description="Citation for case: Thomas v. Ball">213 P. 597, 599</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1923) (citations omitted). “Abandonment is always a voluntary

act, and it is a principle, quite uniformly recognized, that when once an appropriation of

water has been completed, the courts will not lightly decree an abandonment of a property

so valuable in a semi-arid region such as this.” Thomas, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8023683"><a href="/opinion/8065750/thomas-v-ball/#167" aria-description="Citation for case: Thomas v. Ball">66 Mont. at 167</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8023683"><a href="/opinion/8065750/thomas-v-ball/#599" aria-description="Citation for case: Thomas v. Ball">213 P. at 599</a></span><pre class="inline">

(citation omitted). Consequently, the Water Court erred by holding, as a matter of law,

that “unauthorized” use of water is the functional equivalent to an intention to abandon the

water which precludes consideration of the other circumstances.4

¶36    Notably, non-use was not a contested issue in Zier; the Ziers conceded non-use of

the water during both periods, including the years of actual non-use. Zier, at *1. In

contrast, non-use in the present case is highly contested (YC Properties contends that it and

its predecessors continued to use the water for a beneficial use and cannot be considered

non-use, while Appellees argue unauthorized use cannot constitute a beneficial use and

therefore must be a form of non-use). Further, the Zier Court did exactly what we are

requiring here—it weighed the evidence supporting or inferring an intent to abandon

against the evidence inferring no intent to abandon, including the Ziers’ assertion that they

did not intend to abandon their rights. The Zier Court considered the Ziers’ failure to obtain

a change authorization, reasoning that “[w]ithout an approved change application, Parker

had no legal right to divert water from Sage Creek. The failure to comply with applicable

law is evidence of intent to abandon a water right because Parker took no action to secure

a lawful and permanent alternate supply once his primary sources became unavailable.”

Zier, at *4 (emphasis added). Thus, the failure to obtain a change authorization was an

evidentiary consideration for the abandonment determination in Zier, not a mandate of law.

Further, important in Zier was that, during the period Parker was drawing water from Sage

Creek to irrigate his land, he did not use the water assigned by his statements of claim at

all, and thus the Water Court’s abandonment determination rested upon both the evidence

of Parker’s failure to obtain a change authorization and his actual failure to physically use

the water, including the subsequent 15-year period of non-use. Zier, at *5 (“Even if the

period of non-use was shortened when the claimants used an alternative source, there was

still a 15-year period of non-use.”). Other evidence included the “sale or disposal of

irrigation equipment, failure to replace an inoperable pump, failure to maintain existing

ditches, failure to pursue a meaningful and permanent solution to address the inability to

irrigate, and the lack of plan to resume irrigation in the future.” Zier, at *5. Zier thus

supports this Court’s long held position that “intent, or lack of intent, can be drawn from

the actions and inactions of the claimants and their predecessors.” Zier, at *4. Appellees

omit from their arguments the full record evidence in Zier. Thus, even if Zier was binding

precedent, a governing principle that “unauthorized use is the functional equivalent of

nonuse” could not be properly drawn from its facts and holding.

¶37    Montana has a long historical common law governing water rights. “In Montana,

the common law is the rule of decision ‘so far as it is not repugnant to or inconsistent with

the Constitution of the United States, or the Constitution or statutes of Montana[.]’” Phipps

v. Old Republic Nat’l Title Ins. Co., </pre><span class="citation no-link">2021 MT 152</span><pre class="inline">, ¶ 20, </pre><span class="citation no-link">404 Mont. 336</span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation no-link">489 P.3d 507</span><pre class="inline">

(quoting Haker v. Sw. R.R., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1174562"><a href="/opinion/1174562/haker-v-southwestern-railway-co/#368" aria-description="Citation for case: Haker v. Southwestern Railway Co.">176 Mont. 364, 368-69</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1174562"><a href="/opinion/1174562/haker-v-southwestern-railway-co/#727" aria-description="Citation for case: Haker v. Southwestern Railway Co.">578 P.2d 724, 727</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1978)). “The

common law ‘does not control . . . where the law is declared by statute.’” Phipps, ¶ 20

(citation omitted). The Legislature has acted on occasion to revise the common law of

water, and here, as noted above, it has enacted statutes that will apply different

abandonment principles to claims that are finally determined and for which a final decree

has been entered. See § 85-2-404(6), MCA. Included therein is a provision that will

consider an appropriation right to be abandoned “if the appropriator ceases using the

appropriation right according to its terms and conditions with the intention of not

                                             18
complying with those terms and conditions,” thus narrowing for that purpose the common

law abandonment inquiry. Section 85-2-404(1), MCA. That law is not effective for

purposes of the rights at issue here because there has been no final decree.

¶38    We do not intend by our holding to excuse the unauthorized use of water and thereby

incentivize or reward parties who fail to comply with the statutory obligation to obtain a

change authorization when their water use changes. Rather, our holding is required by the

common law and simply reaffirms two fundamental principles thereunder.                First,

abandonment of a water right remains a question of fact. See, e.g., § 89-802, Revised

Codes of Montana, 1947. Second, “an appropriation of a right to the use of running water

flowing in the creeks must be for some useful or beneficial purpose, and when the

appropriator, or his successor in interest, abandons and ceases to use the water for such

purpose, the right ceases.” Power, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6639575"><a href="/opinion/6757014/power-v-switzer/#529" aria-description="Citation for case: Power v. Switzer">21 Mont. at 529</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6639575"><a href="/opinion/6757014/power-v-switzer/#35" aria-description="Citation for case: Power v. Switzer">55 P. at 35</a></span><pre class="inline">. Therefore, in determining

whether a claimant has abandoned its water rights, the Water Court must consider all the

facts and circumstances of the case, as discussed above, including whether the claimant

used the water in an unauthorized manner and whether the claimant continuously applied

the water to a beneficial purpose. By reaching its conclusion regard the intent to abandon

as a matter of law, and failing to consider evidence of YC Properties’ continuing beneficial

use for the water, the Water Court erred in granting summary judgment in favor of

Appellees.

¶39    We reverse the entry of summary judgment and remand this matter for such further

proceedings as necessary to properly determine the abandonment question, and resolve any

remaining objections filed herein, in accordance with this decision.
