ZAHN, J.
        Frank Ellman appeals the district court’s decision denying his petition for a writ of
mandate, seeking an order compelling the Director of the Idaho Department of Health and Welfare
to issue a final order regarding its recommendation, made in a Kootenai County Child Protection
Act (“CPA”) case, that Ellman’s three grandchildren be permanently placed with a non-relative
foster parent instead of Ellman. Ellman sought the final order so he could file a petition for judicial
review of the placement recommendation in a different court. The district court denied Ellman’s
petition after determining that (1) it lacked subject matter jurisdiction to take action related to the
CPA case, (2) Ellman lacked standing to challenge actions taken in the CPA case, and (3) Ellman
could not establish that the Director had a mandatory duty to issue a final order and therefore failed
to establish an entitlement to a writ of mandate.




                                                    1
       For reasons discussed below, we affirm the district court’s decision because it correctly
concluded that it lacked subject matter jurisdiction over Ellman’s petition. The substance of
Ellman’s writ petition sought a final order so he could challenge the Department’s permanency
placement recommendation in a court other than the one handling the CPA case. The CPA granted
the Kootenai County magistrate court exclusive jurisdiction over the CPA case, including any
challenge to the Department’s permanency placement recommendation filed in that case.
       Although we affirm the district court’s decision that the Kootenai County magistrate court
retained exclusive subject matter jurisdiction over issues related to the Department’s
recommendation, we disagree with the State’s assertion that Ellman is categorically precluded
from filing a motion for limited intervention in the CPA proceeding, concerning whether the
Department complied with the CPA in making its recommendation. As we explain below, while
Ellman may seek to intervene in the CPA case, the magistrate court retains discretion whether to
allow Ellman to intervene so he can challenge the Department’s compliance with Idaho Code
section 16-1629(11).
                  I.   FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND
       The following facts are gleaned from the parties’ briefing and documents contained in the
record on appeal. Ellman is the maternal grandfather of three minor children who were removed
from their mother’s home in Kootenai County. A CPA case was initiated against his daughter and
a Kootenai County magistrate judge placed the children in the custody of the Department. The
Department then placed the children with a non-relative foster parent. Ellman subsequently
obtained a license to operate a foster family home and requested the Department place the children
in his custody. The Department declined to do so. Neither party to this appeal requested this Court
take judicial notice of the record in the CPA proceeding. As a result, we cannot tell from the record
on appeal why the Department declined to place the children in Ellman’s custody.
       Ellman alleges that, in November 2025, the Department “verbally informed [Ellman] that
placement was denied, and further, that an Idaho Department of Health and Welfare Permanency
Committee selected the non-relative foster parent as permanent placement.” Ellman requested a
review of the Department’s recommendation. In January 2026, the Department verbally informed
him that the Permanency Review Committee affirmed the decision of the Permanency Committee.
       Ellman then submitted a public records request to the Department, seeking a copy of a final
order that detailed the Department’s findings of fact and conclusions supporting the Permanency



                                                 2
Committee’s decision. The Department advised Ellman that his request for an “extended
placement review” was being processed and he should receive a report the following week. Ellman
responded that he did not want a report but was instead requesting a final order pursuant to the
Idaho Administrative Procedure Act (“APA”). The Department responded that its placement
recommendation was not a final agency action because it was a “planning contingency.”
        Ellman subsequently filed a petition for a writ of mandate in Ada County District Court,
alleging that the Permanency Committee’s decision constituted a final agency action under the
APA and therefore, the Department was required to issue a final order that included its findings of
fact and conclusions of law. Ellman requested that the district court issue a writ of mandate
compelling the Director to issue a final order so Ellman could seek judicial review of the
Permanency Committee’s decision in district court. Ellman also requested a preliminary injunction
staying further action in the Kootenai County CPA case until he was able to obtain judicial review.
        While Ellman’s writ petition was pending in Ada County, the Department filed a petition
for termination of parental rights in the Kootenai County CPA case. This prompted Ellman to file
a motion in the Ada County writ case, requesting the district court to issue an expedited ruling on
his petition. After nine days had passed, and the Ada County district court had not issued a decision
on his motion or the petition, Ellman filed a Verified Petition for Writ of Mandate and Emergency
Request for Injunctive Relief with this Court, seeking an order directing the Ada County district
court to rule on his writ petition. The district court subsequently indicated that it did not believe it
could issue a decision on Ellman’s writ petition while Ellman’s subsequent petition to this Court
was pending. This Court then issued a writ of mandate to the district court, ordering it to rule on
Ellman’s original petition and temporarily staying the Kootenai County CPA case for fourteen
days.
        The district court subsequently issued a written decision denying Ellman’s petition for a
writ of mandate. Ellman timely filed this appeal.
                                  II.     ISSUES ON APPEAL
   1. Whether the district court erred when it concluded it lacked subject matter jurisdiction to
      consider Ellman’s petition for a writ of mandate.
   2. Whether Ellman’s procedural due process rights were violated.
                                        III.   ANALYSIS
        Ellman argues the district court erred when it denied his petition because the district court
incorrectly relied on the CPA to determine it lacked subject matter jurisdiction. Ellman alleges that


                                                   3
he is not seeking to challenge any decision made pursuant to the CPA but instead is seeking a final
order under the APA. Ellman contends that, because he was not challenging any decision made in
the CPA proceeding, the district court had subject matter jurisdiction to consider his writ petition
and to decide his claim arising under the APA. Ellman additionally asserts that the Department’s
refusal to issue a final order, combined with the district court’s dismissal of his petition, violates
his procedural due process rights because it deprives him of the ability to seek judicial review of
the Department’s permanency placement recommendation. For the reasons discussed below, we
affirm the district court’s decision that it lacked subject matter jurisdiction to consider Ellman’s
petition.
A. The district court correctly concluded that it lacked subject matter jurisdiction because
   Ellman’s petition concerned the Department’s recommendation made in the CPA
   proceeding and the Kootenai County magistrate court retained exclusive jurisdiction
   over matters in that case.
    1. The CPA grants the CPA court exclusive subject matter jurisdiction over review of the
       Department’s permanency placement recommendation.
        The Ada County district court concluded that it lacked subject matter jurisdiction to
consider Ellman’s writ petition because, pursuant to Idaho Code section 16-1603, the Kootenai
County magistrate court retained exclusive original jurisdiction over the CPA proceeding
concerning the children. Citing Idaho Code section 16-1629(8), the district court also noted that
the decision where to place the children is “subject to judicial review by the court” with jurisdiction
over the CPA case. Reading these sections together, the district court concluded that judicial
review of the Department’s placement recommendation in a CPA case is restricted to the court
overseeing the CPA case. Therefore, the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction to enter
an order compelling the Department to issue a final order that would permit Ellman to ask a
different court to review the Department’s placement recommendation.
        Issues of subject matter jurisdiction are questions of law that are freely reviewed. Slavens
v. Slavens, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4095335"><a href="/opinion/4318074/james-adam-slavens-v-melanie-slavens/#202" aria-description="Citation for case: James Adam Slavens v. Melanie Slavens">161 Idaho 198, 202</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4095335"><a href="/opinion/4318074/james-adam-slavens-v-melanie-slavens/#965" aria-description="Citation for case: James Adam Slavens v. Melanie Slavens">384 P.3d 962, 965</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2016). Ellman argues the district court erred
because it misconstrued the relief sought in the writ petition. He asserts that he sought a writ of
mandate requiring compliance with the APA, not judicial review of any decision in the CPA case.
Additionally, Ellman asserts that Idaho Code section 7-302 grants the district court subject matter
jurisdiction over petitions seeking issuance of a writ of mandate.
        The district court did not err in dismissing Ellman’s petition. Regardless of how he
describes his claims, the relief Ellman seeks is an order that would permit him to seek judicial


                                                  4
review of the Department’s placement recommendation for the children. The plain language of the
CPA restricts review of these matters to the magistrate court presiding over the CPA case. Idaho
law is clear that the child protection court retains exclusive subject matter jurisdiction over all
matters relating to children that are the subject of the CPA proceeding. I.C. § 16-1603(1)(a)
(“Except as otherwise provided herein, the court shall have exclusive original jurisdiction in all
proceedings under this chapter concerning any child living or found within the state: (a) [w]ho is
neglected, abused or abandoned by his parents, guardian or other legal custodian[.]”). This includes
review of the Department’s consideration of options for the children’s permanency placement.
I.C. § 16-1622(2)(d). Accordingly, if Ellman’s requested writ concerns an issue related to his
grandchildren that are the subject of the pending CPA case, the Kootenai County magistrate court
has exclusive jurisdiction over that issue.
       We look to the substance of Ellman’s claim to ascertain whether it concerns an issue within
the exclusive jurisdiction of the child protection court. See Carelon Behav. Health, Inc. v. State ex
rel. Idaho Dep’t of Admin., </pre><span class="citation no-link">176 Idaho 631</span><pre class="inline">, ___, </pre><span class="citation no-link">581 P.3d 298</span><pre class="inline">, 307–08 (2025). While Ellman
asserts that he only seeks compliance with the APA, not custody of his grandchildren or
intervention in the pending CPA case, his opening brief makes clear that he seeks a final order so
he can obtain judicial review of the Department’s permanency placement recommendation:
       Appellant respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court’s March 11,
       2026 order dismissing his Verified Petition for Writ of Mandate and remand with
       instructions to issue a peremptory writ of mandate directing the Director of the
       Idaho Department of Health and Welfare to issue a written final agency order
       consistent with Idaho Code [section] 67-5248 and Idaho Code [section] 16-
       1629(11), sufficient to permit judicial review.
(Emphasis added.)
       Of the two statutes Ellman cites in his opening brief, the first is inapplicable and the second
reveals the substance of what he seeks: the ability to challenge the Department’s permanency
placement recommendation in a different court. The first statute, Idaho Code section 67-5248, is
part of the APA and is entitled, “Contents of Orders.” It does not address whether the Department
is required to issue an order when it makes a recommendation in a CPA proceeding. Instead, it
identifies what must be included in an agency order.
       The second statute, Idaho Code section 16-1629(11), is part of the CPA and sets forth the
Department’s duties related to the protection of children. Subsection (11) addresses the
Department’s obligations related to the placement of children subject to the CPA:


                                                 5
       (11) At any time the department is considering a placement pursuant to this chapter,
       the department shall make a reasonable effort to place the child in the least
       restrictive environment to the child and in so doing shall consider, consistent with
       the best interest and special needs of the child, placement priority of the child in the
       following order:
           (a) A fit and willing relative;
           (b) A fit and willing nonrelative with a significant relationship with the child;
           (c) Foster parents and other persons licensed in accordance with chapter 12, title
           39, Idaho Code, with a significant relationship with the child;
           (d) Foster parents and other persons licensed in accordance with chapter 12,
           title 39, Idaho Code.
I.C. § 16-1629(11).
       Ellman asserts that he “does not seek custody, visitation, or intervention in a child
protection action.” However, he does seek review of the Department’s compliance with section
16-1629(11): “Appellant seeks only a written, reviewable agency decision sufficient to permit
judicial review of compliance with statutory placement obligations.” The substance of Ellman’s
claim is that he wants to seek judicial review of the Department’s permanency placement
recommendation, which did not recommend Ellman as the children’s permanent placement.
However, that issue concerns the Department’s compliance with the CPA and its recommendation
made in a specific CPA case. Under section 16-1603, the Kootenai County magistrate court
presiding over the children’s CPA case retains exclusive jurisdiction over both issues.
       The court presiding over a CPA case is required to periodically hold hearings according to
prescribed timeframes so it can review the child’s case and permanency plan. I.C. § 16-1622(1)(a).
The CPA defines a “permanency plan” as “a plan for a continuous residence and maintenance of
nurturing relationships during the child’s minority.” I.C. § 16-1602(34). The permanency plan
must include a permanency goal, which can include: (1) continued efforts at reunification, (2)
termination of parental rights and adoption, (3) guardianship or (4) another planned living
arrangement if the children are age 16 or older. I.C. § 16-1622(2)(a). If the permanency goal is
termination of parental rights, then the permanency plan must include a recommendation for a
permanent placement for the child. Id.; I.C. § 16-1621(3)(d). After reviewing the Department’s
permanency plan, the court approves, rejects, or modifies the permanency plan. I.C. § 16-
1622(2)(b). The permanency plan, as approved by the court, is entered as an order of the court. Id.




                                                  6
       Ellman concedes that, through judicial review, he seeks to challenge whether the
Department complied with Idaho Code section 16-1629(11) in making its permanency placement
recommendation. That Ellman’s writ petition is only the first step in seeking judicial review by a
different court does not change the fact that the substance of what he seeks is to challenge the
Department’s recommendation filed in the CPA case. The CPA is clear that exclusive jurisdiction
over that issue lies with the Kootenai County magistrate court presiding over the CPA case. As a
result, the district court did not err when it concluded it lacked subject matter jurisdiction over
Ellman’s writ petition, and we affirm its decision on that point.
       Although Ellman appears to assert that the APA provides an independent basis for review,
the APA actually defers to the CPA in this instance. Idaho Code section 67-5270 provides that
judicial review of agency action is governed by the APA unless another provision of law is
applicable. I.C. § 67-5270(1). Here, Ellman ultimately seeks to challenge the Department’s
compliance with section 16-1629(11), which is part of the CPA, and to challenge the Department’s
permanency placement recommendation filed in a pending CPA case concerning his
grandchildren. Because his challenge concerns the Department’s compliance with the CPA in a
pending CPA proceeding, section 16-1603(1) applies here. The CPA controls over any competing
provisions of the APA. See I.C. § 67-5270(1). As previously discussed, section 16-1603(1) vests
the Kootenai County magistrate court with exclusive jurisdiction over Ellman’s arguments
concerning the Department’s compliance with section 16-1629(11).
       Our holding today is also consistent with the CPA’s stated purpose to “[t]ake such actions
as may be necessary to provide the child with permanency including concurrent planning[.]” I.C.
§ 16-1601(3). To that end, the CPA provides that, “if [a] child has been in the temporary or legal
custody of the department for twelve (12) of the most recent twenty-two (22) months, the
department shall file, prior to the last day of the twelfth month, a petition to terminate parental
rights” unless the court makes certain findings. I.C. § 16-1622(2)(g). Based on the dates alleged in
Ellman’s petition, Idaho law required the Department to file a petition for termination. Permitting
Ellman to seek review of the Department’s recommendation in an entirely different court would
only delay providing the children with permanency in a timely manner.
       Allowing Ellman to seek judicial review of the Department’s permanency placement
recommendation in a different court would likely add months or years to the review process. A
judicial review process would: (1) require the Department to hold a contested case hearing before



                                                 7
making its recommendation; (2) require preparation of a record and transcript concerning the
contested case; (3) require a briefing schedule for the parties to present written argument to the
district court; (4) require a hearing so the parties could present argument to the district court; (5)
require the district court to prepare a written order deciding the petition for judicial review; and
(6) possibly require repeating steps 2 to 5 if one of the parties decided to appeal the district court’s
decision to this Court. In short, our decision today applies the plain language of the CPA, is
consistent with the APA, and advances the legislative purposes of the CPA.
        Despite Ellman’s arguments to the contrary, nothing in our statutory framework concerning
writs of mandamus changes this result. Ellman asserts that, regardless of what the CPA provides,
the district court retains subject matter jurisdiction under Idaho Code section 7-302. That code
section addresses when courts may issue writs of mandamus. It does not state that it overrides any
other provision of law or otherwise allows the district court to ignore the exclusive jurisdiction
granted by section 16-1603.
        Ellman asserts that, if we affirm the district court, he will be deprived of the ability to seek
judicial review of the Department’s permanency placement recommendation concerning his
grandchildren. This argument is unpersuasive because the CPA provides for judicial review of the
Department’s recommendation. The permanency plan, including the permanency placement
recommendation, is subject to judicial review and approval by the magistrate court presiding over
the CPA case. I.C. § 16-1622(2)(b).
        The Department’s placement recommendation is merely that—a recommendation to the
magistrate court. See id. It is the magistrate court that determines whether the Department’s
permanency placement recommendation is appropriate and in the best interest of the children, in
which case it accepts the recommendation and enters it as an order of the court. Id. The magistrate
court’s consideration of the Department’s recommendation can include inquiring of the
Department concerning its compliance with section 16-1629(11).
    2. Ellman is not precluded from filing a motion for limited intervention concerning the
       Department’s compliance with section 16-1629(11), but the magistrate court retains
       discretion whether to permit intervention and the scope of any intervention.
        The essence of Ellman’s argument here is that he is personally entitled to challenge the
Department’s permanency plan recommendation. This is clear from Ellman’s argument that he
must have a right to judicial review of the Department’s recommendation under the APA because
he is not a party to the CPA case and therefore cannot challenge the Department’s recommendation


                                                   8
in that proceeding. In response, the Director contends that Ellman has no right to challenge the
Department’s permanency placement recommendation. The Director asserts that the lack of review
reflects a deliberate choice by the legislature, and Ellman cannot use the APA to make an “end-
run around” the legislature’s decision to not provide a mechanism for relatives to seek judicial
review of the Department’s recommendation. Relying on this Court’s decision in Roe v. State (In
re Doe), </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2599644"><a href="/opinion/2599644/in-re-doe/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Doe">134 Idaho 760</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2599644"><a href="/opinion/2599644/in-re-doe/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Doe">9 P.3d 1226</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2000), the Director contends this Court “has expressly found
that [Ellman] lacks a right to participate” in the CPA case.
       We agree that the CPA does not expressly provide Ellman a right to seek judicial review
of the Department’s permanency placement recommendation. The CPA provides that, when legal
custody of a child is vested in the Department, “any party or counsel for a child may . . . file and
serve a written motion to contest matters relating to the placement of the child by the department.”
I.C. § 16-1619(11). Ellman concedes he is not a party to the CPA case and therefore, the CPA does
not provide him a right to file a motion contesting the Department’s permanency placement
recommendation.
       However, we disagree with the Director’s contention that our decision in Roe precludes
Ellman from filing a motion for permissive intervention in the CPA case. The Director argues that
in Roe, we held that a grandmother did not have a right to intervene in a CPA proceeding because
“[a] CPA action is not intended to provide a forum for multiple claimants to litigate their right to
custody.” (Alteration in original; quoting Roe, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2599644"><a href="/opinion/2599644/in-re-doe/#767" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Doe">134 Idaho at 767</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2599644"><a href="/opinion/2599644/in-re-doe/#1233" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Doe">9 P.3d at 1233</a></span><pre class="inline">.) While the
Director is correct concerning what we decided in Roe, we conclude that our decision is no longer
applicable in light of subsequent legislative amendments to the CPA.
       Roe concerned a grandmother’s appeal of a magistrate court order denying her motion for
permissive intervention in a CPA case concerning her grandchild. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2599644"><a href="/opinion/2599644/in-re-doe/#762" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Doe">Id. at 762</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2599644"><a href="/opinion/2599644/in-re-doe/#1228" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Doe">9 P.3d at 1228</a></span><pre class="inline">. The
grandmother sought intervention to argue that the magistrate court should give her custody of the
child rather than giving custody to the child’s biological father. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2599644"><a href="/opinion/2599644/in-re-doe/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Doe">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> The grandmother asserted that
she was entitled to custody under section 16-1629(k) and therefore she had a right to intervene to
assert her rights under the statute. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2599644"><a href="/opinion/2599644/in-re-doe/#764" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Doe">Id. at 764</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2599644"><a href="/opinion/2599644/in-re-doe/#1230" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Doe">9 P.3d at 1230</a></span><pre class="inline">. Section 16-1623(k) has since been
amended and recodified as Idaho Code section 16-1629(11). See Act of Apr. 14, 2005, ch. 391, §
31, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2005 Idaho Sess. Laws 1261</span><pre class="inline">, 1288–89. This is the same code section that Ellman cites here as
the basis on which he would seek judicial review of the Department’s recommendation in his
grandchildren’s CPA case.



                                                 9
        In Roe, we held that the CPA did not provide the grandmother a conditional right to
intervene because the CPA gave the Department the affirmative right to determine where the child
would live, and the Department’s decision was not subject to judicial review. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2599644"><a href="/opinion/2599644/in-re-doe/#765" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Doe">134 Idaho at 765</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2599644"><a href="/opinion/2599644/in-re-doe/#1231" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Doe">9
P.3d at 1231</a></span><pre class="inline">. Further, we recognized that section 16-1623(k) only required the Department to
consider related persons as an option for placement, but did not mandate that the Department place
the children with relatives. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2599644"><a href="/opinion/2599644/in-re-doe/#764" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Doe">Id. at 764</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2599644"><a href="/opinion/2599644/in-re-doe/#1230" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Doe">9 P.3d at 1230</a></span><pre class="inline">. Given the statutory language, this Court
concluded that the statute did not create a permissive right for relatives to intervene in the CPA
proceeding. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2599644"><a href="/opinion/2599644/in-re-doe/#764" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Doe">Id. at 764, 768</a></span><pre class="inline">; </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2599644"><a href="/opinion/2599644/in-re-doe/#1230" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Doe">9 P.3d at 1230, 1234</a></span><pre class="inline">.
        However, subsequent amendments to the CPA have undermined our holding. Following
our decision in Roe, the Idaho legislature amended the CPA in 2013 to remove the Department’s
ability to determine the child’s permanent placement. The amendments required the magistrate
court to approve, modify, or reject the Department’s permanency plan, including its
recommendation for where the children should live. Compare Act of Apr. 14, 2005, ch. 391, § 22,
</pre><span class="citation no-link">2005 Idaho Sess. Laws 1263</span><pre class="inline">, 1282–83, with Act of Apr. 9, 2013, ch. 287, § 4, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2013 Idaho Sess.
Laws 741</span><pre class="inline">, 747–48. As previously discussed, the magistrate court now determines the children’s
permanent placement. See I.C. § 16-1620(4), (6). So, to the extent that Roe held that a grandparent
did not have a right to permissive intervention because the Department made the decision
concerning the child’s permanent placement, that premise no longer holds true.
        Additionally, following our decision in Roe, the legislature enacted a new section in the
CPA concerning attendance at CPA hearings. See Act of Mar. 22, 2001, ch. 107, § 7, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2001 Idaho
Sess. Laws 350</span><pre class="inline">, 358 (current version at I.C. § 16-1613). Idaho Code section 16-1613 excludes the
general public from attending hearings in CPA cases. I.C. § 16-1613(1). However, it also provides
an exception to this general rule. Those who are “found by the court to have a direct interest in the
case” may attend these hearings. Id. The statute thus provides a basis for individuals asserting a
direct interest in a CPA case to file a motion to intervene. It is then up to the court presiding over
the CPA case to make factual findings concerning whether the movant has a direct interest in the
case.
        These legislative amendments and enactments undermine the basis for our holding in Roe
concerning the availability of permissive intervention. As a result, we hold that the permissive
intervention holding in Roe is no longer applicable in light of the legislative actions following our
decision in that case.



                                                 10
       Nevertheless, this opinion should not be read as holding that Ellman has an absolute right
to intervene in the Kootenai County CPA proceeding. We stand by our statement in Roe that the
legislature did not intend for CPA cases to become a forum for custody battles. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2599644"><a href="/opinion/2599644/in-re-doe/#767" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Doe">134 Idaho at 767</a></span><pre class="inline">,
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="2599644"><a href="/opinion/2599644/in-re-doe/#1233" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Doe">9 P.3d at 1233</a></span><pre class="inline">. We only hold that, in light of subsequent changes to the CPA, Ellman is not
precluded from filing a motion for permissive intervention. However, the decision whether to
permit intervention, and the scope of any intervention, is left to the discretion of the CPA court.
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="2599644"><a href="/opinion/2599644/in-re-doe/#763" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Doe">Id. at 763</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2599644"><a href="/opinion/2599644/in-re-doe/#1229" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Doe">9 P.3d at 1229</a></span><pre class="inline">.
       Finally, we reiterate our statement in Roe that nothing in the plain language of section 16-
1629(11) grants Ellman a right to custody of his grandchildren. While section 16-1629(11)(a)
requires the Department to consider fit and willing relatives for placement, consistent with the best
interest and special needs of the child, it does not mandate that the Department place children with
relatives. Depending on the information contained in the Department’s permanency plan and the
other evidence presented in the CPA case, Ellman may be able to establish a direct interest in the
CPA case for purposes of assuring the Department’s compliance with section 16-1629(11) when
making its permanency placement recommendation. Even then, however, the CPA court retains
discretion whether to permit Ellman to intervene on a limited basis, for purposes related to the
Department’s statutory compliance with section 16-1629(11). It is entirely possible that the CPA
court could already have sufficient information to assess the Department’s statutory compliance.
B. Ellman’s procedural due process rights were not violated.
       Ellman also asserts that the combined effect of the Department’s refusal to issue a final
order and the district court’s refusal to issue a writ of mandate amounts to a violation of his
procedural due process rights. However, Ellman’s assertion is without merit.
       Procedural due process is a right guaranteed by the Fourteenth Amendment of the United
States Constitution. U.S. Const. amend XIV, § 1. This right requires notice and a hearing prior to
the deprivation of a life, liberty, or property interest. Telford v. Nye, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="880086"><a href="/opinion/880086/holli-l-telford-v-hon-david-c-nye/#611" aria-description="Citation for case: Holli L. Telford v. Hon. David C. Nye">154 Idaho 606, 611</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="880086"><a href="/opinion/880086/holli-l-telford-v-hon-david-c-nye/#269" aria-description="Citation for case: Holli L. Telford v. Hon. David C. Nye">301 P.3d
264, 269</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2013). Protected interests arise either from the Due Process Clause of the United States
Constitution or from state law. Ky. Dep’t of Corrs. v. Thompson, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9431672"><a href="/opinion/112260/kentucky-department-of-corrections-v-thompson/#460" aria-description="Citation for case: Kentucky Department of Corrections v. Thompson">490 U.S. 454, 460</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1989). Ellman,
however, has failed to establish any protected liberty or property interest as he has not pointed to
a constitutional or statutory right to the custody or placement of his grandchildren during the
pendency of their CPA case. Consequently, Ellman has failed to establish a violation of his right
to procedural due process.
