BRODY, Chief Justice
        This appeal involves the district court’s decision to reject the application of the merger
doctrine in a real property dispute and its decision to award attorney fees to the sellers under Idaho
Code section 12-120(3). Brandaan and Melissa DeGroot purchased a family cabin and its contents
from Dave and Ardean Armitage. After closing the sale, a dispute arose over payment for the
personal property, and the Armitages sued for breach of contract. Following a bench trial, the
district court entered judgment in favor of the Armitages for $96,538 in damages plus attorney
fees.
        The DeGroots challenge the district court’s denial of their post-trial motion for
reconsideration. They do not challenge the district court’s findings of fact but instead argue that


                                                  1
the district court erred in refusing to apply the merger doctrine to bar the Armitages’ claim. They
contend that, given the district court’s conclusion that the parties mutually intended to transfer both
the real and personal property in a single transaction for a total amount of $450,000, their promise
to pay the $100,000 for the personal property merged into the warranty deed and was extinguished
at closing. They argue the district court’s legal analysis of the merger doctrine was erroneous and
that the case should be remanded for reconsideration of the issue. The DeGroots also challenge the
district court’s award of attorney fees to the Armitages under Idaho Code section 12-120(3). Their
contention is that any civil action that falls within the ambit of section 12-120(3) must involve a
“commercial transaction.” For the reasons discussed below, we affirm the district court’s decisions.
                        I.   FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND
A. The property and the parties.
       In July 2024, the Armitages agreed to sell their real property in Lenore, Idaho, to the
DeGroots. The ten-acre property included a cabin, a three-bay enclosed shop, a shed, outhouse,
wooded areas, and an open meadow overlooking Dworshak Reservoir (collectively, “Property”).
Dave Armitage and his twin brother, Doug, built the cabin, and the two families owned the property
together for about twenty years. The Armitages became the sole owners of the Property when Doug
passed away. The Armitages and their extended family treated the Property as their getaway spot,
hosting vacations, reunions, and a wedding on the property. Because of their advancing age, and
Dave’s health problems, Ardean decided it was time to sell the Property in the summer of 2024.
B. The transaction.
       On July 4, 2024, Ardean told her neighbors that she and Dave were ready to sell the
Property. The news traveled fast, and the DeGroot family heard about the opportunity the next day.
Three days later the parties shook hands, agreeing on $450,000 cash for the real property and the
personal property on the premises. On July 8, 2024, the DeGroots arrived back at the Property with
two documents they drafted to effectuate the transaction. While the Armitages had reservations
with there being two documents instead of one, they signed both documents.
       The first document, titled “Idaho Residential Real Estate Purchase Agreement” (“Real
Estate Agreement”) addressed the purchase of the single-family home and ten acres of land. The
second document, titled “Idaho General Bill of Sale” (“Personalty Agreement”) addressed the sale
of the personal property. The Real Estate Agreement provided that the Armitages agreed to sell the
real property to the DeGroots for $350,000, cash. The Real Estate Agreement also addressed the



                                                  2
sale of the personal property on the premises in three different provisions: (1) “the Seller shall
include the following personal property: Separate Agreement,” (2) “the Seller DOES NOT include
the following fixtures: Personal Items and Pictures only,” and (3) “Additional Terms and
Conditions: Seller to Include All contents, furniture, tools, Appliances, 2 Four Wheelers, Boat, and
any/all other Contents of Shop and House.”
       The Personalty Agreement provided a separate purchase price of $100,000 for “[a]ny and
[a]ll contents of all buildings” located on the premises. It also listed two 4-Wheeler recreational
vehicles, a boat, and a trailer, with the “REMAINDER UNKNOWN.” The district court noted in
its findings of fact that neither party performed a complete inventory of the personal property at
signing or by closing. Above the description of the personal property, the Personalty Agreement
stated: “Purchase Conditioned upon Completed Purchase of [Physical Address], by: DEGROOTS,
FROM: ARMITAGE.”
       The district court found that the Armitages intended to sell the real property and the
personal property altogether for $450,000. The district court also found that the DeGroots would
not have purchased the personal property independent of the real property.
C. The disappearances of personal property from the premises.
       After the Real Estate Agreement and Personalty Agreement were signed, the Armitages
promptly packed up the few personal items that the Real Estate Agreement allowed them to retain
and headed back home to Washington. The items they took included a large sign saying, “Doug’s
Den” (the name of Dave’s twin), which Brandaan DeGroot helped them load into their car, as well
as a sauerkraut cutter and some photos. When the Armitages left the Property that day, they
mistakenly left behind Ardean’s customized hiking poles. The remainder of the personal property
in and around the Property were to be transferred to the DeGroots at closing, except for some
fishing poles that were going to be picked up by the Armitages’ nephew, Jared Meske.
       The parties closed their transaction on July 26, 2024. It is not disputed that the Armitages
conveyed title of the Property to the DeGroots, and the DeGroots paid the $350,000 in return. The
warranty deed does not appear to have been presented as an exhibit at trial, and it does not
otherwise appear to be in the record on appeal. The DeGroots never paid the $100,000
contemplated under the Personalty Agreement.
       When the DeGroots arrived at the Property after closing, they discovered some of the items
that they had anticipated being left on the premises were missing. They claimed the items included:



                                                 3
(1) a radial saw; (2) a trolling motor; (3) a pair of walkie talkies; (4) the large “Doug’s Den” sign;
(5) a white utility trailer and damaged tire; (6) high-power binoculars; (7) various hand tools,
including a mallet and a rake; (8) Ardean’s custom hiking poles; (9) various gas cans; (10) a Sorry
board game; (11) a spotting scope and related stand; (12) an electric constellation finder; (13) a
binocular stand; and (14) a shovel.
       Many of these items were later discovered to be in the possession of Meske who had visited
the property prior to closing to pick up the fishing poles that the Armitages had expressly excluded
from the sale. The binoculars, the Sorry board game, the electric constellation finder, and binocular
stand were allegedly returned prior to closing. The white utility trailer (minus a tire) was returned
after closing and after Meske’s wedding. The district court estimated the value of the missing items
to be, at most, $3,462.
D. The breach of contract complaint and resulting litigation.
       The Armitages filed suit, seeking recovery of the $100,000 contemplated by the Personalty
Agreement. Following the bench trial, the district court ruled in favor of the Armitages on their
breach of contract claim. The district court concluded that the Armitages had substantially
complied with their obligations and that the removal of the personal property did not constitute a
material breach. In its judgment, the district court awarded the $100,000 in damages to the
Armitages; however, it offset the $100,000 award by $3,462 to account for the missing items. The
Armitages have not challenged the offset on appeal.
       Following the issuance of the findings of fact and conclusions of law, the DeGroots filed a
motion for reconsideration. Contrary to the position they took at trial, the DeGroots argued that,
given the district court’s finding that the parties intended the Real Estate Agreement and Personalty
Agreement to constitute a single transaction, the DeGroots’ promise to purchase the personal
property merged with the warranty deed at the time of closing and extinguished their obligation to
pay the $100,000 contemplated in the Personalty Agreement. The district court denied the
DeGroots’ motion.
       The Armitages filed a motion for attorney fees pursuant to Idaho Code section 12-120(3).
The district court initially denied the request for attorney fees, but granted it upon reconsideration.
The Armitages argued that because the Personalty Agreement was titled “Idaho General Bill of
Sale,” it constituted a “bill” under Idaho Code section 12-120(3). Drawing on a Black’s Law
Dictionary definition of “bill of sale,” the district court concluded the Personalty Agreement was



                                                  4
a “bill” as that term is used in the statute and granted the Armitages attorney fees. The DeGroots
timely appealed.
                                  II.    STANDARDS OF REVIEW
        When deciding a motion for reconsideration, the district court employs the same standard
of review that it used when deciding the original motion that is being reconsidered. Fragnella v.
Petrovich, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="880186"><a href="/opinion/880186/tiffany-ann-marie-fragnella-v-robert-b-petrovich-jr/#276" aria-description="Citation for case: Tiffany Ann Marie Fragnella v. Robert B. Petrovich, Jr.">153 Idaho 266, 276</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="880186"><a href="/opinion/880186/tiffany-ann-marie-fragnella-v-robert-b-petrovich-jr/#113" aria-description="Citation for case: Tiffany Ann Marie Fragnella v. Robert B. Petrovich, Jr.">281 P.3d 103, 113</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2012). This Court reviews a trial court’s decision
to grant or deny a motion for reconsideration using the same standard employed by the trial court.
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="880186"><a href="/opinion/880186/tiffany-ann-marie-fragnella-v-robert-b-petrovich-jr/" aria-description="Citation for case: Tiffany Ann Marie Fragnella v. Robert B. Petrovich, Jr.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">
        Since this Court is reviewing the district court’s decision on the DeGroot’s motion for
reconsideration of the findings of fact and conclusions of law, this Court “is limited to determining
whether the evidence supports the findings of fact and whether the findings of fact support the
conclusions of law.” Burns Concrete, Inc. v. Teton County, </pre><span class="citation no-link">168 Idaho 442</span><pre class="inline">, 451, </pre><span class="citation no-link">483 P.3d 985</span><pre class="inline">, 994
(2020) (citing Caldwell Land &amp; Cattle, LLC v. Johnson Thermal Sys., Inc., </pre><span class="citation no-link">165 Idaho 787</span><pre class="inline">, 795,
</pre><span class="citation no-link">452 P.3d 809</span><pre class="inline">, 817 (2019)). This Court employs free review over the district court’s conclusions of
law and may determine whether the trial court correctly stated the applicable law and whether the
facts sustain its legal conclusions. </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline"> (citing Kunz v. Nield, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4185254"><a href="/opinion/4408001/bret-and-marti-kunz-v-nield-inc/#438" aria-description="Citation for case: Bret and Marti Kunz v. Nield, Inc.">162 Idaho 432, 438</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4185254"><a href="/opinion/4408001/bret-and-marti-kunz-v-nield-inc/#171" aria-description="Citation for case: Bret and Marti Kunz v. Dales Used Cars, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2583934"><a href="/opinion/2583934/haight-v-dales-used-cars-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Haight v. Dales Used Cars, Inc.">139 Idaho 853</a></span><pre class="inline">, (Ct. App. 2003) (citing Staggie v. Idaho Falls Consol.

        When reviewing a motion for reconsideration of an award of attorney fees, this Court

</pre><span class="citation no-link">163 Idaho 281</span><pre class="inline">, 282–83, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4245018"><a href="/opinion/4467765/med-recovery-servs-llc-v-lopez/" aria-description="Citation for case: Med.                                          III.    ANALYSIS
A. The district court did not err by concluding that the promise contemplated by the
   Personalty Agreement did not merge with the warranty deed.
        The DeGroots challenge the district court’s denial of their amended motion for
reconsideration. After the district court issued its findings of fact and conclusions of law, the
DeGroots argued that the merger doctrine barred the Armitages’ claim. They argue that, since the


                                                    5
district court found that the parties intended a single $450,000 transaction for the purchase of the
real property and the personal property, their promise to pay the $100,000 for the personal property
merged with the warranty deed at closing. The district court rejected that argument, pointing out
that the Armitages took the position prior to trial that the merger doctrine did not apply. The district
court went on to analyze the merger defense and concluded that merger did not apply because the
Personalty Agreement was not antecedent to the warranty deed and the promise of payment
envisioned by the Personalty Agreement was collateral to the deed.
       On appeal, the DeGroots do not contest that the Personalty Agreement contemplates a
$100,000 promise to pay the Armitages after closing. Rather, the DeGroots argue that the merger
doctrine should apply for two reasons: (1) the unified contract—the Personalty Agreement and the
Real Estate Agreement—was antecedent to closing and (2) the collateral promise exception does
not apply. On this basis, the DeGroots ask that this Court reverse the district court’s order.
       The DeGroots are correct that the Personalty Agreement was “antecedent” to the warranty
deed but are incorrect on the application of the collateral promise exception. The district court’s
determination that the Real Estate Agreement and Personalty Agreement constituted a single
contract is consistent with the legal conclusion that the one contract may contemplate two
transactions—more accurately, two promises of payment—one of which the parties intended to
survive closing. Therefore, we affirm the district court’s decision on the basis of the collateral
promise exception.
       The merger doctrine is a fundamental principle of real property law. See Jolley v. Idaho
Sec., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9844610"><a href="/opinion/1189836/jolley-v-idaho-securities-inc/#382" aria-description="Citation for case: Jolley v. Idaho Securities, Inc.">90 Idaho 373, 382</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9844610"><a href="/opinion/1189836/jolley-v-idaho-securities-inc/#884" aria-description="Citation for case: Jolley v. Idaho Securities, Inc.">414 P.2d 879, 884</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1966). It is a sister doctrine of the parol evidence
rule. Both the merger doctrine and the parol evidence rule, when applied, prevent prior or separate
agreements from altering the terms of a final written agreement. The merger doctrine creates a
“prima facie presumption of law” that acceptance of a deed is “execution of the whole contract.”
A. G. Shepard, Annotation, Deed as superseding, or merging, provisions of antecedent contract
imposing obligations upon the vendor, </pre><span class="citation no-link">84 A.L.R. 1008</span><pre class="inline"> (1933). This Court explained more than
sixty years ago that once a deed is accepted, it generally replaces any earlier contract, and any
resulting claim must be based on the covenants or promises within the deed itself, not the prior
contract:
       [A]cceptance of a deed to premises generally is considered as a merger of the
       agreements of an antecedent contract into the terms of the deed, and any claim for



                                                   6
        relief must be based on the covenants or agreements contained in the deed, not the
        covenants or agreements as contained in the prior agreement.
Jolley, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9844610"><a href="/opinion/1189836/jolley-v-idaho-securities-inc/#382" aria-description="Citation for case: Jolley v. Idaho Securities, Inc.">90 Idaho at 382</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9844610"><a href="/opinion/1189836/jolley-v-idaho-securities-inc/#884" aria-description="Citation for case: Jolley v. Idaho Securities, Inc.">414 P.2d at 884</a></span><pre class="inline">; see also Bear Crest Ltd., LLC v. State ex rel. Idaho Transp.
Dep’t, </pre><span class="citation no-link">176 Idaho 262</span><pre class="inline">, ___, </pre><span class="citation no-link">575 P.3d 846</span><pre class="inline">, 856 (2025) (“Put differently, the four corners of the deed
govern the conveyance. The deed generally subsumes and extinguishes any covenants or promises
in the contract of sale.”).
        The district court concluded that the merger doctrine did not apply to the Personalty
Agreement since it and the Real Estate Agreement “were made simultaneously.” The DeGroots
assert that this is a misapplication of the merger doctrine. They argue that because the district court
found that the Personalty Agreement and the Real Estate Agreement constitute one contract, the
Personalty Agreement must be considered antecedent to the deed, which is the focus of the merger
doctrine. The DeGroots are correct; antecedence is measured relative to the deed, not relative to
the companion contract. While the district court’s analysis on this point was erroneous, its ultimate
decision to reject the application of the merger doctrine was still correct.
        The district court correctly rejected the application of the merger doctrine because the
promise within the Personalty Agreement fits within the collateral promise exception to the merger
doctrine. The district court concluded that “the rights with respect to the personal property were
conferred to the DeGroots collaterally and independently from the deed because the DeGroots
opted to draft two separate contracts.” The district court supported this by stating: “the DeGroots
opted to not write one contract” but “instead . . . created two transactions to [e]ffect the sale of real
estate and personal property.” In other words, the district court concluded that there was one
contract, and that by supplying two documents to effectuate the sale, the DeGroots intended that
there be two promises of payment: one promise of payment of $350,000 for real estate, as set forth
in the Real Estate Agreement, and a second promise of $100,000 for the personal property to be
paid later, as set forth in the Personalty Agreement.
        This Court has recognized two exceptions to the merger doctrine. The first exception is that
the merger doctrine will not apply where a court finds fraud or mutual mistake. See West v. Bowen,
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="5174278"><a href="/opinion/5341688/west-v-bowen/#130" aria-description="Citation for case: West v. Bowen">127 Idaho 128, 130</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5174278"><a href="/opinion/5341688/west-v-bowen/#61" aria-description="Citation for case: West v. Bowen">898 P.2d 59, 61</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1995). The second exception is the collateral promise
exception. Generally, this exception provides that “the merger doctrine does not apply when there
are collateral or independent agreements that the parties intended to survive the delivery of the
deed.” 9 Thompson on Real Property § 82.13(d) (David A. Thomas 4th ed., 2025). This exception



                                                   7
allows a promise within a real estate contract to survive merger with the deed at closing. Fuller v.
Callister, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="874142"><a href="/opinion/874142/fuller-v-dave-callister/#853" aria-description="Citation for case: Fuller v. DAVE CALLISTER">150 Idaho 848, 853</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="874142"><a href="/opinion/874142/fuller-v-dave-callister/#1271" aria-description="Citation for case: Fuller v. DAVE CALLISTER">252 P.3d 1266, 1271</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2011).
        We have addressed the collateral promise exception in a number of cases over the years,
but we have not clearly articulated an analytical framework to be applied. See Jolley, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9844610"><a href="/opinion/1189836/jolley-v-idaho-securities-inc/#383" aria-description="Citation for case: Jolley v. Idaho Securities, Inc.">90 Idaho at
383</a></span><pre class="inline">, 414 P.2d at 884–85 (“[T]he question to be determined is whether the parties have intentionally
surrendered or waived [delivery and acceptance-related] stipulations.”); Sells v. Robinson, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2595556"><a href="/opinion/2595556/sells-v-robinson/#772" aria-description="Citation for case: Sells v. Robinson">141
Idaho 767, 772</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2595556"><a href="/opinion/2595556/sells-v-robinson/#104" aria-description="Citation for case: Sells v. Robinson">118 P.3d 99, 104</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2005) (“Where the right claimed under the contract . . . inheres
in the very subject-matter with which the deed deals, a prior contract covering the same subject-
matter cannot be shown as against the provisions of the deed.” (citation modified)). See also Fuller,
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="874142"><a href="/opinion/874142/fuller-v-dave-callister/#853" aria-description="Citation for case: Fuller v. DAVE CALLISTER">150 Idaho at 853</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="874142"><a href="/opinion/874142/fuller-v-dave-callister/#1271" aria-description="Citation for case: Fuller v. DAVE CALLISTER">252 P.3d at 1271</a></span><pre class="inline"> (“[W]here not all of the prior contract’s provisions were
complied with, where those contractual provisions are collateral stipulations there is no
presumption that any party intended to give up the benefit of such covenants, and this is a question
of intent.”). Distilling these holdings, today we make clear that the collateral promise exception
applies if (1) the promise at issue is “collateral” and (2) the parties intended that promise to survive
merger with the deed.
        A promise within a purchase and sale agreement is considered to be “collateral” if it does
not relate to title, possession, parcel size, or agricultural crops. McOmber v. Thompson, </pre><span class="citation no-link">175 Idaho
970</span><pre class="inline">, 987, </pre><span class="citation no-link">572 P.3d 736</span><pre class="inline">, 753 (2025) (citing Jolley, 90 Idaho at 383–84, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9844610"><a href="/opinion/1189836/jolley-v-idaho-securities-inc/#885" aria-description="Citation for case: Jolley v. Idaho Securities, Inc.">414 P.2d at 885</a></span><pre class="inline">). For
instance, in Fuller, the Fullers agreed to sell 12.73 acres of land but executed an addendum to the
contract reserving to themselves the proceeds from the anticipated condemnation of a right-of-way
over the land by the Ada County Highway District (“ACHD”). </pre><span class="citation" data-id="874142"><a href="/opinion/874142/fuller-v-dave-callister/#850" aria-description="Citation for case: Fuller v. DAVE CALLISTER">150 Idaho at 850</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="874142"><a href="/opinion/874142/fuller-v-dave-callister/#1268" aria-description="Citation for case: Fuller v. DAVE CALLISTER">252 P.3d at 1268</a></span><pre class="inline">.
The addendum provided:
        3. Seller to receive any and all funds paid for road right of way including land,
        landscaping, fencing, sprinklers and temporary easements.
        Escrow instructions by the title company will cover the receipt and disbursement
        of the right of way funds. It is understood that buyer will be deeding the right of
        way to ACHD and that the seller, Dave and Shirley Fuller will receive all of said
        funds paid by ACHD. Said amount has not yet been determined and Dave and
        Shirley Fuller retain the right to negotiate the amount with ACHD.
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="874142"><a href="/opinion/874142/fuller-v-dave-callister/#852" aria-description="Citation for case: Fuller v. DAVE CALLISTER">Id. at 852</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="874142"><a href="/opinion/874142/fuller-v-dave-callister/#1270" aria-description="Citation for case: Fuller v. DAVE CALLISTER">252 P.3d at 1270</a></span><pre class="inline">. This reservation did not appear in the subsequent warranty deed issued
at closing. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="874142"><a href="/opinion/874142/fuller-v-dave-callister/" aria-description="Citation for case: Fuller v. DAVE CALLISTER">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">
        The district court in Fuller applied the doctrine of merger and granted summary judgment
in favor of the purchasers. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="874142"><a href="/opinion/874142/fuller-v-dave-callister/" aria-description="Citation for case: Fuller v. DAVE CALLISTER">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> at 850–51, 252 P.3d at 1268–69. This Court reversed the district


                                                   8
court’s decision, holding the addendum was enforceable. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="874142"><a href="/opinion/874142/fuller-v-dave-callister/#854" aria-description="Citation for case: Fuller v. DAVE CALLISTER">Id. at 854, 856</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="874142"><a href="/opinion/874142/fuller-v-dave-callister/#1272" aria-description="Citation for case: Fuller v. DAVE CALLISTER">252 P.3d at 1272, 1274</a></span><pre class="inline">.
This Court concluded the addendum was collateral because it dealt only with money. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="874142"><a href="/opinion/874142/fuller-v-dave-callister/#852" aria-description="Citation for case: Fuller v. DAVE CALLISTER">Id. at 852</a></span><pre class="inline">,
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="874142"><a href="/opinion/874142/fuller-v-dave-callister/#1270" aria-description="Citation for case: Fuller v. DAVE CALLISTER">252 P.3d at 1270</a></span><pre class="inline">. It held that, since the “reservation of the potential condemnation proceeds was
part of the consideration for the property,” the “reservation” did not merge with the deed. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="874142"><a href="/opinion/874142/fuller-v-dave-callister/#854" aria-description="Citation for case: Fuller v. DAVE CALLISTER">Id. at

intended the term to survive the merger of the agreement with the deed, we explained that, “[b]y
the very nature of the obligation established in Addendum # 1 [sic], it is clear that the parties
expected that provision to continue in effect after the execution of the warranty deed.” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3729973"><a href="/opinion/3978255/mcgovern-builders-inc-v-davis/" aria-description="Citation for case: McGovern Builders, Inc. v. Davis">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">
Addendum #1 required that the buyer deed over a right-of-way to ACHD. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3729973"><a href="/opinion/3978255/mcgovern-builders-inc-v-davis/" aria-description="Citation for case: McGovern Builders, Inc. v. Davis">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> “If the transfer of
the right-of-way was expected to occur prior to the buyer’s acquisition of the property[,] the buyer
would not need to be involved in the transfer of deed for that right-of-way.” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3729973"><a href="/opinion/3978255/mcgovern-builders-inc-v-davis/" aria-description="Citation for case: McGovern Builders, Inc. v. Davis">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">
       The DeGroots spend much of their opening brief arguing against the district court’s
decision to reject the merger doctrine. They argue that the district court’s assertion that the
Personalty Agreement contained a promise collateral to the real estate conveyance “blatantly
contradicts” its “foundational” conclusion that the parties mutually intended that the two
agreements constitute a single contract. The DeGroots essentially argue that the agreements cannot
simultaneously be treated as part of the same contract and as separate contracts for purposes of
determining whether the promise within the Personalty Agreement is collateral. They argue that,
in order for a promise to be collateral, the promise must be in a separate contract unrelated to the
real estate purchase. However, the DeGroots misunderstand the collateral promise exception. Even
if there is only a single contract in a single document, a promise within the contract itself may still
be collateral to the deed and survive closing. See Fuller, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="874142"><a href="/opinion/874142/fuller-v-dave-callister/#853" aria-description="Citation for case: Fuller v. DAVE CALLISTER">150 Idaho at 853</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="874142"><a href="/opinion/874142/fuller-v-dave-callister/#1271" aria-description="Citation for case: Fuller v. DAVE CALLISTER">252 P.3d at 1271</a></span><pre class="inline">
(“Noting that an abstract of title does not relate to the title, possession, quantity or emblements of
the land, the Jolley Court concluded that the agreement to furnish an abstract was a collateral
stipulation that was not merged through acceptance of the warranty deed.”) (citing Jolley, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9844610"><a href="/opinion/1189836/jolley-v-idaho-securities-inc/#385" aria-description="Citation for case: Jolley v. Idaho Securities, Inc.">90 Idaho
at 385</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9844610"><a href="/opinion/1189836/jolley-v-idaho-securities-inc/#886" aria-description="Citation for case: Jolley v. Idaho Securities, Inc.">414 P.2d at 886</a></span><pre class="inline">).
       Turning to the substance of the DeGroots’ merger argument, the promise within the
Personalty Agreement is collateral to the deed because it does not relate to the title, possession,



                                                  9
parcel size, or agricultural crops. McOmber, 175 Idaho at 987, 572 P.3d at 753 (citing Fuller, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="874142"><a href="/opinion/874142/fuller-v-dave-callister/#853" aria-description="Citation for case: Fuller v. DAVE CALLISTER">150
Idaho at 853</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="874142"><a href="/opinion/874142/fuller-v-dave-callister/#1271" aria-description="Citation for case: Fuller v. DAVE CALLISTER">252 P.3d at 1271</a></span><pre class="inline">). It is a term concerning money, which is the consideration for the
sale of the personal property left by the Armitages. Consideration does not impact the validity or
invalidity of title within the deed, nor does consideration set forth terms as to who can possess the
property; it does not describe the parcel’s size or bounds, and it has nothing to do with crops.
       Moving to the second element, the parties’ intent that the term survive merger with the
deed, we need to begin with the deed itself. If intent is “plainly expressed in the very terms of the
deed, it will be decisive.” Fuller, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="874142"><a href="/opinion/874142/fuller-v-dave-callister/#854" aria-description="Citation for case: Fuller v. DAVE CALLISTER">150 Idaho at 854</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="874142"><a href="/opinion/874142/fuller-v-dave-callister/#1272" aria-description="Citation for case: Fuller v. DAVE CALLISTER">252 P.3d at 1272</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2011) (quoting A. G. Shepard,
Annotation, Deed as superseding, or merging, provisions of antecedent contract imposing
obligations upon the vendor, </pre><span class="citation no-link">84 A.L.R. 1008</span><pre class="inline"> (1933)). Since examining for “plainly expressed”
language in a deed involves principles of contractual interpretation, this is a question of law. Bear
Crest Ltd. LLC, 176 Idaho at ___, 575 P.3d at 858 (“If the terms of the contract are unambiguous,
intent can be ascertained as a matter of law by looking within the four corners of the document.”).
       Here, however, the deed is not in the record on appeal. Nor does it appear to have been
presented to the district court in connection with the DeGroots’ amended motion for
reconsideration. “It is the responsibility of the appellant to provide a sufficient record to
substantiate his or her claims on appeal.” Turcott v. Est. of Bates, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6536314"><a href="/opinion/6658882/turcott-v-estate-of-bates/#188" aria-description="Citation for case: Turcott v. Estate of Bates">443 P.3d
197, 202</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2019) (quoting Greenfield v. Smith, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4174666"><a href="/opinion/4397413/christina-j-greenfield-v-ian-d-smith/#253" aria-description="Citation for case: Christina J. Greenfield v. Ian D. abrogated on other grounds by, Rich v. Hepworth Holzer, LLP, </pre><span class="citation no-link">172 Idaho 696</span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation no-link">535 P.3d 1069</span><pre class="inline">
(2023)). “[T]he missing portions of that record are presumed to support the action of the trial

McLaughlin">101 Idaho 292, 293</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1270446"><a href="/opinion/1270446/rutter-v-mclaughlin/#136" aria-description="Citation for case: Rutter v. citing Talbot v. Desert View Care Ctr., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9799996"><a href="/opinion/2679746/talbot-v-desert-view-care-center/#520" aria-description="Citation for case: Talbot v. Desert View Care Center">156 Idaho 517, 520</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9799996"><a href="/opinion/2679746/talbot-v-desert-view-care-center/#500" aria-description="Citation for case: Talbot v. Desert View Care Center">328 P.3d 497, 500</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2014)). Since the
district court ruled that the collateral promise exception applied in this case, we could simply
presume the deed supports the district court’s decision and affirm solely on that basis.
       Setting aside the record problem, we agree with the district court’s analysis of the parties’
intent. If intent is not plainly expressed within the deed, “the question is open to other evidence.”
Fuller, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="874142"><a href="/opinion/874142/fuller-v-dave-callister/#854" aria-description="Citation for case: Fuller v. DAVE CALLISTER">150 Idaho at 854</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="874142"><a href="/opinion/874142/fuller-v-dave-callister/#1272" aria-description="Citation for case: Fuller v. inferred intent from the evidence that the DeGroots drafted two contract documents to effectuate

that the actions of the DeGroots show that the parties intended the payment term of the Personalty
Agreement to survive merger:

               The DeGroots argue in the Amended Motion to Reconsider that the
       Personalty Contract was not a collateral agreement because the Personalty Contract

B. The district court did not err when it concluded that the term “commercial transaction”
   does not apply to all causes of action identified in Idaho Code section 12-120(3).
       Section 12-120(3) authorizes an award of attorney fees in eight different types of civil
cases, including actions to recover on an “open account,” “bill,” “contract relating to the purchase
or sale of goods,” and “any commercial transaction”:
       In any civil action to recover on an open account, account stated, note, bill,
       negotiable instrument, guaranty, or contract relating to the purchase or sale of
       goods, wares, merchandise, or services and in any commercial transaction unless
       otherwise provided by law, the prevailing party shall be allowed a reasonable
       attorney’s fee to be set by the court, to be taxed and collected as costs.
I.C. § 12-120(3). The district court awarded attorney fees to the Armitages under this provision,
concluding that the Personalty Agreement was a “bill of sale” as that term is defined in Black’s
Law Dictionary. Bill of Sale, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) (“An instrument for
conveying title to personal property, absolutely or by way of security.”). The DeGroots do not
challenge this conclusion. They do argue, however, that this case does not involve a “commercial
transaction” and that fees should not have been awarded. The district court rejected that argument,
concluding that the phrase: “and in any commercial transactions” within Idaho code section 12-
120(3) is not limiting language burdening the remainder of the subsection, but rather is an
independent authorization for attorney fees for cases involving “commercial transactions.” As



                                                 11
explained below, for forty years, we have applied the “commercial transaction” language in
subsection 12-120(3) as an independent authorization of attorney fees, not as limiting language
that applies to all the other the causes of action enumerated in that provision. Therefore, we affirm
the district court’s award of attorney fees on reconsideration.
       Because this appeal involves a challenge to the award of attorney fees based upon the
interpretation of Idaho Code section 12-120(3), this Court exercises free review over the
interpretation of that statute. Med. Recovery Servs., LLC v. Lopez, </pre><span class="citation no-link">163 Idaho 281</span><pre class="inline">, 282–83, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4245018"><a href="/opinion/4467765/med-recovery-servs-llc-v-lopez/" aria-description="Citation for case: Med. Recovery Servs., LLC v. Lopez">411
P.3d 1182</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1183–84 (2018). The DeGroots contend that the term “commercial transaction” as used
in subsection 12-120(3) applies to all award authorizations governed by the statute. The district
court rejected this argument, quoting the Court of Appeals’ decision in Eriksen v. Blue Cross of
Idaho Health Services, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1298211"><a href="/opinion/1298211/eriksen-v-blue-cross-of-idaho-health-services-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Eriksen v. Blue Cross of Idaho Health Services, Inc.">116 Idaho 693</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1298211"><a href="/opinion/1298211/eriksen-v-blue-cross-of-idaho-health-services-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Eriksen v. Blue Cross of Idaho Health Services, Inc.">778 P.2d 815</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Ct. App. 1989). The DeGroots contend
that Eriksen was wrongly decided and that it was subsequently overruled by our decision in Brower
v. E.I. DuPont De Nemours and Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1427743"><a href="/opinion/1427743/brower-v-ei-dupont-de-nemours-and-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Brower v. EI DuPont De Nemours and Co.">117 Idaho 780</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1427743"><a href="/opinion/1427743/brower-v-ei-dupont-de-nemours-and-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Brower v. EI DuPont De Nemours and Co.">792 P.2d 345</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1990). We disagree.
       In Eriksen, Blue Cross of Idaho contracted with Albert and Patricia Eriksen to provide
coverage for medical and hospital expenses. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1298211"><a href="/opinion/1298211/eriksen-v-blue-cross-of-idaho-health-services-inc/#694" aria-description="Citation for case: Eriksen v. Blue Cross of Idaho Health Services, Inc.">116 Idaho at 694</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1298211"><a href="/opinion/1298211/eriksen-v-blue-cross-of-idaho-health-services-inc/#816" aria-description="Citation for case: Eriksen v. Blue Cross of Idaho Health Services, Inc.">778 P.2d at 816</a></span><pre class="inline">. After Mrs. Eriksen
underwent surgery, Blue Cross initially paid some of her bills but later refused further payments,
claiming she failed to disclose a prior health issue on her application, leading to unilateral
rescission of the contract. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1298211"><a href="/opinion/1298211/eriksen-v-blue-cross-of-idaho-health-services-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Eriksen v. Blue Cross of Idaho Health Services, Inc.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> The Eriksens sued for breach of contract, and Blue Cross
counterclaimed to recover payments made before the rescission. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1298211"><a href="/opinion/1298211/eriksen-v-blue-cross-of-idaho-health-services-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Eriksen v. Blue Cross of Idaho Health Services, Inc.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> The magistrate court ruled in
favor of the Eriksens and awarded attorney fees pursuant to Idaho Code section 12-120(3). </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1298211"><a href="/opinion/1298211/eriksen-v-blue-cross-of-idaho-health-services-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Eriksen v. Blue Cross of Idaho Health Services, Inc.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> The
district court affirmed the attorney fee award, and Blue Cross appealed. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1298211"><a href="/opinion/1298211/eriksen-v-blue-cross-of-idaho-health-services-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Eriksen v. Blue Cross of Idaho Health Services, Inc.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">
       When the Idaho Legislature first enacted what is now codified as subsection (3), Idaho
Code section 12-120 read, in pertinent part:
       In any civil action to recover on an open account, account stated, note, bill,
       negotiable instrument, or contract relating to the purchase or sale of goods, wares,
       or merchandise, unless otherwise provided by law, the prevailing party shall be
       allowed a reasonable attorney fee to be set by the court, to be taxed and collected
       as costs.
Act of Mar. 18, 1975, ch. 65, </pre><span class="citation no-link">1975 Idaho Sess. Laws 131</span><pre class="inline"> (originally codified at I.C. § 12-120(2)).
       In 1986, three years before Eriksen was decided, the legislature amended this provision in
several respects. Pertinent to this appeal, the amendment added language to the statute permitting
an award of attorney fees to the prevailing party in civil actions to recover on a contract for
“services and in any commercial transaction”:


                                                 12
       In any civil action to recover on an open account, account stated, note, bill,
       negotiable instrument, guaranty, or contract relating to the purchase or sale of
       goods, wares, or merchandise, or services and in any commercial transaction unless
       otherwise provided by law, the prevailing party shall be allowed a reasonable
       attorney fee to be set by the court, to be taxed and collected as costs.
Act of Apr. 3, 1986, ch. 205, </pre><span class="citation no-link">1986 Idaho Sess. Laws 511</span><pre class="inline">, 511–12 (codified at I.C. § 12-120(3))
(underlining in original). The first paragraph of subsection (3) has remained unchanged since.
       Turning back to Eriksen, Blue Cross argued, among other things, that the statute did not
authorize the award of attorney fees because the insurance contract at issue did not constitute a
commercial transaction. 116 Idaho at 694–95, 778 P.2d at 816–17 (Ct. App. 1989). The Idaho Court
of Appeals rejected the argument that the addition of “commercial transaction” created a limitation
upon the other enumerated causes of action set forth in the statute:
               The linchpin of Blue Cross’s argument is the supposed limitation of the term
       “services” by the words “commercial transaction.” We have held that other terms
       preceding the words “commercial transaction,” such as “open account, account
       stated, note, bill or negotiable instrument,” are not limited by the ensuing reference
       to purchase or sale of “goods, wares, [or] merchandise.” See Cheney v. Smith, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1128180"><a href="/opinion/1128180/cheney-v-smith/" aria-description="Citation for case: Cheney v. Smith">108
       Idaho 209</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1128180"><a href="/opinion/1128180/cheney-v-smith/" aria-description="Citation for case: Cheney v. Smith">697 P.2d 1223</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Ct. App. 1985); Camp v. Jiminez, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1173047"><a href="/opinion/1173047/camp-v-jiminez/" aria-description="Citation for case: Camp v. Jiminez">107 Idaho 878</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1173047"><a href="/opinion/1173047/camp-v-jiminez/" aria-description="Citation for case: Camp v. Jiminez">693
       P.2d 1080</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Ct. App. 1984); Boise Truck &amp; Equipment, Inc. v. Hafer Logging, Inc.,
       </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1173055"><a href="/opinion/1173055/boise-truck-equipment-inc-v-hafer-logging-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Boise Truck &amp; Equipment, Inc. v. Hafer Logging, Inc.">107 Idaho 824</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1173055"><a href="/opinion/1173055/boise-truck-equipment-inc-v-hafer-logging-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Boise Truck &amp; Equipment, Inc. v. Hafer Logging, Inc.">693 P.2d 470</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Ct. App. 1984). By parity of construction, we believe
       that “services” and the other enumerated elements of [Idaho Code section] 12-
       120(3) are not limited by the words “commercial transaction.” Moreover, it is well
       settled that statutes should not be read to create surplusage. Hartley v. Miller-
       Stephan, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9547528"><a href="/opinion/1173192/hartley-v-miller-stephan/" aria-description="Citation for case: Hartley v. Miller-Stephan">107 Idaho 688</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9547528"><a href="/opinion/1173192/hartley-v-miller-stephan/" aria-description="Citation for case: Hartley v. Miller-Stephan">692 P.2d 332</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1984). If the words preceding “commercial
       transaction” were treated merely as examples of the types of commercial
       transactions to which the statute could apply, they would be no more than
       surplusage.
                We deem it clear that the [l]egislature put the term “commercial transaction”
       in this statute, not to narrow its scope, but to extend its coverage to litigation arising
       from commercial disputes as well as from certain non-commercial disputes. This
       intent is evinced by the [l]egislature’s use of the conjunctive phrase “and in any
       commercial transaction” (emphasis added). Consequently, we need not decide
       whether the Eriksen contract, which is a contract for the sale of services, also
       involves a commercial transaction other than for personal or household purposes.
       Because all service contracts are encompassed by [Idaho Code section] 12-120(3),
       the statute was correctly applied to this case.
Eriksen, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1298211"><a href="/opinion/1298211/eriksen-v-blue-cross-of-idaho-health-services-inc/#695" aria-description="Citation for case: Eriksen v. Blue Cross of Idaho Health Services, Inc.">116 Idaho at 695</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1298211"><a href="/opinion/1298211/eriksen-v-blue-cross-of-idaho-health-services-inc/#817" aria-description="Citation for case: Eriksen v. Blue Cross of Idaho Health Services, Inc.">778 P.2d at 817</a></span><pre class="inline"> (first alteration in original). We agree with the Court of
Appeals’ analysis of the statute. Accordingly, we hold that the phrase “and in any commercial
transaction” in Idaho Code section 12-120(3) operates as an additional, independent basis for a fee
award and does not limit the statute’s other enumerated causes of action.


                                                  13
       The DeGroots argue that Brower, an opinion by this Court a year after Eriksen, abrogated
Eriksen and created a narrower interpretation of the statute. Brower, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1427743"><a href="/opinion/1427743/brower-v-ei-dupont-de-nemours-and-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Brower v. EI DuPont De Nemours and Co.">117 Idaho 780</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1427743"><a href="/opinion/1427743/brower-v-ei-dupont-de-nemours-and-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Brower v. EI DuPont De Nemours and Co.">792 P.2d 345</a></span><pre class="inline">.
To support this argument, the DeGroots assert that Brower’s holding was that the only way Idaho
Code section 12-120(3) applies is if a case involves a commercial transaction. We disagree.
       In Brower, a farmer sued DuPont after applying DuPont’s herbicide “Glean” to his farm,
alleging the product contaminated his soil and caused crop-related losses. 117 Idaho at 780–81,
792 P.2d at 345–46. On appeal, this Court held Brower’s claims were time-barred and, separately,
reversed the district court’s award of attorney fees to DuPont under Idaho Code section 12-120(3).
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="1427743"><a href="/opinion/1427743/brower-v-ei-dupont-de-nemours-and-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Brower v. EI DuPont De Nemours and Co.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> at 781–84, 792 P.2d at 346–49. This Court’s attorney fee analysis focused on the “commercial
transaction” language because that was the only plausible basis for fees on the facts presented: a
products-liability/misrepresentation suit only tangentially connected to Brower’s purchase of the
herbicide. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1427743"><a href="/opinion/1427743/brower-v-ei-dupont-de-nemours-and-co/#781" aria-description="Citation for case: Brower v. EI DuPont De Nemours and Co.">Id. at 781</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1427743"><a href="/opinion/1427743/brower-v-ei-dupont-de-nemours-and-co/#346" aria-description="Citation for case: Brower v. EI DuPont De Nemours and Co.">792 P.2d at 346</a></span><pre class="inline">. Brower articulated the “commercial transaction” gravamen
test, but nothing in Brower purports to hold that “commercial transactions” are the exclusive
pathway to fees under section 12-120(3).
       To further support their position, the DeGroots selectively point to this Court’s statement
in Brower that, unless a commercial transaction is integral to the claim, it would convert an
“exceptional [statutory] remedy . . . to a matter of right in virtually every lawsuit filed.” See </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1427743"><a href="/opinion/1427743/brower-v-ei-dupont-de-nemours-and-co/#784" aria-description="Citation for case: Brower v. EI DuPont De Nemours and Co.">id. at
784</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1427743"><a href="/opinion/1427743/brower-v-ei-dupont-de-nemours-and-co/#349" aria-description="Citation for case: Brower v. EI DuPont De Nemours and Co.">792 P.2d at 349</a></span><pre class="inline">. When the full context of the paragraph, including that statement, is
considered, it is clear that the statement at issue addresses the breadth of the “commercial
transaction” basis, not the subsection as a whole:
               These cases lead to the conclusion that the award of attorney’s fees is not
       warranted every time a commercial transaction is remotely connected with the case.
       Rather, the test is whether the commercial transaction comprises the gravamen of
       the lawsuit. Attorney’s fees are not appropriate under [Idaho Code section] 12-
       120(3) unless the commercial transaction is integral to the claim, and constitutes
       the basis upon which the party is attempting to recover. To hold otherwise would
       be to convert the award of attorney’s fees from an exceptional remedy justified only
       by statutory authority to a matter of right in virtually every lawsuit filed.
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="1427743"><a href="/opinion/1427743/brower-v-ei-dupont-de-nemours-and-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Brower v. EI DuPont De Nemours and Co.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">

a party can recover attorney fees under Idaho Code section 12-120(3) depends on whether the

       Take SilverWing, for example. There, the developer of a residential hangar project had a

not fit within the other causes of action enumerated in subsection (3). Because we agree with the
Court of Appeals’ analysis in Eriksen, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1298211"><a href="/opinion/1298211/eriksen-v-blue-cross-of-idaho-health-services-inc/#695" aria-description="Citation for case: Eriksen v. not made any other challenge to the district court’s award of attorney fees, we affirm the district
court’s decision on reconsideration.
C. The Armitages are awarded reasonable attorney fees and costs on appeal.
       Both the Armitages and the DeGroots request attorney fees on appeal should they prevail.
The Armitages are the prevailing party. They request fees under Idaho Code sections 12-120(3)
and 12-121. The DeGroots have made no arguments against the Armitages’ attorney fee request.
       Since we have affirmed the district court’s award of attorney fees pursuant to Idaho Code
section 12-120(3) and the DeGroots do not otherwise challenge the availability of attorney fees
under the statute, we award reasonable attorney fees on appeal. There is no need for this Court to
address the Armitages’ fee request under section 12-121. The Armitages are entitled to costs
pursuant to Idaho Appellate Rule 40.
                                       IV.   CONCLUSION
       We affirm the district court’s decision on reconsideration to reject the DeGroots’ merger
defense, and we affirm the district court’s decision on reconsideration to award the Armitages
attorney fees. We award the Armitages reasonable attorney fees on appeal pursuant to Idaho Code
section 12-120(3) and costs pursuant to Idaho Appellate Rule 40.
