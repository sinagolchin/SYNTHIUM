                       INTRODUCTION
   Maman Lawan Ali appeals from the district court’s decree
foreclosing his interest in his marital residence, which he owns
with his wife as a joint tenant with right of survivorship. The
decree ordered “the Sheriff or a special master of Plaintiff’s
choice” to sell the real property to satisfy a judgment lien that
11819 Miracle Hills Drive, LP (Miracle Hills), obtained against
Ali individually. The primary issue before us is the applicabil-
ity and operation of the homestead statutes 1 to the facts of
this case. We conclude that the homestead exemption did not
prevent the court from entering the decree. However, we find
plain error in the decree and, accordingly, vacate the decree
and remand the cause for further proceedings.
                       BACKGROUND
   In a prior action, Miracle Hills obtained a money judgment
against Ali related to a commercial lease agreement. Miracle
Hills filed the instant action by way of a “Complaint for
Foreclosure of Judgment Lien (Equity),” seeking to foreclose
its judgment lien against Ali’s undivided one-half interest in
real property owned by him and his wife as joint tenants with
rights of survivorship. It is undisputed that the property is the
couple’s primary residence and family home. In their answers
to Miracle Hills’ complaint, neither Ali nor his wife raised
any defenses or counterclaims.
   Miracle Hills moved for summary judgment. At the hear-
ing on the motion, Ali and his wife appeared, both self-
represented. Three exhibits were offered and received. The

                 ASSIGNMENTS OF ERROR
   Ali assigns that the trial court erred in (1) granting sum-
mary judgment despite material issues of fact regarding the
homestead status of the property, (2) ordering foreclosure
of his undivided interest in the marital residence contrary to
§§ 40-101 and 40-103, and (3) extending a judgment lien
against one spouse to divest the nondebtor spouse of her co-
ownership interest in the homestead.

                           ANALYSIS
   We begin by recognizing the unique action that Miracle
Hills brought to enforce its judgment lien. Instead of seeking
a writ of execution on Ali’s property, 5 Miracle Hills brought
what it called an equitable foreclosure action. We have, at
least on two occasions, recognized such an action; however,
the facts of those cases were extreme and readily distinguish-
able from the instant action. 6 Nevertheless, the district court
heard the action and entered a decree, and Ali has not chal-
lenged the type of action brought. Accordingly, we do not con-
sider whether an equitable foreclosure action is proper under
these circumstances.
   On appeal, all three of Ali’s assignments of error concern
the effect and applicability of the homestead exemption to this
case. In sum, he argues that the homestead exemption pre-
vented the district court from ordering the sale of his interest
in the real property. Miracle Hills argues that, if the homestead
exemption applies, it is not a defense to foreclosure but applies
“at the execution stage.” 7 As we discuss below, we disagree
with both parties. To understand why, we first provide an over-
view of the homestead statutes 8 before turning to the resolu-
tion of this case.
                     Homestead Statutes

enacting the homestead statutes was to protect the debtor and
the debtor’s family residing in a home from the forced sale of
the home on execution or attachment. 9 A homestead is gener-

stead exemption could not be raised in this action prior to the
“execution stage.” 27 The text of § 40-105 is plain: Ali “may at

   It is undisputed that Ali did not file an application for a
homestead exemption that comported with the requirements
of §§ 40-105 and 40-106. But Ali asserts that the homestead
exemption acts as a limit on judicial power, “a substantive
legislative restriction on what relief a court . . . may order.” 28
We disagree.
   Ali’s argument is premised on the contention that a home-
stead is a residence. As discussed above, a homestead is consti-
tuted by two acts, residence and selection. 29 A homestead does
not exist until it is selected. Thus, the homestead exemption is
not a limit on judicial power. Instead, as mentioned above, the
purpose of the homestead statutes is to protect the debtor and
the debtor’s family residing in a home from the forced sale of
the home on execution or attachment. 30
   [4] To select a homestead under § 40-101, an application
under § 40-105 that comports with §§ 40-106 and 40-107 must
be filed with the district court. Here, Ali did not file such an
application. Accordingly, the homestead exemption did not
apply to the proceeding before the court. However, for the rea-
sons explained next, we find plain error in the decree.

                          Plain Error
   Plain error may be found on appeal when an error unas-
serted or uncomplained of below, but plainly evident from the
record, prejudicially affects a litigant’s substantial right and, if
uncorrected, would result in damage to the integrity, reputa-
tion, and fairness of the judicial process. 31

   Two errors are plain from a review of the district court’s
decree. First, the decree defines the “Real Property” as Ali
and his wife’s marital residence. However, as Miracle Hills
concedes, the judgment lien attached only to Ali’s one-half
interest in the marital residence. Yet, the decree calls for the
sale of the “Real Property,” and not merely Ali’s one-half
interest. Because the judgment lien attached only to Ali’s one-
half interest, the district court erred by ordering the sale of the
entire marital residence.
   Second, the district court did not identify the party respon-
sible for selling Ali’s one-half interest in the marital resi-
dence. The court’s decree specifies that the sale be conducted
by “the Sheriff or a special master of [Miracle Hills’] choice.”
This provision is insufficient. The court must identify the
party responsible for the sale of the real property. The court
cannot leave the choice to one party’s discretion, as the par-
ties to an action have opposing interests. The court plainly
erred in not identifying the party ordered to sell Ali’s one-half
interest in the marital residence.
   These plain errors require us to vacate the court’s decree and
remand the cause for further proceedings.

                          CONCLUSION
   The homestead exemption does not apply in the absence of
an application filed by Ali, in accordance with §§ 40-105 to
40-107. However, the district court plainly erred by ordering
the sale of the entire marital residence, rather than Ali’s one-
half interest, and in not identifying the party ordered to sell that
interest. Accordingly, we vacate the court’s decree and remand
the cause for further proceedings.
