               ROBERT J. KRASKO ET AL. v.
                ROBERT KONKOS ET AL.
                      (AC 48824)
                        Moll, Seeley and Palmer, Js.

                                   Syllabus

The defendants, owners of certain real property that is subject to a right-
of-way easement benefitting the plaintiffs’ neighboring property, appealed
from the trial court’s judgment ordering the defendants to authorize the
removal of a utility pole that is located in the easement area so that the
plaintiffs could widen their driveway. The defendants claimed, inter alia,
that the court erred in finding that their refusal to consent to the removal
of the pole was an obstruction of the plaintiffs’ easement. Held:

The trial court did not err in concluding that the defendants’ refusal to
consent to the removal of the utility pole constituted an obstruction of the
plaintiffs’ easement, as the court properly applied the reasonable use analysis
articulated in Williams v. Green Power Ventures, LLC (</pre><span class="citation" data-id="9901316"><a href="/opinion/9443491/williams-v-green-power-ventures-llc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Williams v. Green Power Ventures, LLC">221 Conn. App. 657</a></span><pre class="inline">),
the court’s analysis of whether the plaintiffs’ proposed use of the easement
was reasonably necessary was sound, the majority of the court’s factual find-
ings were not clearly erroneous, and any erroneous findings were harmless.

The trial court did not err by ordering unpleaded declaratory relief as claimed
by the defendants but, rather, properly awarded injunctive relief as requested
by the plaintiffs in their operative complaint, as the judgment directed the
defendants to take a specific action, namely, to authorize the utility companies
to remove the utility pole and to transfer the defendants’ electrical service to
a different utility pole, rather than merely establishing the existing rights
and legal relations of the parties, and the purpose of the order was to stop
the defendants’ interference with the plaintiffs’ use and enjoyment of the
easement, which is a classic purpose of an injunction.

tiffs, as the owners of the former lot 8, hold a valid ease-
ment appurtenant over the defendants’ property, the
former lot 7. In the parlance of easements, the plaintiffs’
property is the dominant estate, and the defendants’
property is the servient estate. There is also no dispute
as to the general character, extent, and scope of the
easement. The easement agreement incorporates a pre-
existing twenty-five foot right-of-way “for all lawful
purposes” and specifically gives the plaintiffs the right
to “ingress and egress to and from [the public street] on
foot and by motor vehicle; including the right to construct
a driveway in common with [the defendants’ property]”
within the twenty-five foot right-of-way. (Emphasis
added.) Rather, the present case centers on a specific
proposed use of the easement by the plaintiffs, as hold-

so that they can expand their driveway into the area
currently occupied by the original pole. This proposed
use falls within the general character and scope of the
easement. See, e.g., State v. Quintiliano, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4765977"><a href="/opinion/4957783/state-v-quintiliano/#724" aria-description="Citation for case: State v. Quintiliano">206 Conn.

plaintiffs’ rights under the easement.

Williams was whether the dominant estate holder’s

quoted language from Somers does not establish a test
meaningfully different from the test applied by the court.

lated in Williams was the correct standard for the court

their preferred standard is correct. Thus, the defendants’
claim fails.
