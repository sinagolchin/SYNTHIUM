[1]   Terry Curella (“Terry”) acquired an undivided one-half interest in a Kokomo

      home (the “House”) from his brother (“Gregg”) in 2021 and petitioned to

      partition the House by sale fifteen months later. Gregg lived in the House from

      2001 to 2004, but he had not made any mortgage payments. Gina Behny

      (“Gina”) – whom Gregg had been dating during his three years in the House –

      still lived in the House when Terry petitioned for sale, had lived in the House

      since 1999, and had made all the mortgage payments and necessary

      improvements on the House. Gina opposed a sale. After a bench trial, the trial

      court awarded the entire property to Gina, ordered Terry to convey his interest

      to her, and entered a $4,307.95 judgment against Terry.


[2]   Terry appeals to challenge the trial court’s denial of his motion to add the

      parties’ proposed findings to the Clerk’s Record, its equitable adjustment of the

      values of their cotenant shares, and its inclusion of a lost homestead deduction

      and an expired judgment lien in the money judgment. Although the trial court

      erred by not making the proposed findings and conclusions part of the official

      record, any error therein was harmless under the specific facts of this case.

      Terry has not demonstrated error in the trial court’s determination that equity

      required him to sign over his interest in the House to Gina, he waived his

      argument regarding the homestead deduction, and the trial court’s order that

      Terry pay Gina for the money judgment was not error. We accordingly affirm

      the trial court’s judgment.

      tenants with right of survivorship[.]” (Ex. Vol. at 8) (emphasis removed).

      Gregg moved out in the fall of 2004, but his name was never removed from the

      title.

      her husband, Peter, and later his disabled adult son. Gina made all mortgage

      payments on the Kokomo house, even when Gregg lived there. Real estate

      taxes and homeowner’s insurance were escrowed and paid as part of the

      monthly mortgage payment. Gina also paid the utilities and paid for repairs

      and maintenance, including a new roof, furnace, and water-heater, along with

      bathroom repairs and a $1,000 insurance deductible for water damage. Her

      total contribution toward the House over the years has been $275,462.25. (Ex.

      Vol. at 45.)

       Discussion and Decision
       1. Motion to Add Proposed Orders to the Record
[12]   Terry first argues the trial court erred when it denied his motion to add the

       parties’ proposed orders to the Clerk’s Record. He contends the proposed

       orders are necessary for his appeal so that we may see that the trial court

       adopted Gina’s proposal verbatim – which, as Terry notes, impacts our scrutiny

       of a case on appeal. (See Br. of Appellant at 11-12) (quoting Dallas v. Cessna,

       </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2730253"><a href="/opinion/2730253/cody-dallas-v-brandon-cessna/" aria-description="Citation for case: Cody Dallas v. Brandon Cessna">968 N.E.2d 291</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Ind. Ct. App. 2012)). In Dallas, our court explained:


               We note that in this case, the trial court adopted Cessna’s
               proposed findings and conclusions verbatim. Although
               wholesale adoption is not prohibited, we do not encourage trial
               courts to engage in this practice. We have recognized that “this
               practice weakens our confidence as an appellate court that the
               findings are the result of considered judgment by the trial court.”
               Although we do not apply an altered standard of review when a
               trial court adopts a party’s findings verbatim, “near verbatim
               reproductions may appropriately justify cautious appellate
               scrutiny.”


       </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2730253"><a href="/opinion/2730253/cody-dallas-v-brandon-cessna/#296" aria-description="Citation for case: Cody Dallas v. Brandon Cessna">968 N.E.2d at 296</a></span><pre class="inline"> (internal citations omitted).


[13]   The trial court twice denied Terry’s motion – presumably for the reasons

       asserted by Gina in her responses to Terry’s motions. Gina asserted the

       proposed orders could not properly be made part of the Record on Appeal 1

       judge, the trial court clerk did not place the filings on the Chronological Case

       Summary or in the Clerk’s Record. Because the trial court’s verbatim adoption

       of one party’s proposed order impacts the scrutiny that we give to a trial court’s

       final order, proposed findings of fact filed by parties must be available in the

       to his motion to add the filings to the Clerk’s Record placed the documents in

       the Record on Appeal. Accordingly, with our confidence “that the findings

       reflect the considered judgment of the trial court” eroded, Prowell v. State, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="853470"><a href="/opinion/853470/prowell-v-state/#709" aria-description="Citation for case: Prowell v. State">741

       erodes appellate court confidence in the judgment’s validity), we turn to Terry’s

       arguments on the merits.


       2. Award of House to Gina
[16]   Terry next challenges the trial court’s equitable adjustment of the parties’

       interests in the House, which resulted in the award of the House to Gina.

       When a court enters findings and conclusions at a party’s request, we apply a

       two-tiered standard of review: we first determine whether the evidence supports

       3. Money Judgment

       $3,061.78 (due for judgment lien, interest, and court costs thereon) and

       subtracted $1,153.83 (for the two duplicative payments Terry made toward the

       Conclusion
[22]   The trial court erred when it denied Terry’s motion to add the parties’ proposed

       orders to the Clerk’s Record after the parties filed those documents with the

       clerk and served them on opposing counsel, but the error was harmless. None

       of Terry’s arguments regarding financial contributions by Gina demonstrate the

       trial court clearly erred when it determined equity required him to sign his

       interest in the House over to her. Terry forfeited his homestead-deduction

       argument by failing to raise it at trial, and the trial court did not err when it

       ordered Terry to pay the judgment lien. We therefore affirm the trial court’s

       judgment.
