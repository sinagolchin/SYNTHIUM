[¶1] Melissa Reilly (Mother) appeals from the district court’s order awarding Kevin
Sullivan (Father) primary physical custody of their daughter, RAS. We affirm.

                                                ISSUES

[¶2]    Mother raises four issues, which we restate as three: 1

                1. Did the district court abuse its discretion by assigning little
                   weight to Mother’s status as RAS’s primary caregiver?

                2. Did the district court abuse its discretion by improperly
                   minimizing the importance of preserving RAS’s
                   relationship with her half brother and by failing to articulate
                   a reasoned explanation for separating them?

                3. Did the district court clearly err when it failed to evenly
                   weigh the parties’ credibility?

B.      Primary Caregiver Status, Separation of Siblings, and Credibility

[¶20] Mother’s arguments concern the weight given her primary caregiver status, the
separation of RAS and KF, and the court’s credibility findings. We take those in turn.

I.      Did the district court abuse its discretion by assigning little weight to Mother’s
        status as RAS’s primary caregiver?

[¶21] In awarding custody to Father, the court recognized a change in RAS’s primary
caregiver is a “weighty matter,” but found a change would not result in instability or
insecurity for RAS. It gave Mother’s status as RAS’s primary caregiver “little weight”
because she “manufactured” that status by actively preventing Father from fulfilling that
same role.

[¶22] Mother argues the district court abused its discretion by giving “little weight” to her
role as RAS’s primary caregiver after finding she “manufactured” that status by refusing
to allow Father to visit RAS anywhere but Casper. She claims the evidence showed she
never refused him visitation. Instead, she asserts, Father sought visitation only when it was
convenient for him, and she accommodated his requests to the best of her ability given the
geographical distance and RAS’s age. She also contends she had legitimate safety
concerns with RAS traveling to Big Sky, especially in the winter, given RAS’s age and the
seven-hour, one-way drive. Mother further argues that the court relied on her leaving
Father off RAS’s school records but claims she left him off only as an emergency contact
because he lived seven hours away. She points out that she never stopped Father from
contacting the school or getting information and that she fixed the issue before trial by
adding him as an emergency contact. Mother maintains the district court’s finding that she
prevented Father from fulfilling a primary caregiver role ignores that Father failed to
exercise his parental rights or to take affirmative steps to establish custody or visitation for
nearly five years.

[¶23] Mother also argues that in discounting her primary caregiver role, the district court
improperly relied on speculative safety concerns related to Mr. Clark’s DUI convictions.
Mother contends his most recent DUI conviction was more than seven years ago and there
was no evidence that he ever drove RAS while he was intoxicated or that his driving


                                               7
privileges were suspended or inhibited in any way. Because the district court criticized her
for relying on Mr. Clark to assist with RAS’s care while she was working and noted
Father’s “established career,” Mother insists the district court penalized her for being a
“single working mother managing childcare responsibilities while [Father] remained
absent seven hours away.” She contends rather than penalizing her for maintaining
employment, the court should have evaluated whether RAS’s needs were being met and
focused on RAS’s established routines, attachments, and day-to-day stability. Properly
evaluated, Mother argues there was no evidence that RAS’s care, safety, or well-being have
been compromised.

[¶24] “A child’s primary caregiver is ‘the parent who is primarily responsible for the
hands-on, day-to-day care of the child.’” Gardels, ¶ 26, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9364444"><a href="/opinion/9368968/laura-christine-gardels-nka-laura-christine-birt-v-phillip-joseph/" aria-description="Citation for case: Laura Christine Gardels N/K/A Laura Christine Birt v....">522 P.3d at 1057</a></span><pre class="inline"> (quoting Williams
v. Williams, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3178505"><a href="/opinion/3178523/kelli-sue-williams-v-charles-leslie-williams/#21" aria-description="Citation for case: Kelli Sue Williams v. Charles Leslie Williams">2016 WY 21, ¶ 21</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3178505"><a href="/opinion/3178523/kelli-sue-williams-v-charles-leslie-williams/#546" aria-description="Citation for case: Kelli Sue Williams v. Charles Leslie Williams">368 P.3d 539, 546</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Wyo. 2016), overruled on other
grounds by Bruegman, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6533699"><a href="/opinion/6656422/bruegman-v-bruegman/" aria-description="Citation for case: Bruegman v. Bruegman">417 P.3d 157</a></span><pre class="inline">).                 “[A] change of a child’s primary
custodian/caregiver is a ‘weighty matter’ that ‘raises a significant concern about
relationship stability and security for the child.’” Id. ¶ 25, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9364444"><a href="/opinion/9368968/laura-christine-gardels-nka-laura-christine-birt-v-phillip-joseph/" aria-description="Citation for case: Laura Christine Gardels N/K/A Laura Christine Birt v....">522 P.3d at 1056</a></span><pre class="inline"> (quoting
Johnson v. Clifford, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2018 WY 59</span><pre class="inline">, ¶ 12, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6533873"><a href="/opinion/6656582/johnson-v-clifford/#823" aria-description="Citation for case: Johnson v. Clifford">418 P.3d 819, 823</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Wyo. 2018); citing Martin,
¶ 22, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6534835"><a href="/opinion/6657491/martin-v-hart/#64" aria-description="Citation for case: Martin v. Hart">429 P.3d at 64</a></span><pre class="inline">). “While we have recognized that a child is almost always harmed
when [she] is taken from the parent who had been the primary nurturer[,]” we have also
confirmed that “primary caregiver status is not determinative; it is one of many factors
considered in determining the child’s best interests.” Kelly v. Kelly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9397117"><a href="/opinion/9401641/andrea-m-kelly-v-mitchell-c-kelly/#15" aria-description="Citation for case: Andrea M. Kelly v. Mitchell C. Kelly">2023 WY 48, ¶ 15</a></span><pre class="inline">,
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="9397117"><a href="/opinion/9401641/andrea-m-kelly-v-mitchell-c-kelly/#499" aria-description="Citation for case: Andrea M. Kelly v. Mitchell C. Kelly">529 P.3d 494, 499</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Wyo. 2023) (internal quotation marks omitted). “Under the unique
facts of a case, other factors may outweigh a party’s primary caregiver status.” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9397117"><a href="/opinion/9401641/andrea-m-kelly-v-mitchell-c-kelly/" aria-description="Citation for case: Andrea M. Kelly v. Mitchell C. Kelly">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> (quoting
Gardels, ¶ 25, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9364444"><a href="/opinion/9368968/laura-christine-gardels-nka-laura-christine-birt-v-phillip-joseph/#1056" aria-description="Citation for case: Laura Christine Gardels N/K/A Laura Christine Birt v....">522 P.3d at 1056</a></span><pre class="inline">).

[¶25] Several of Mother’s arguments misstate the record. Others rest on her own version
of events, which is contrary to our standard of review. See Callaway, ¶ 28, 587 P.3d at 511
(“We consider the evidence presented in the light most favorable to the district court’s
decision, affording every favorable inference to the prevailing party and omitting from our
consideration the conflicting evidence.” (quoting Smith, ¶ 13, 580 P.3d at 511)). Mother
did not refuse Father visitation altogether but did deny him visitation in Big Sky until his
attorney became involved. Father did not seek visitation only when it was convenient for
him. He has spent one week a month with RAS since she was six weeks old. Mother did
more than leave Father off the preschool emergency contact list. She admitted at trial she
also did not list him as RAS’s father. She may not have barred him from calling the school
or getting records, but by omitting him as RAS’s father, she made it far harder. Father did
not wait five years to seek custody. He filed his petition in July 2024, when RAS was three
years old. The district court did not penalize Mother for being a single working parent. It
considered her work schedule and Father’s job under the second best-interests factor—
“[t]he ability of each parent to provide adequate care for each child throughout each period
of responsibility, including arranging for each child’s care by others as needed.” </pre><span class="citation no-link">Wyo.
Stat. Ann. § 20-2-201</span><pre class="inline">(a)(ii). It found Mother’s work schedule and the location of her job
left RAS in Mr. Clark’s care for two weeks of the month. Given his criminal history,


                                             8
unemployment, and continued drinking, the court had concrete safety concerns if an
emergency occurred while Mother was working. Mother also faults the court for not
considering RAS’s established routines and attachments, but the record is silent on both.

[¶26] On the evidence as it actually stands, viewed in the light most favorable to the
district court’s decision, there is little difficulty concluding the district court did not abuse
its discretion when it gave “little weight” to Mother’s status as RAS’s primary caregiver.
The evidence supports the finding that Mother provided RAS’s day-to-day care but kept
Father from filling that same role. The district court recognized that we have said a change
in a child’s primary caregiver almost always harms the child. Kelly, ¶ 15, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9397117"><a href="/opinion/9401641/andrea-m-kelly-v-mitchell-c-kelly/#499" aria-description="Citation for case: Andrea M. Kelly v. Mitchell C. Kelly">529 P.3d at 499</a></span><pre class="inline">.
But, given the circumstances of the case, it found that a change in RAS’s primary caregiver
from Mother to Father would not result in instability or insecurity for RAS. This finding
is supported by the record. The evidence showed that RAS had a strong bond with both
parents, but there was no evidence that RAS was more attached to Mother than Father.
While RAS had primarily lived with Mother, she spent the first six weeks of her life with
Father and visited with him at least one week per month thereafter. We find no abuse of
discretion.

II.    Did the district court abuse its discretion by improperly minimizing the
       importance of preserving RAS’s relationship with her half brother and by failing
       to articulate a reasoned explanation for separating them?

[¶27] On the separation of siblings factor, the court found the bond between RAS and KF
was significant. Even so, that bond had to “be balanced against the father-child relationship
[and] the need to ensure [RAS’s] safety and well-being regarding the individuals [Mother]
introduces into [RAS’s] environment at this young age.” The court found the balance
tipped in favor of Father.

[¶28] Mother argues the district court abused its discretion by minimizing the importance
of preserving RAS’s relationship with KF. She contends that rather than meaningfully
addressing the undisputed testimony that RAS has lived with KF all her life and that the
two share a close bond, the district court dismissed these facts because they are half
siblings, they will attend different schools, and custody and visitation decisions “may
inevitably” interfere to some extent with sibling relationships. Mother also faults the
district court for relying on speculative safety concerns related to Mr. Fuller. Mother
acknowledges that Mr. Fuller was convicted of first-degree sexual assault and is a
registered sex offender. She argues Mr. Fuller’s offense involved an adult, that the
conviction predates his relationship with Mother, and that he has not reoffended. She says
that RAS has encountered Mr. Fuller only in passing because of his court-ordered visitation
with KF, and RAS has never spent time alone with him. The district court stated Mr.
Fuller’s presence in RAS’s life understandably concerned Father. Mother responds that
Father was aware of who Mr. Fuller was and his status before she became pregnant with
RAS yet did nothing about it for nearly five years. She argues this timing suggests Father


                                               9
raised the issue for litigation, not out of any real, contemporaneous concern for RAS’s
welfare.

[¶29]         “[W]e have consistently acknowledged a strong public policy
              in favor of preserving sibling relationships and ‘[k]eeping
              siblings together in the same household[.]’” [Ianelli,] ¶ 30, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6536408"><a href="/opinion/6658972/ianelli-v-camino/" aria-description="Citation for case: Ianelli v. Camino">444
              P.3d at 69</a></span><pre class="inline"> (quoting Paden v. Paden, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5178529"><a href="/opinion/5345632/paden-v-paden/#19" aria-description="Citation for case: Paden v. Paden">2017 WY 118, ¶ 19</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5178529"><a href="/opinion/5345632/paden-v-paden/#141" aria-description="Citation for case: Paden v. Paden">403
              P.3d 135, 141</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Wyo. 2017)). This policy is “equally applicable
              whether the children are full sibling, half sibling, or
              stepsiblings.” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5178529"><a href="/opinion/5345632/paden-v-paden/" aria-description="Citation for case: Paden v. Paden">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> (quoting Aragon v. Aragon, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2640048"><a href="/opinion/2640048/aragon-v-aragon/#26" aria-description="Citation for case: Aragon v. Aragon">2005 WY 5,
              ¶ 26</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2640048"><a href="/opinion/2640048/aragon-v-aragon/#764" aria-description="Citation for case: Aragon v. Aragon">104 P.3d 756, 764</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Wyo. 2005)). We have instructed
              district courts to “articulate the reasons supporting any
              decision to separate siblings to assure that a ‘comprehensive
              evaluation of all relevant factors occurred’ prior to the custody
              determination.” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2640048"><a href="/opinion/2640048/aragon-v-aragon/" aria-description="Citation for case: Aragon v. Aragon">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> (citation omitted).

Gutierrez, ¶ 24, 500 P.3d at 990. That said, separation of siblings “is just one of several
factors the district court considers in the best interests analysis, and no single factor is
determinative of the custody question.” Gardels, ¶ 23, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9364444"><a href="/opinion/9368968/laura-christine-gardels-nka-laura-christine-birt-v-phillip-joseph/" aria-description="Citation for case: Laura Christine Gardels N/K/A Laura Christine Birt v....">522 P.3d at 1056</a></span><pre class="inline"> (citing Paden v.
Paden, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5178529"><a href="/opinion/5345632/paden-v-paden/#19" aria-description="Citation for case: Paden v. Paden">2017 WY 118, ¶ 19</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5178529"><a href="/opinion/5345632/paden-v-paden/#141" aria-description="Citation for case: Paden v. Paden">403 P.3d 135, 141</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Wyo. 2017)). When, as here, half siblings
with different parents exist, “it is inevitable that custody or visitation arrangements will
interfere, to some extent, with time the child subject to the order can spend with those
siblings. This fact alone, does not necessarily [override] the interests of the subject child
in having a relationship with both of her parents.” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5178529"><a href="/opinion/5345632/paden-v-paden/" aria-description="Citation for case: Paden v. Paden">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">

[¶30] Mother misstates the record. The district court did not minimize RAS’s relationship
with KF because they are half siblings or because they will soon attend separate schools.
It simply noted those facts, along with their respective ages. Relying on this court’s
precedent, it treated sibling separation as one factor in the best interests analysis—one that
must be balanced against the father-child relationship and RAS’s safety. See Gardels, ¶ 23,
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="9364444"><a href="/opinion/9368968/laura-christine-gardels-nka-laura-christine-birt-v-phillip-joseph/#1056" aria-description="Citation for case: Laura Christine Gardels N/K/A Laura Christine Birt v....">522 P.3d at 1056</a></span><pre class="inline">. It found the balance favored Father.

[¶31] Father did not know about Mr. Fuller’s felony conviction and status as a registered
sex offender before Mother became pregnant. The evidence showed Father did not learn
about Mr. Fuller’s criminal record until at least May 2023. There is no evidence that
Father’s concern was manufactured for this case. The court’s concern that Mother brought
Mr. Clark and Mr. Fuller into RAS’s life when she was very young is supported by the
record and is not speculative.

[¶32] Mother also contends the district court failed to articulate a reasoned explanation to
support separating RAS from KF, as required by Ianelli, ¶ 30, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6536408"><a href="/opinion/6658972/ianelli-v-camino/#69" aria-description="Citation for case: Ianelli v. Camino">444 P.3d at 69</a></span><pre class="inline">. In Ianelli,

[¶33] The district court did not abuse its discretion. It addressed sibling separation in
balancing the statutory factors, recognized the bond between RAS and KF, and explained
why that bond did not control.

III.      Did the district court clearly err when it failed to evenly weigh the parties’
          credibility?

[¶34] On credibility, the court weighed Mother’s violation of the sequestration order, and
her redaction of Mr. Clark’s driver’s license, against her. It considered Father’s testimony
about alcohol monitoring and found no credible evidence he continues to use alcohol.

[¶35] Mother acknowledges credibility determinations are committed to the district
court’s discretion and asserts she is not asking us to reweigh credibility, make new factual
findings, or substitute our judgment regarding witness credibility. She contends the district
court weighed the parties’ credibility unevenly. According to her, that imbalance skewed
the court’s best-interest balance; led the court to discount her primary caregiver status,
elevate the safety concerns, and excuse Father’s lack of involvement; and undermined the
court’s ultimate custody determination. In her view, the court weighed credibility against
her based on her violation of the sequestration order and questioned her judgment and
parenting based on the criminal histories of Mr. Clark and Mr. Fuller but never addressed
Father’s credibility with respect to his alcohol use. She maintains that on cross-
examination, Father denied he had been subject to alcohol monitoring during his
anesthesiology residency but then admitted it on rebuttal. She also points out that she
admitted to violating the sequestration order, while Father tried to cover up his inconsistent
testimony by claiming he misheard the question. Mother points to the angry voicemails
Father left her and claims he was likely intoxicated when they were made. Even so, she
says the district court minimized his alcohol use by stating “the Court understands that at
one point early in his career [Father] may have struggled with alcohol use.”

[¶36] We see no clear error in the district court’s credibility findings. Mother admitted
she violated the court’s sequestration order by discussing the trial proceedings with Mr.
Clark, a potential witness. The court explicitly informed Mother that this violation would
weigh into its assessment of credibility. Mother also admitted that she redacted Mr. Clark’s
driver’s license before providing it to Father in discovery in order to protect Mr. Clark.
The district court was reasonably suspect of these actions as Mr. Clark had lived in
Wyoming for years yet did not have a Wyoming driver’s license. Given his DUI
convictions, the court found it highly likely he would have a restricted license and, by
statute, would be required to have an interlock device on his vehicle. Because his driver’s
license was issued by Colorado, it is unclear what conditions Wyoming would place on
him if he were to apply for a Wyoming license. The district court reasonably weighed


                                             11
Mother’s redaction of Mr. Clark’s license and her violation of the sequestration order
against her in assessing credibility.

[¶37] On the question of Father’s alcohol use, Father was cross-examined about his
decision not to pursue anesthesiology. Mother’s attorney then asked him, “You were not
on monitoring?” He answered, “No.” Her attorney then asked, “For alcohol?” and he
again responded, “No.” Later, Mother testified Father told her that he was subject to
monitoring because he “tried to kill himself, and he had to blow into a machine to make
sure that he wasn’t drinking.” During rebuttal, Father attested that the cross-examination
question was not whether he was subject to monitoring but was instead whether there were
any stipulations on his medical license. He then admitted he was subject to monitoring
during his anesthesiology residency because he was depressed and standard monitoring
included drug and alcohol testing. Mother claims Father was trying to cover up his false
testimony. The district court considered Father’s rebuttal testimony and referred to it in
analyzing RAS’s best interests. The court found Father had been subject to monitoring and
“at one point early in his career . . . may have struggled with alcohol use.” It determined,
however, that there was no credible evidence he continues to use alcohol. That finding is
supported by the testimony. One of Father’s witnesses testified that in the almost eight
years she has known him, she has never seen him drink alcohol or appear intoxicated.
Father also testified he does not drink alcohol. While Mother claimed Father was likely
drunk when he left her the angry voicemails following RAS’s dental surgery because he
slurred his words, Father testified he was not drunk but upset. The district court listened
to those voicemails and apparently did not adopt Mother’s conclusion. We are not at liberty
to second-guess that credibility determination in the absence of any evidence that a mistake
was made. See Soames, ¶ 16, 512 P.3d at 632 (credibility findings are reviewed for clear
error, which “occurs when, after reviewing the entire evidence, we are ‘left with the definite
and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed’” (quoting Fuger, ¶ 8, 478 P.3d at
181)).

[¶38] Mother’s claim that the district court “unevenly” weighed the parties’ credibility
starts from the wrong premise. The district court is entitled to weigh credibility and, if
appropriate, to credit one parent over the other. Credibility determinations are reviewed
for clear error. We find no clear error.

                                      CONCLUSION

[¶39] The district court did not abuse its discretion by giving little weight to Mother’s
primary caregiver status, or in weighing and explaining the separation of RAS and KF. Its
credibility findings are supported by the record and are not clearly erroneous. We affirm
the award of primary physical custody of RAS to Father.
